In plain words first
The idea in one line

Encoders understand. Decoders generate. Put them back together and you get a model built for input → output tasks: translate, summarize, rewrite, transcribe.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
Encoder-decoder "Seq2seq transformer" Two stacks: bidirectional encoder for input, causal decoder with cross-attention for output.
Cross-attention "Where source talks to target" Decoder's Q × encoder's K/V. The only place encoder information enters the decoder.
Span corruption "T5's pretraining trick" Replace random spans with sentinel tokens; decoder outputs the spans.
Denoising objective "BART's game" Apply a noise function to the input, train the decoder to reconstruct the clean sequence.
Sentinel token "The <extra_id_N> placeholder" Special tokens that tag corrupted spans in the source and re-tag them in the target.
Flan "Instruction-tuned T5" T5 fine-tuned on >1,800 tasks; made encoder-decoder competitive at instruction-following.
Beam search "Decoding strategy" Keep top-k partial sequences at each step; standard for translation/summarization.
Teacher forcing "Training-time input" During training, feed the true previous output token to the decoder, not the sampled one.

The problem

In plain words first

Many tasks turn one text (or recording) into another: translate this sentence, summarise this article, transcribe this audio. An encoder–decoder model splits the job in two. The encoder reads the whole input once, with no mask, and turns it into a set of vectors. The decoder then writes the output word by word like GPT, but at every step it can also look back at the encoder's vectors through cross-attention. T5 and BART pretrained such models by damaging text and asking the decoder to repair it. This lesson runs the lesson's span-corruption and noising code, then a real translation model, showing that its encoder runs once while its decoder runs once per word and which source word each German word looks at, and the original T5 filling in hidden spans the way it was trained to. Nothing is trained.

Decoder-only GPT and encoder-only BERT each strip down the 2017 architecture for a different goal. But many tasks are naturally input-output:

  • Translation: English → French.
  • Summarization: 5,000-token article → 200-token summary.
  • Speech recognition: audio tokens → text tokens.
  • Structured extraction: prose → JSON.

For these, encoder-decoder makes the cleanest fit. The encoder produces a dense representation of the source. The decoder generates the output, cross-attending to that representation at every step. Training is shift-by-one on the output side. Same loss as GPT, just conditioned on the encoder output.

Two papers defined the modern playbook:

  1. T5 (Raffel et al. 2019). "Text-to-Text Transfer Transformer." Every NLP task reframed as text-in, text-out. Single architecture, single vocabulary, single loss. Pretrained on masked span prediction (corrupt spans in the input, decode them in the output).
  2. BART (Lewis et al. 2019). "Bidirectional and Auto-Regressive Transformer." Denoising autoencoder: corrupt input in multiple ways (shuffle, mask, delete, rotate), ask the decoder to reconstruct the original.

In 2026 the encoder-decoder format lives on where input structure matters:

  • Whisper (speech → text).
  • Dedicated translation models (Marian/OPUS-MT, Meta's NLLB-200), which are small enough to run on a CPU.
  • Google's T5Gemma (2025), encoder–decoder models adapted from Gemma for input-to-output tasks.
  • Flan-T5 and variants for classification and structured extraction.

Decoder-only won the spotlight, but encoder-decoder never went away.

The idea

Idea 1 · The forward loop

source tokens ─▶ encoder ─▶ (N_src, d_model)  ──┐
                                                 │
target tokens ─▶ decoder block                   │
                 ├─▶ masked self-attention       │
                 ├─▶ cross-attention ◀───────────┘
                 └─▶ FFN
                ↓
              next-token logits

Crucially, the encoder runs once per input. The decoder runs autoregressively but cross-attends to the same encoder output at every step. Caching the encoder output is a free speedup for long inputs.

Idea 2 · T5 pretraining — span corruption

Pick random spans of the input (average length 3 tokens, 15% total). Replace each span with a unique sentinel: <extra_id_0>, <extra_id_1>, etc. The decoder outputs only the corrupted spans with their sentinel prefix:

source: The quick <extra_id_0> fox jumps <extra_id_1> dog
target: <extra_id_0> brown <extra_id_1> over the lazy

Cheaper signal than predicting the whole sequence. Competitive with MLM (BERT) and prefix-LM (UniLM) in the T5 paper's ablation.

Idea 3 · BART pretraining — multi-noise denoising

BART tries five noising functions:

  1. Token masking.
  2. Token deletion.
  3. Text infilling (mask a span, decoder inserts the right length).
  4. Sentence permutation.
  5. Document rotation.

Combining text infilling + sentence permutation produced the best downstream numbers. The decoder always reconstructs the original. BART's output is the full sequence, not just the corrupted spans — so pretraining compute is higher than T5.

Idea 4 · Inference

Same autoregressive generation as GPT. Greedy / beam / top-p sampling apply. Beam search (width 4–5) is standard for translation and summarization because the output distribution is narrower than chat.

Idea 5 · When to pick each variant in 2026

Task Encoder-decoder? Why
Translation Yes, usually Clear source sequence; fixed output distribution; beam search works
Speech-to-text Yes (Whisper) Input modality differs from output; encoder shapes audio features
Chat / reasoning No, decoder-only No persistent "input" — the conversation is the sequence
Code completion Usually no Decoder-only with long context wins; code models like Qwen 2.5 Coder are decoder-only
Summarization Either works BART, PEGASUS beat earlier decoder-only baselines; modern decoder-only LLMs match them
Structured extraction Either T5 is clean because "text → text" absorbs any output format

The trend since ~2022: decoder-only takes over tasks that encoder-decoder used to own because (a) instruction-tuned decoder-only LLMs generalize to anything via prompting, (b) one architecture scales easier than two, (c) most post-training tooling (SFT, RLHF, DPO) is built around decoders. Encoder-decoder holds on where input modality differs (speech, images) or where beam search quality matters.

The same idea as a formula

What the model computes. For a source x and an output y, the encoder produces E=Enc(x), one vector per source token, and the decoder factorises

p(y∣x)=t=1∏m​p(yt​∣y<t​, E)

trained with the same shift-by-one cross-entropy as GPT, on the output side only.

Cross-attention. In each decoder block, queries come from the decoder's states H and keys and values from the encoder output:

CrossAttn(H,E)=softmax(dk​​(HWQ​)(EWK​)⊤​)EWV​

with no causal mask, since the whole source is known. EWK​ and EWV​ are computed once and reused at every generation step.

Cost. For a source of n tokens and an output of m: the encoder's attention is O(n2), paid once; each decoder step costs O(t) for self-attention and O(n) for cross-attention. A decoder-only model doing the same task instead carries the n source tokens in its own sequence.

T5 span corruption. Corrupt a fraction r of the tokens in spans of mean length ℓ: about rn/ℓ spans, each replaced by one sentinel. The source shrinks to n−rn+rn/ℓ tokens and the target holds only the removed tokens plus their sentinels, so the decoder's work is a small fraction of the text.

Beam search. Keep the B partial outputs with the highest total ∑t​logp(yt​∣y<t​,E), extend each by every token, keep the best B again; divide by length$^\alpha$ to avoid favouring short outputs. It suits tasks with one right answer, like translation.

Worked example
Span corruption by hand

A 512-token passage, r=0.15, mean span 3, with the lesson's corrupt_spans arithmetic:

  • Tokens removed: ⌊0.15×512⌋=76, in round(76/3)=25 spans.
  • Source: 512−76+25=461 tokens (each span collapsed to one sentinel).
  • Target: the 76 removed tokens, 25 sentinels in front of them, and one closing sentinel: 102 tokens.
  • BART instead reconstructs the whole passage, a 512-token target, five times as long; T5's paper chose span corruption partly for this saving.
  • A decoder-only model given the same 461-token input would hold all of it in its own self-attention at every generated token; the encoder–decoder reads it once and attends to it through cross-attention.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Cross-attention is masked like the decoder's self-attention."
    ✓ It is not: the whole source is available from the start, so every output position may look at every source token.
  • ✗ "The encoder runs again for every generated token."
    ✓ It runs once per input; only the decoder runs once per output token (the run under "Use it" counts 1 against 10).
  • ✗ "T5's target is the whole repaired sentence."
    ✓ T5 predicts only the removed spans, each after its sentinel; BART reconstructs the full text.

Step 1 · span corruption

See code/main.py. We implement T5-style span corruption for a toy corpus — the most useful single piece of this lesson because it shows up in every encoder-decoder pretraining recipe since.

def corrupt_spans(tokens, mask_rate=0.15, mean_span=3.0, rng=None):
    """Pick spans summing to ~mask_rate of tokens. Return (corrupted_input, target)."""
    n = len(tokens)
    n_mask = max(1, int(n * mask_rate))
    n_spans = max(1, int(round(n_mask / mean_span)))
    ...

The target format is the T5 convention: <sent0> span0 <sent1> span1 .... The corrupted input interleaves unchanged tokens with the sentinel tokens at span locations.

Step 2 · verify round-trip

Given the corrupted input and target, reconstruct the original sentence. If your corruption is reversible, the forward pass is well-defined. This is a sanity check — real training never does this, but the test is cheap and catches off-by-one bugs in your span bookkeeping.

Step 3 · BART noising

Five functions: token_mask, token_delete, text_infill, sentence_permute, document_rotate. Compose two of them and show the result.

Reading this output

The corrupted source keeps most tokens and puts one sentinel where each span was; the target lists only the removed spans ("brown", "stitch in"), each after its sentinel, and ends with a closing sentinel. The round trip rebuilds the original exactly, so the bookkeeping is consistent. BART's noises differ in what the decoder must infer: token masking keeps the length, deletion hides where tokens were removed, text infilling hides how many tokens a single <mask> replaced, and permutation and rotation scramble order. In every case BART's target is the full, clean text.

See it drawn
A translation model at work

Helsinki-NLP's opus-mt-en-de (a Marian encoder–decoder used for production translation), with hooks counting how often the encoder and decoder run; then the last decoder layer's cross-attention, averaged over heads.

import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForSeq2SeqLM

repo = "Helsinki-NLP/opus-mt-en-de"                  # a MarianMT encoder-decoder translation model (74M parameters)
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(repo)
mt = AutoModelForSeq2SeqLM.from_pretrained(repo, attn_implementation="eager").eval()
calls = {"encoder": 0, "decoder": 0}
mt.get_encoder().register_forward_hook(lambda *a: calls.__setitem__("encoder", calls["encoder"] + 1))
mt.get_decoder().register_forward_hook(lambda *a: calls.__setitem__("decoder", calls["decoder"] + 1))

enc = tok("The black cat is sleeping on the warm sofa.", return_tensors="pt")
with torch.no_grad():
    out = mt.generate(**enc, max_new_tokens=32, num_beams=1)
print("German:", tok.decode(out[0], skip_special_tokens=True))
print(f"encoder ran {calls['encoder']} time; decoder ran {calls['decoder']} times (once per generated token)\n")

# Cross-attention of the last decoder layer, averaged over heads: where does each output token look?
with torch.no_grad():
    r = mt(**enc, decoder_input_ids=out[:, :-1], output_attentions=True)
xattn = r.cross_attentions[-1][0].mean(0)            # (output length, source length)
src = tok.convert_ids_to_tokens(enc.input_ids[0])
tgt = tok.convert_ids_to_tokens(out[0, 1:])
print("output token -> source token with the most cross-attention")
for t, row in zip(tgt, xattn):
    j = int(row.argmax())
    print(f"  {t:10s} -> {src[j]:10s} {row[j]:.2f}")

Reading this output

The translation is correct, and the counters confirm the formula box: the encoder ran once for the whole sentence, the decoder 10 times, once per output token including the end token. The cross-attention table is a word alignment that nobody labelled: schwarze → black (0.47), Katze → cat (0.73), schläft → sleeping (0.60), warmen → warm, Sofa → sofa (0.74). German word order and English word order happen to match here, but the alignment comes from content, not position. The function words "Die", "auf", "dem" and the full stop put their largest weight on the end-of-source token </s>: they need grammar more than a specific source word, and, like the first token in a decoder-only model, </s> serves as a place to park attention.

T5 doing what it was pretrained to do

The original t5-base checkpoint (span corruption only, no instruction tuning) given sentinel inputs. Its raw output and the spans written back into the gaps.

import re
from transformers import T5ForConditionalGeneration

# The original T5 checkpoint, pretrained on span corruption only (no instruction tuning).
ttok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("google-t5/t5-base")
t5 = T5ForConditionalGeneration.from_pretrained("google-t5/t5-base").eval()
for text in ["The quick brown <extra_id_0> over the lazy dog.",
             "The Eiffel Tower is located in <extra_id_0>, the capital of <extra_id_1>.",
             "Water boils at <extra_id_0> degrees Celsius and freezes at <extra_id_1> degrees Celsius.",
             "After the long <extra_id_0>, the team finally <extra_id_1> the championship."]:
    n = text.count("<extra_id_")
    ids = ttok(text, return_tensors="pt").input_ids
    with torch.no_grad():
        out = t5.generate(ids, max_new_tokens=24)
    raw = ttok.decode(out[0], skip_special_tokens=False).replace("<pad>", "").replace("</s>", "")
    spans = re.split(r"<extra_id_\d+>", raw)[1 : n + 1]          # the decoder's text after each sentinel
    filled = text
    for i, s in enumerate(spans):
        filled = filled.replace(f"<extra_id_{i}>", f"[{s.strip()}]")
    print(f"decoder output: {raw.strip()[:90]}\nfilled in:      {filled}\n")

Reading this output

Each gap is filled with a span after its sentinel, exactly the target format of the lesson's corrupt_spans. The model has no idea where to stop, so after the spans it needs it keeps producing more sentinels and repeats (" Paris…"); the code keeps only the first n. The fills show what span corruption teaches: fluent, plausible spans, not checked facts. "Paris" and "France", "wait" and "won" are right; "dog is" is a likely span after "brown" but breaks the sentence; and water "boils at 0 degrees Celsius and freezes at 0" gets the freezing point right and the boiling point wrong, because "0 degrees Celsius" is simply a frequent span near those words. Turning this into a model that follows instructions is what Flan's multitask tuning added.

Break it on purpose

When training an encoder–decoder by hand, you pass the target tokens as decoder_input_ids exactly as they are (instead of letting the model shift them one position to the right, starting with a start token). Training runs and the loss drops almost to zero within a few hundred steps. What does the decoder see at position t, and what will it do at generation time? (Solution B1.)

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Speech recognition is encoder–decoder. Whisper and its faster variants (distil-whisper, faster-whisper on CTranslate2) are the most widely deployed open speech-to-text models.
  • Dedicated translation models such as Marian/OPUS-MT and Meta's NLLB-200 run in production pipelines and on-premise, because they are small and fast for one job; general LLMs handle translation in chat products.
  • Encoder–decoders made from LLMs. Google's T5Gemma (2025) and T5Gemma 2 adapt Gemma checkpoints into encoder–decoder models aimed at summarisation and other input-to-output tasks.

Sources: Whisper, faster-whisper, OPUS-MT models, NLLB-200, T5Gemma, T5Gemma 2.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Encoder–decoder: an unmasked encoder reads the input once; a causal decoder writes the output and reads the encoder through unmasked cross-attention.
  • T5 pretrains on span corruption (predict only the removed spans); BART on denoising (rebuild the whole text).
  • Cross-attention learns alignments: content words point at their translations.
  • Decoder-only models took over general tasks; encoder–decoders remain strong for speech, translation and fixed input-to-output jobs.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Content words align; function words park

The content words align with their English counterparts: schwarze → black, Katze → cat (0.73), schläft → sleeping, warmen → warm, Sofa → sofa (0.74). "Die", "auf", "dem" and the final full stop do not point at "The" or "on"; their strongest weight is on the end-of-source token </s> (0.39–0.64). Articles and prepositions depend on German grammar (gender, case) as much as on one English word, and a fixed token like </s> serves as a default place to put attention, the encoder–decoder version of the attention sink seen in GPT-2.

SolutionB1 · The decoder sees the answer

Unshifted, the decoder's input at position t is the target token yt​ it is asked to predict, and its causal mask allows it to see position t itself. It learns to copy its input to its output, so the loss collapses towards zero. At generation time it has no access to the next token (it has not been generated yet), so it repeats whatever it was given or produces garbage. The right input is the target shifted right by one with a start token in front (transformers does this automatically when you pass labels), so position t sees only y<t​.
