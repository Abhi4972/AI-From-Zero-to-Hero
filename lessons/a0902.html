In plain words first
The idea in one line

Dynamic programming is RL with cheating. You already know the transition and reward functions; you just iterate the Bellman equation until V or π stops moving. It is the benchmark every sampling-based method tries to approach.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
Policy iteration "DP algorithm" Alternating evaluation ($V^\pi$) and improvement (greedy $\pi$ w.r.t. $V^\pi$) until the policy stops changing.
Value iteration "Faster DP" Bellman optimality backup applied in one sweep; converges to $V^{\ast}$ geometrically.
Bellman operator "The recursion" $(T V)(s) = \max_a \sum P (r + \gamma V(s'))$; a $\gamma$-contraction in sup-norm.
Contraction "Why DP converges" Any operator $T$ with $\lVert T x - T y \rVert \le \gamma \lVert x - y \rVert$ has a unique fixed point.
GPI "Everything is DP" Generalized Policy Iteration: any method driving $V$ and $\pi$ to mutual consistency.
Synchronous update "Jacobi-style" Use old $V$ throughout a sweep; cleanly analyzable but slower.
In-place update "Gauss-Seidel-style" Use $V$ as it's being updated; converges faster in practice.

The problem

In plain words first

If you know the rules of the world exactly (for every state and action, which states can follow, with what probabilities and rewards), you do not need to learn by trial and error: you can compute the best behaviour. Dynamic programming does this by repeating one simple update everywhere: a state's value is the best action's immediate reward plus the discounted value of where that action leads. Policy iteration alternates "work out how good the current plan is" with "switch to the best action everywhere"; value iteration folds both into one repeated sweep. The answers are exact, which makes them the yardstick for every learning method that follows. This lesson runs the lesson's solvers, watches the error shrink sweep by sweep, and then solves real Gymnasium environments from their own rule tables and checks the answers by playing them. Nothing is trained.

You have an MDP with a known model: you can query P(s′∣s,a) and R(s,a,s′) for any state-action pair. An inventory manager knows the demand distribution. A board game has deterministic transitions. A gridworld is four lines of Python. You have a model.

Model-free RL (Q-learning, PPO, REINFORCE) was invented for the case where you don't have a model — you can only sample from the environment. But when you do have one, there are faster, better methods: dynamic programming. Bellman designed them in 1957. They still define correctness: when people say "optimal policy for this MDP," they mean the policy DP would return.

You need them in 2026 for three reasons. First, every tabular environment in RL research (GridWorld, FrozenLake, CliffWalking) is solved with DP to produce the gold-standard policy. Second, exact values let you debug sampling methods: if Q-learning's estimate for V∗(s0​) disagrees with the DP answer by 30%, your Q-learning has a bug. Third, modern offline RL and planning methods (MCTS, AlphaZero's search, model-based RL such as MuZero, Lesson 9.12) all iterate a Bellman backup over a learned or given model.

The idea

Two algorithms, both fixed-point iteration on Bellman.

Policy iteration. Alternates two steps until the policy stops changing.

  1. Evaluation: given policy π, compute Vπ by repeatedly applying V(s)←∑a​π(a∣s)∑s′,r​P(s′,r∣s,a)[r+γV(s′)] until it converges.
  2. Improvement: given Vπ, make π greedy w.r.t. Vπ: π(s)←argmaxa​∑s′,r​P(s′,r∣s,a)[r+γV(s′)].

Convergence is guaranteed because (a) each improvement step either keeps π the same or strictly increases Vπ for some state, (b) the space of deterministic policies is finite. Usually converges in ~5–20 outer iterations even for large state spaces.

Value iteration. Collapses evaluation and improvement into one sweep. Apply the Bellman optimality equation:

V(s)←amax​s′,r∑​P(s′,r∣s,a)[r+γV(s′)]

Repeat until maxs​∣Vnew​(s)−V(s)∣<ϵ. Extract the policy at the end by taking the greedy action. Strictly faster per iteration — no inner evaluation loop — but typically needs more iterations to converge.

Generalized policy iteration (GPI). The unifying framing. Value function and policy are locked in a two-way improvement loop; any method that drives both toward mutual consistency (async value iteration, modified policy iteration, Q-learning, actor-critic, PPO) is an instance of GPI.

Why γ<1 matters. The Bellman operator is a γ-contraction in the sup-norm: ∥TV−TV′∥∞​≤γ∥V−V′∥∞​. Contraction implies unique fixed point and geometric convergence. Drop γ<1 and you lose the guarantee — you need a finite horizon or an absorbing terminal state.

The same idea as a formula

Bellman optimality operator. For any value table V,

(TV)(s)=amax​s′,r∑​P(s′,r∣s,a)[r+γV(s′)]

and the optimal values are its fixed point, V∗=TV∗.

Contraction. For any two tables, ∥TV−TV′∥∞​≤γ∥V−V′∥∞​ (a max of averages cannot differ by more than the largest difference, scaled by γ). Hence a unique fixed point, and value iteration's error after k sweeps is at most γk times the initial error.

Stopping rule. If successive sweeps differ by ε in the sup-norm, the remaining error is at most εγ/(1−γ).

Policy improvement theorem. If π′ is greedy with respect to Vπ, then Vπ′(s)≥Vπ(s) for every state, with equality everywhere only when π is already optimal. Policy iteration therefore improves strictly until it stops, and stops after finitely many steps because there are finitely many deterministic policies.

Greedy policy from values: π∗(s)=argmaxa​∑P(s′,r∣s,a)[r+γV∗(s′)].

Worked example
One Bellman backup by hand

A state with two actions, γ=0.9, current estimates V(s1​)=10, V(s2​)=4:

  • Action A: reward 1, lands in s1​ with probability 0.8 and s2​ with 0.2: 1+0.9(0.8×10+0.2×4)=1+0.9×8.8=8.92.
  • Action B: reward 3, always lands in s2​: 3+0.9×4=6.6.
  • Backup: V(s)←max(8.92,6.6)=8.92, greedy action A, despite B's larger immediate reward.

How many sweeps? With γ=0.9, reaching error 10−6 from an initial error of 10 needs 0.9k×10≤10−6, so k≥ln(10−7)/ln0.9≈153 sweeps at worst. With γ=0.99 the bound becomes about 1,600 sweeps; absorbing goal states usually make it far faster in practice.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Value iteration needs the right starting values."
    ✓ Any start converges to the same V∗ because the operator is a contraction; good starts only save sweeps.
  • ✗ "Policy iteration and value iteration can give different optimal policies."
    ✓ They reach the same V∗; policies can differ only between actions that tie exactly (the run after Step 5 finds identical policies).
  • ✗ "The optimal policy always takes the shortest path."
    ✓ It maximises expected return; on slippery ice it may walk away from the goal to avoid holes (FrozenLake, under "Use it").

Step 1 · build the GridWorld MDP model

Use the same 4×4 GridWorld from Lesson 01. We add a stochastic variant: with probability 0.1 the agent slips to a random perpendicular direction.

SLIP = 0.1

def transitions(state, action):
    if state == TERMINAL:
        return [(state, 0.0, 1.0)]
    outcomes = []
    for direction, prob in action_probs(action):
        outcomes.append((apply_move(state, direction), -1.0, prob))
    return outcomes

transitions(s, a) returns a list of (s', r, p). This is the entire model.

Step 2 · policy evaluation

Given a policy π(s) = {action: prob}, iterate the Bellman equation until V stops moving:

def policy_evaluation(policy, gamma=0.99, tol=1e-6):
    V = {s: 0.0 for s in states()}
    while True:
        delta = 0.0
        for s in states():
            v = sum(pi_a * sum(p * (r + gamma * V[s_prime])
                              for s_prime, r, p in transitions(s, a))
                   for a, pi_a in policy(s).items())
            delta = max(delta, abs(v - V[s]))
            V[s] = v
        if delta < tol:
            return V

Step 3 · policy improvement

Replace π with the greedy policy w.r.t. V. If π did not change, return — we are at the optimum.

def policy_improvement(V, gamma=0.99):
    new_policy = {}
    for s in states():
        best_a = max(
            ACTIONS,
            key=lambda a: sum(p * (r + gamma * V[s_prime])
                              for s_prime, r, p in transitions(s, a)),
        )
        new_policy[s] = best_a
    return new_policy

Step 4 · stitch them together

def policy_iteration(gamma=0.99):
    policy = {s: "up" for s in states()}   # arbitrary start
    for _ in range(100):
        V = policy_evaluation(lambda s: {policy[s]: 1.0}, gamma)
        new_policy = policy_improvement(V, gamma)
        if new_policy == policy:
            return V, policy
        policy = new_policy

Typical convergence on 4×4: 4–6 outer iterations. Outputs V∗(0,0)≈−6 and a policy that strictly decreases the step count.

Step 5 · value iteration (the one-loop version)

def value_iteration(gamma=0.99, tol=1e-6):
    V = {s: 0.0 for s in states()}
    while True:
        delta = 0.0
        for s in states():
            v = max(sum(p * (r + gamma * V[s_prime])
                       for s_prime, r, p in transitions(s, a))
                   for a in ACTIONS)
            delta = max(delta, abs(v - V[s]))
            V[s] = v
        if delta < tol:
            break
    policy = policy_improvement(V, gamma)
    return V, policy

Same fixed point, fewer lines of code.

Pitfalls

  • Forgetting to handle terminals. If you apply Bellman to an absorbing state, it still picks up a "best action" that changes nothing. Guard with if s == terminal: V[s] = 0.
  • Sup-norm vs L2 convergence. Use max |V_new - V|, not average. The theoretical guarantee is on the sup-norm.
  • In-place vs synchronous updates. Updating V[s] in-place (Gauss-Seidel) converges faster than a separate V_new dict (Jacobi). Production code uses in-place.
  • Policy ties. If two actions have equal Q-value, argmax may break ties differently each iteration, causing the "policy stable" check to oscillate. Use a stable tie-break (first action in fixed order).
  • State-space explosion. DP is O(∣S∣⋅∣A∣) per sweep. Works up to ~10⁷ states. Beyond that, you need function approximation (Phase 9 · 05 onwards).

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

In 2026, DP is the correctness baseline and the inner loop of planners:

Use case Method
Solve a small tabular MDP exactly Value iteration (simpler) or policy iteration (fewer outer steps)
Verify a Q-learning / PPO implementation Compare to DP-optimal V∗ on a toy environment
Model-based RL (MuZero, Dreamer) Bellman backup or planning on a learned transition model
Planning in AlphaZero / MuZero Monte Carlo Tree Search = async Bellman backup
Offline RL (CQL, IQL) Conservative Q-iteration — DP with a penalty on OOD actions

Every time someone says "the optimal value function," they mean "the DP fixed point." When you see V∗ or Q∗ in a paper, picture this loop.

Solving Gymnasium environments exactly

Value iteration over the transition tables Gymnasium exposes (env.unwrapped.P) for FrozenLake 4 × 4 and 8 × 8 (slippery) and CliffWalking, then the optimal policies played in the real environments.

import numpy as np, gymnasium as gym

def solve(env, gamma=1.0, tol=1e-10):
    """Value iteration on a Gymnasium environment's own transition table env.unwrapped.P."""
    P, nS, nA = env.unwrapped.P, env.observation_space.n, env.action_space.n
    V = np.zeros(nS); sweeps = 0
    while True:
        Q = np.array([[sum(p * (r + gamma * V[s2] * (not d)) for p, s2, r, d in P[s][a]) for a in range(nA)] for s in range(nS)])
        sweeps += 1
        if np.max(np.abs(Q.max(1) - V)) < tol: return Q.max(1), Q.argmax(1), sweeps
        V = Q.max(1)

def play(env, policy, n=10_000, seed=0):
    total = 0.0
    for ep in range(n):
        s, _ = env.reset(seed=seed + ep); done = False
        while not done:
            s, r, term, trunc, _ = env.step(int(policy[s])); total += r; done = term or trunc
    return total / n

for name, kw in (("FrozenLake 4x4", dict(id="FrozenLake-v1", is_slippery=True)),
                 ("FrozenLake 8x8", dict(id="FrozenLake-v1", map_name="8x8", is_slippery=True))):
    env = gym.make(**kw, max_episode_steps=1000)
    V, pi, sweeps = solve(env)
    n = int(np.sqrt(env.observation_space.n))
    print(f"{name}: {sweeps} sweeps; optimal chance of reaching the goal from the start {V[0]:.4f}; "
          f"played 10,000 times with the optimal policy: {play(env, pi):.4f}")
    if n == 4:
        arrows = np.array(list("<v>^"))[pi].reshape(4, 4); desc = env.unwrapped.desc.astype(str)
        print("  policy (H = hole, G = goal):  " + " / ".join("".join(d if d in "HG" else a for a, d in zip(ar, dr))
                                                          for ar, dr in zip(arrows, desc)))

cliff = gym.make("CliffWalking-v1")
V, pi, sweeps = solve(cliff, gamma=1.0)
print(f"CliffWalking (-1 per step, -100 for the cliff): {sweeps} sweeps, optimal return from the start {V[36]:.0f} "
      f"(the shortest path along the cliff edge is 13 steps)")

Break it on purpose

In policy_iteration, replace "stop when the policy does not change" with "stop when the policy's value does not change", but break ties in policy_improvement randomly instead of by a fixed action order. On a grid where two actions are equally good in several cells, what does the loop do? And what goes wrong if you keep the original policy-equality test with random tie-breaking? (Solution B1.)

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • DP as the reference answer. Tabular benchmarks in Gymnasium (FrozenLake, CliffWalking, Taxi) are solved exactly by value iteration to check learning algorithms; libraries such as mdptoolbox and pymdptoolbox provide the classic solvers.
  • Bellman backups inside modern methods. MuZero-style planners (and DeepMind's open mctx library) back values up through a search tree; offline RL methods such as CQL and IQL are Q-iteration with conservative penalties; operations research still solves inventory and maintenance MDPs exactly.
  • Approximate DP at scale. When the state space is too large to enumerate, the same backup is applied to a neural network's estimates: fitted Q-iteration and DQN (Lesson 9.5).

Sources: Gymnasium toy text environments, mctx, Sutton & Barto ch. 4, IQL.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • With a known model, compute optimal behaviour instead of learning it.
  • Value iteration repeats V←TV; the error shrinks by at least γ per sweep.
  • Policy iteration alternates exact evaluation and greedy improvement; few outer steps.
  • Both reach the same V∗; extract π∗ greedily.
  • DP answers are the yardstick for sampling-based RL.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · About 100%

About 100%: with no discount and enough time, the optimal policy on slippery FrozenLake 8 × 8 reaches the goal in every one of 10,000 games (exact value 1.0000). With γ=1 there is no penalty for taking long, so the best policy uses moves whose possible slips never lead into a hole, usually pushing against walls or along safe corridors, and waits for the ice to carry it forward. With a discount below 1 the optimal policy would accept some risk to arrive sooner, and its success rate would drop. "Optimal" always means optimal for the reward and discount you specified.

SolutionB1 · Endless flipping

With random tie-breaking, each improvement step may pick a different one of two equally good actions in some cells, so the policy changes every iteration even though nothing improves. The original test, "stop when the policy is unchanged", then never fires and the loop runs until its iteration limit. Testing the value instead fixes termination: tied policies have identical values, so the loop stops as soon as no state's value improves. The standard fixes are a fixed tie-break order (first best action, as policy_improvement does with max over a fixed action tuple) or stopping when values stop improving; never compare policies that are allowed to break ties randomly.
