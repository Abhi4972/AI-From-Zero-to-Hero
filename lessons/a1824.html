In plain words first
The idea in one line

Four primary regulatory regimes define the 2026 AI governance landscape. EU AI Act (in force 1 August 2024) — prohibited practices and AI literacy from 2 February 2025; GPAI obligations from 2 August 2025; Article 50 transparency and most remaining provisions 2 August 2026; high-risk obligations postponed by the 2026 Digital Omnibus to 2 December 2027 (Annex III uses) and 2 August 2028 (high-risk AI embedded in regulated products); fines up to 35M EUR or 7% of global turnover for prohibited practices and up to 15M EUR or 3% for most other breaches. GPAI Code of Practice (10 July 2025): three chapters — Transparency, Copyright, Safety and Security — 12 commitments; enforcement begins August 2026. UK AISI -> AI Security Institute (February 2025): rename signals narrower scope. US AISI -> CAISI (June 2025): Center for AI Standards and Innovation under NIST; shift toward pro-growth posture. Korean AI Framework Act (passed December 2024, effective January 2026): Article 12 establishes AISI under MSIT; mandates local representatives for foreign AI companies, risk assessment, safety measures for high-impact and generative AI.

The problem

In plain words first

Lab safety frameworks (Lesson 18) are voluntary; laws are not. Between 2024 and 2026 the first broad AI laws came into force, and they differ by country. The EU AI Act sorts AI uses by risk: some practices are banned outright, high-risk uses such as hiring or credit scoring need risk management and conformity checks, general-purpose AI (GPAI) models have their own duties, and systems that talk to people or generate content have transparency duties. Its rules switch on in stages, and a 2026 amendment (the Digital Omnibus) pushed the high-risk stages back. The UK and US renamed their AI safety institutes in 2025 toward security and standards; South Korea passed a broad AI law effective January 2026. If you deploy in several places, you map each obligation to a date and a control.

Lab frameworks (Lesson 18.18) are voluntary. Regulatory frameworks are compulsory. The 2024-2026 period saw the first wave of comprehensive AI regulation enter force. Deployers must map technical controls to regulatory obligations; the mapping differs by jurisdiction.

Idea 1 · EU AI Act

In force 1 August 2024. Risk-tier structure:

  • Prohibited practices (Article 5). Social scoring, real-time remote biometric identification in public (with law-enforcement exceptions), exploitative manipulation of vulnerable groups. Applied 2 February 2025.
  • High-risk systems (Annex III). Employment, education, credit, law enforcement, justice, migration. Require conformity assessment, risk management, logging, transparency.
  • General-Purpose AI (GPAI) models. Applied 2 August 2025. All GPAI providers have obligations; systemic-risk GPAI (>1e25 FLOP training compute) have additional obligations.
  • Limited-risk systems. Transparency obligations under Article 50 (AI-generated content labelling). Applied 2 August 2026.

Timeline, as amended by the Digital Omnibus on AI (Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, adopted in 2026): - 2 Feb 2025: prohibited practices + AI literacy. - 2 Aug 2025: GPAI obligations + governance + penalty regime. - 2 Aug 2026: Article 50 transparency and the Commission's power to fine GPAI providers. Generative systems already on the market before this date have until 2 Dec 2026 for the Article 50(2) marking duty. - 2 Dec 2026: two new prohibited practices added by the Omnibus (AI that generates non-consensual intimate imagery, and child sexual abuse material). - 2 Aug 2027: GPAI models placed on the market before 2 Aug 2025 must comply. - 2 Dec 2027: high-risk obligations for Annex III uses (employment, education, credit and the rest), postponed from 2 Aug 2026. - 2 Aug 2028: high-risk AI that is a safety component of products under EU product law (Annex I), postponed from 2 Aug 2027.

Fines reach 35M EUR or 7% of global annual turnover for prohibited practices, and 15M EUR or 3% for most other obligations.

Idea 2 · GPAI Code of Practice

Published 10 July 2025. Three chapters:

  • Transparency. All GPAI providers.
  • Copyright. All GPAI providers.
  • Safety and Security. Systemic-risk GPAI providers (estimated 5-15 companies).

12 commitments total. A Signatory Taskforce chaired by the AI Office manages implementation. Enforcement begins 2 August 2026; until then, good-faith compliance is accepted.

Idea 3 · Transparency Code for Article 50

First draft 17 December 2025. Second draft March 2026. Final version June 2026. Covers AI-generated content labelling including deepfakes — the regulatory layer that requires Lesson 18.23's watermarking technology.

Idea 4 · UK AI Security Institute (February 2025)

Renamed from AI Safety Institute. The rebrand narrows scope: drops algorithmic bias and free-speech framings; focuses on frontier capability security. Open-sourced the Inspect evaluation tool (May 2024). Collaborates with Redwood (Lesson 18.10) on control safety cases.

Idea 5 · US CAISI (June 2025)

Trump administration transforms NIST's AI Safety Institute into the Center for AI Standards and Innovation. Shift toward "pro-growth AI policies" per VP Vance's Paris AI Action Summit remarks. Reduced emphasis on pre-deployment evaluation; emphasis on standards and innovation support. Domestic counterweight to EU AI Act's regulatory posture.

Idea 6 · Korean AI Framework Act

Passed December 2024. Enacted January 2025. Effective January 2026. Consolidates 19 separate AI bills.

Article 12 establishes an AISI under the Ministry of Science and ICT (MSIT). Mandates: - Local representatives for foreign AI companies operating in Korea. - Risk assessment for "high-impact" AI systems. - Safety measures for generative AI and high-impact AI.

First Asian jurisdiction with a comprehensive horizontal AI regulation.

Idea 7 · Cross-jurisdiction dynamics

  • EU: strict, risk-tiered, heavy penalties. Benchmark for privacy-adjacent regulation.
  • US: innovation-favouring, decentralized, states (e.g., California AB 2013 — Lesson 18.27) fill federal gaps.
  • UK: narrow security focus, strong evaluation infrastructure.
  • Korea: MSIT-led, foreign-provider-focused.

Competing regulatory philosophies. Deployers in multiple jurisdictions have to comply with the strictest, which in 2026 is typically the EU AI Act.

Worked example
Mapping one deployment to EU dates

A company offers a chatbot that also screens job applications, built on a third-party GPAI model, available in the EU since 2025.

  1. Chatbot. Users must be told they are talking to an AI (Article 50): from 2 August 2026.
  2. Generated text and images. If the system generates synthetic content, the provider must mark it machine-readably; for a system already on the market before August 2026, by 2 December 2026.
  3. Application screening. Employment is an Annex III high-risk use: risk management, logging, human oversight, conformity assessment, now from 2 December 2027.
  4. The underlying model. GPAI duties fall on the model provider, not this company, but the company should get the documentation the provider must supply, since it needs it for its own obligations.

An obligation calendar for one deployment
from datetime import date
RULES = [   # (obligation, applies to, date) under the AI Act as amended in 2026
    ("tell users they are talking to an AI", "interacts", date(2026, 8, 2)),
    ("machine-readable marking of output (new systems)", "generates_new", date(2026, 8, 2)),
    ("machine-readable marking of output (on market before Aug 2026)", "generates_old", date(2026, 12, 2)),
    ("high-risk duties for an Annex III use", "annex3", date(2027, 12, 2)),
    ("high-risk duties for an Annex I product", "annex1", date(2028, 8, 2)),
]
def calendar(placed, interacts, generates, annex3=False, annex1=False):
    tags = {"interacts"} if interacts else set()
    if generates: tags.add("generates_old" if placed < date(2026, 8, 2) else "generates_new")
    if annex3: tags.add("annex3")
    if annex1: tags.add("annex1")
    return sorted((d, ob) for ob, tag, d in RULES if tag in tags)

for d, ob in calendar(date(2026, 3, 1), interacts=True, generates=True, annex3=True):
    print(d.isoformat(), " ", ob)

Reading this output

For a system placed on the market in March 2026, the first duty is telling users they are talking to an AI (August 2026), then marking generated output (December 2026, because it was already on the market), and much later the high-risk duties for employment screening (December 2027). Dates like these change through amendments, as the 2026 Omnibus showed, so a compliance calendar should cite the legal text and its version for every row.

The script prints a timeline built before the 2026 amendments. Its entries up to the Article 50 Code match the record (the final Code was published on 10 June 2026), but its last line is out of date: under the Digital Omnibus, embedded high-risk systems moved to 2 August 2028 and Annex III high-risk uses to 2 December 2027; only legacy GPAI models keep 2 August 2027. Given a deployment description (provider jurisdiction, infrastructure jurisdiction, user jurisdiction), map the applicable AI regulatory obligations.

Produce:

1. EU exposure. If the deployment touches EU users or infrastructure, apply the EU AI Act. Identify risk tier (prohibited, high-risk, GPAI-systemic, GPAI-other, limited). State the deadline for each obligation class.
2. UK exposure. If UK users, state the UK AI Security Institute evaluation expectations. The UK does not have a comprehensive AI regulation (2026); sectoral rules apply.
3. US exposure. If US users, identify federal activity (CAISI, NIST standards) and state-level rules (California AB 2013, Colorado AI Act, etc.). Federal framework is pro-growth; state rules set the floor.
4. Korea exposure. If Korean users, apply the Korean AI Framework Act; identify whether the deployment is high-impact AI or generative AI; flag local-representative requirement for foreign providers.
5. Binding-rule determination. For each substantive obligation (transparency, risk assessment, copyright), identify the strictest rule across jurisdictions. That is the binding rule.

Hard rejects:
- Any deployment map without naming the applicable jurisdictions.
- Any EU exposure assessment without risk-tier identification.
- Any US exposure assessment that ignores state-level rules.

Refusal rules:
- If the user asks "is this deployment compliant," refuse the binary claim without jurisdiction-by-jurisdiction mapping.
- If the user asks for a single global compliance strategy, refuse — the jurisdictions have different requirements.

Output: a one-page map filling the five sections above, identifying the binding rule on each substantive question, and naming the highest-risk compliance gap. Cite EU AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689), GPAI Code of Practice (2025), and Korean AI Framework Act once each.

Exercises

  1. Read the EU AI Act (regulation 2024/1689) and the GPAI Code of Practice (10 July 2025). Identify three obligations that apply to every GPAI provider and three that apply only to systemic-risk GPAI.

  2. A deployment is made by a US company, runs on EU infrastructure, and serves Korean users. Which three jurisdictions' rules apply, and which rule binds on each substantive question?

  3. The UK AI Security Institute's rename narrows scope. Argue for and against the narrower framing. Identify the policy assumption each position depends on.

  4. CAISI's "pro-growth" framing is a departure from the 2022-2024 AI safety institute model. Identify two measurable policy shifts that would follow from this framing.

  5. Korea's AI Framework Act requires local representatives for foreign providers. Describe the operational implications for a Bay Area company serving Korean users.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · All providers against systemic-risk providers

Every GPAI provider (Article 53): keep technical documentation for the AI Office and downstream providers; put in place a policy to comply with EU copyright law, including text-and-data-mining opt-outs; publish a sufficiently detailed summary of training content using the Commission's template. Systemic-risk providers additionally (Article 55): evaluate the model, including adversarial testing; assess and mitigate systemic risks; track and report serious incidents; ensure adequate cybersecurity for the model and its infrastructure.

SolutionB1 · Two corrections

After the 2026 Digital Omnibus, high-risk duties for Annex III uses such as employment apply from 2 December 2027, not 2 August 2026 (a tool already on the market is covered once substantially modified after that date). Fines for most breaches reach 15M EUR or 3% of turnover, whichever is higher, but prohibited practices reach 35M EUR or 7%. The memo should cite the amended regulation and its date.
