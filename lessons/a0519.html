The idea in one line

Word tokenizers choke on unseen words. Character tokenizers blow up sequence length. Subword tokenizers split the difference. Every modern LLM ships on one.

The problem

In plain words first

A tokenizer decides the pieces a language model reads. Whole words fail on any word they have not seen; single characters make sequences very long. Subword tokenizers sit in between: frequent words stay whole, rare words are split into familiar pieces. Lesson 5.8 built byte-pair encoding (BPE), which repeatedly merges the most frequent pair of symbols. This lesson adds the other two algorithms in production use. WordPiece, used by BERT, merges the pair that most improves the likelihood of the training text. Unigram, used by T5 and many multilingual models, starts with a large vocabulary, prunes it, and treats segmentation as choosing the most probable split, so it can also sample different splits during training as data augmentation. You will see the math of each, train two real tokenizers on a novel in seconds, and measure what newer OpenAI vocabularies changed for non-English text.

Your vocabulary has 50,000 words. A user types "untokenizable". Your tokenizer returns [UNK]. The model now has no signal about the word. Worse: every document contains rare words (names, typos, technical terms, new coinages), and each [UNK] erases whatever that word contributed.

Subword tokenization solves this. Common words stay single tokens. Rare words decompose into meaningful pieces: untokenizable → un, token, izable. Training data covers everything because any string is ultimately a sequence of bytes.

Every frontier LLM in 2026 ships on one of three algorithms (BPE, Unigram, WordPiece), wrapped in one of three libraries (tiktoken, SentencePiece, HF Tokenizers). You cannot ship a language model without picking one.

The idea

BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding). Start with a character-level vocabulary. Count every adjacent pair. Merge the most frequent pair into a new token. Repeat until you hit the target vocabulary size. Dominant algorithm: GPT-2/3/4, Llama, Gemma, Qwen2, Mistral.

Byte-level BPE. Same algorithm but over raw bytes (256 base tokens) instead of Unicode characters. Guarantees zero [UNK] tokens — any byte sequence encodes. GPT-2 uses 50,257 tokens (256 bytes + 50,000 merges + 1 special).

Unigram. Start with a huge vocabulary. Assign each token a unigram probability. Iteratively prune tokens whose removal least increases the corpus log-likelihood. Probabilistic at inference: can sample tokenizations (useful for data augmentation via subword regularization). Used by T5, mBART, ALBERT, XLNet, Gemma.

WordPiece. Merge pairs that maximize likelihood of the training corpus rather than raw frequency. Used by BERT, DistilBERT, ELECTRA.

SentencePiece vs tiktoken. SentencePiece is the library that trains vocabularies (BPE or Unigram) directly on raw Unicode text, encoding whitespace as ▁. tiktoken is OpenAI's fast encoder against pre-built vocabularies; it does not train.

Rule of thumb:

  • Training a new vocabulary: SentencePiece (multilingual, no pre-tokenization) or HF Tokenizers.
  • Fast inference against GPT vocab: tiktoken (cl100k_base, o200k_base).
  • Both: HF Tokenizers — one library, training + serving.

The same idea as a formula

BPE (as in Lesson 5.8) merges the pair with the highest count, argmax(a,b)​c(a,b). It is greedy compression: each merge removes as many symbols from the corpus as possible.

WordPiece merges the pair that most increases the likelihood of the corpus under a unigram model of the current symbols. Because adding the symbol ab changes the likelihood by roughly logp(a)p(b)p(ab)​ per occurrence, the merge score is

score(a,b)=c(a)c(b)c(a,b)​

It prefers pairs whose parts rarely occur apart, not merely frequent pairs: "q" + "u" scores high because "q" almost never appears without "u".

Unigram (Kudo, 2018) gives each piece x a probability p(x) and a segmentation x=(x1​,…,xm​) of a word the probability P(x)=∏i​p(xi​). The tokenizer outputs the most probable segmentation, found by Viterbi over the positions of the word. Training alternates: 1. E-step: given p, compute the expected count of each piece over all segmentations of the corpus. 2. M-step: set p(x)∝ expected count. 3. Prune: remove the 10-20% of pieces whose removal lowers the corpus log-likelihood ∑w​log∑x∈S(w)​P(x) the least, until the target size is reached.

Subword regularisation. Instead of always the best segmentation, sample one with probability ∝P(x)α during training, so the model sees "unbelievable" split several ways and becomes robust to segmentation; α near 0 is close to uniform, large α is close to always-best.

Worked example
Three algorithms, one tiny corpus

Corpus symbol counts after splitting into characters: "h" 10, "u" 12, "g" 6, "q" 2, and pair counts "h u" 8, "u g" 6, "q u" 2.

  • BPE picks the most frequent pair: "h u" (8).
  • WordPiece scores: "h u" =8/(10×12)=0.067; "u g" =6/(12×6)=0.083; "q u" =2/(2×12)=0.083. It picks "u g" or "q u", pairs whose parts rarely occur apart ("g" never appears without a preceding "u" here, nor "q" without a following "u").
  • Unigram, segmenting "hug" with p(hu)=0.05, p(ug)=0.04, p(h)=0.1, p(g)=0.05, p(hug)=0.001: candidates "hu g" =0.05×0.05=0.0025, "h ug" =0.1×0.04=0.004, "hug" =0.001. Viterbi returns "h ug"; sampling with α=1 would return "hu g" about 0.0025/0.0075=33% of the time.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Tokenizers just split on spaces and punctuation."
    ✓ Subword tokenizers split inside words using learned statistics; SentencePiece does not even assume spaces exist, which matters for Chinese, Japanese and Thai.
  • ✗ "The same text always gives the same tokens."
    ✓ Only for a fixed tokenizer, fixed normalisation and fixed whitespace handling. "hello" and " hello" differ, and Unigram can sample segmentations on purpose.
  • ✗ "Bigger vocabularies are always better."
    ✓ They shorten sequences but enlarge the embedding and output layers and leave rare tokens undertrained; the best size grows with model size.

Step 1 · BPE from scratch

See code/main.py. The loop:

def train_bpe(corpus, num_merges):
    vocab = {tuple(word) + ("</w>",): count for word, count in corpus.items()}
    merges = []
    for _ in range(num_merges):
        pairs = Counter()
        for symbols, freq in vocab.items():
            for a, b in zip(symbols, symbols[1:]):
                pairs[(a, b)] += freq
        if not pairs:
            break
        best = pairs.most_common(1)[0][0]
        merges.append(best)
        vocab = apply_merge(vocab, best)
    return merges

Three facts the algorithm encodes. </w> marks word end so "low" (suffix) and "lower" (prefix) stay distinct. Frequency weighting makes high-frequency pairs win early. The merge list is ordered — inference applies merges in training order.

Step 2 · encode with the learned merges

def encode_bpe(word, merges):
    symbols = list(word) + ["</w>"]
    for a, b in merges:
        i = 0
        while i < len(symbols) - 1:
            if symbols[i] == a and symbols[i + 1] == b:
                symbols = symbols[:i] + [a + b] + symbols[i + 2:]
            else:
                i += 1
    return symbols

Naive O(n⋅∣merges∣). Production implementations (tiktoken, HF Tokenizers) use merge-rank lookup with priority queues and run in near-linear time.

Step 3 · SentencePiece in practice

import sentencepiece as spm
import nltk
from nltk.corpus import gutenberg
nltk.download("gutenberg", quiet=True)

with open("corpus.txt", "w") as f:                       # Jane Austen's Emma, one sentence per line
    for sent in gutenberg.sents("austen-emma.txt"):
        f.write(" ".join(sent) + "\n")

for model_type in ("bpe", "unigram"):
    spm.SentencePieceTrainer.train(
        input="corpus.txt",
        model_prefix=f"my_tokenizer_{model_type}",
        vocab_size=4000,           # Emma is small: 8,000 pieces would mostly be whole words
        model_type=model_type,
        character_coverage=1.0,    # all characters kept; use 0.9995 for large multilingual corpora
        normalization_rule_name="nmt_nfkc",
        minloglevel=2,
    )

sp = spm.SentencePieceProcessor(model_file="my_tokenizer_bpe.model")
print(sp.encode("untokenizable", out_type=str))

Reading this output

Training a SentencePiece model on a whole novel takes a second or two. The lesson's listing shows ['▁un', 'token', 'izable'], a split from a larger, more general corpus; a 4,000-piece vocabulary learned from Emma has never seen "token", so it uses smaller pieces. Which pieces exist depends entirely on the training text, which is why domain-specific tokenizers (code, chemistry, a new language) are trained on that domain.

Notice: no pre-tokenization required, space encoded as ▁, character_coverage controls how aggressively rare characters are preserved vs mapped to <unk>.

Break it on purpose

In the lesson's encode_bpe, apply the merges in reverse order (for a, b in reversed(merges)). Encode a word whose pieces were built up step by step, such as "lower" with merges learned from Lesson 5.8's toy corpus. What happens, and why does order matter? (Solution B1.)

Pitfalls that still ship in 2026

  • Tokenizer drift. Training on vocab A, deploying against vocab B. Token IDs differ; model outputs garbage. Check tokenizer.json hash in CI.
  • Whitespace ambiguity. BPE "hello" vs " hello" produce different tokens. Always specify add_special_tokens and add_prefix_space explicitly.
  • Multilingual undertraining. English-heavy corpora produce vocabularies that split non-Latin scripts into 5-10x more tokens. With GPT-2's tokenizer the same sentence costs several times more in Arabic or Hindi than in English, and even more for some Indic scripts (measured in Lesson 5.27). OpenAI's o200k_base (GPT-4o, 2024) cut the gap substantially compared with cl100k_base (GPT-3.5 and GPT-4); the experiment under "Use it" measures by how much.
  • Emoji splits. A single emoji can take 5 tokens. Checkpoint emoji handling when budgeting context.

Reading this output

The two algorithms make visibly different choices. BPE keeps frequent words whole ("happiness", "Highbury"), because merges build them up completely, and covers unseen words with arbitrary fragments: "untokenizable" becomes un | t | ok | en | i | z | able. Unigram, choosing the most probable combination of the pieces it kept, prefers reusable parts even for frequent words: happi | ness, High | bury, and un | to | ken | iz | able, which lines up with how a reader would split the word. Neither is wrong; they encode the ordinary sentence in a similar number of pieces (13 and 14). The sampling lines show Unigram's unique trick: at alpha 0.1, 30 samples of "unbelievably" give 28 different segmentations; at alpha 1.0 only 5, all close to the best one. Training on sampled segmentations makes a model less brittle to how a word happens to be split.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Vocabulary sizes grew. Llama 3 (about 128k), GPT-4o (o200k_base, about 200k) and Gemma 3 (about 262k) use much larger vocabularies than GPT-2's 50k, mainly to encode non-English text and code in fewer tokens.
  • Training your own tokenizer. Hugging Face tokenizers (byte-level BPE, as in this lesson) and SentencePiece are the standard tools when a domain-specific model needs one; most teams fine-tune an existing model and keep its tokenizer.
  • Tokenizer quirks reach production. Rare, under-trained vocabulary entries ("glitch tokens") have made deployed models misbehave, one reason providers keep tokenizers fixed across a model family.

Sources: Hugging Face tokenizers, SentencePiece, tiktoken, Fishing for Magikarp: glitch tokens.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

The 2026 stack:

Situation Pick
Training a monolingual model from scratch HF Tokenizers (BPE)
Training a multilingual model SentencePiece (Unigram, character_coverage=0.9995)
Serving an OpenAI-compatible API tiktoken (o200k_base for GPT-4+)
Domain-specific vocab (code, math, protein) Train custom BPE on domain corpus, merge with base vocab
Edge inference, small model A small vocabulary (8k to 32k) of either type: the embedding and output layers shrink with it

Vocabulary size is a scaling decision, not a constant. Rough heuristic: 32k for <1B params, 50-100k for 1-10B, 200k+ for multilingual/frontier. Tao et al. (NeurIPS 2024) fitted scaling laws for vocabulary size and concluded that most LLMs use vocabularies smaller than compute-optimal, and that the optimal vocabulary grows with model size.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • BPE merges the most frequent pair; WordPiece merges the pair with the highest c(a,b)/(c(a)c(b)); Unigram prunes a large vocabulary and picks the most probable segmentation with Viterbi.
  • Byte-level vocabularies never produce [UNK]; SentencePiece trains directly on raw text with spaces as ▁.
  • Unigram can sample segmentations (subword regularisation) to make models robust.
  • The tokenizer must match the model exactly, including whitespace handling and normalisation.
  • Larger vocabularies shorten non-English text and grow with model size, at the cost of bigger embedding layers.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Only partly

"Woodhouse", which occurs hundreds of times, is a single piece in both. But they disagree elsewhere, even on the novel's own words: BPE keeps "Highbury" whole while Unigram splits it into High | bury, because Unigram's pruning keeps pieces that are useful across many words and its segmentation picks the most probable combination. For the unseen "untokenizable", BPE falls back to small fragments (un | t | ok | en | i | z | able) while Unigram finds un | to | ken | iz | able. Same data, same vocabulary size, different inventories and different splits.

SolutionB1 · Pieces never form

With reversed merges, a merge like low + er is tried before lo + w or e + r have been applied, so the symbols it needs do not exist yet and nothing happens; by the time the early merges run, the later ones have already been passed. The word stays split into small pieces, usually single characters plus a few short pairs. Each merge was learned on text that already contained all earlier merges, so encoding must replay them in exactly that order: that is why tokenizers store merges as a ranked list.
