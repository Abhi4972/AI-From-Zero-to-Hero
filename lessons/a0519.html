The problem

In plain words first

A tokenizer decides the pieces a language model reads. Whole words fail on any word they have not seen; single characters make sequences very long. Subword tokenizers sit in between: frequent words stay whole, rare words are split into familiar pieces. Lesson 5.4 built byte-pair encoding (BPE), which repeatedly merges the most frequent pair of symbols. This lesson adds the other two algorithms in production use. WordPiece, used by BERT, merges the pair that most improves the likelihood of the training text. Unigram, used by T5 and many multilingual models, starts with a large vocabulary, prunes it, and treats segmentation as choosing the most probable split, so it can also sample different splits during training as data augmentation. You will see the math of each, train two real tokenizers on a novel in seconds, and measure what newer OpenAI vocabularies changed for non-English text.

Your vocabulary has 50,000 words. A user types "untokenizable". Your tokenizer returns [UNK]. The model now has no signal about the word. Worse: every document contains rare words (names, typos, technical terms, new coinages), and each [UNK] erases whatever that word contributed.

Subword tokenization solves this. Common words stay single tokens. Rare words decompose into meaningful pieces: untokenizable → un, token, izable. Training data covers everything because any string is ultimately a sequence of bytes.

Every frontier LLM in 2026 ships on one of three algorithms (BPE, Unigram, WordPiece), wrapped in one of three libraries (tiktoken, SentencePiece, HF Tokenizers). You cannot ship a language model without picking one.

The idea

BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding). Start with a character-level vocabulary. Count every adjacent pair. Merge the most frequent pair into a new token. Repeat until you hit the target vocabulary size. Dominant algorithm: GPT-2/3/4, Llama, Gemma, Qwen2, Mistral.

Byte-level BPE. Same algorithm but over raw bytes (256 base tokens) instead of Unicode characters. Guarantees zero [UNK] tokens — any byte sequence encodes. GPT-2 uses 50,257 tokens (256 bytes + 50,000 merges + 1 special).

Unigram. Start with a huge vocabulary. Assign each token a unigram probability. Iteratively prune tokens whose removal least increases the corpus log-likelihood. Probabilistic at inference: can sample tokenizations (useful for data augmentation via subword regularization). Used by T5, mBART, ALBERT, XLNet, Gemma.

WordPiece. Merge pairs that maximize likelihood of the training corpus rather than raw frequency. Used by BERT, DistilBERT, ELECTRA.

SentencePiece vs tiktoken. SentencePiece is the library that trains vocabularies (BPE or Unigram) directly on raw Unicode text, encoding whitespace as ▁. tiktoken is OpenAI's fast encoder against pre-built vocabularies; it does not train.

Rule of thumb:

  • Training a new vocabulary: SentencePiece (multilingual, no pre-tokenization) or HF Tokenizers.
  • Fast inference against GPT vocab: tiktoken (cl100k_base, o200k_base).
  • Both: HF Tokenizers — one library, training + serving.

The same idea as a formula

BPE (as in Lesson 5.4) merges the pair with the highest count, argmax(a,b)​c(a,b). It is greedy compression: each merge removes as many symbols from the corpus as possible.

WordPiece merges the pair that most increases the likelihood of the corpus under a unigram model of the current symbols. Because adding the symbol ab changes the likelihood by roughly logp(a)p(b)p(ab)​ per occurrence, the merge score is

score(a,b)=c(a)c(b)c(a,b)​

It prefers pairs whose parts rarely occur apart, not merely frequent pairs: "q" + "u" scores high because "q" almost never appears without "u".

Unigram (Kudo, 2018) gives each piece x a probability p(x) and a segmentation x=(x1​,…,xm​) of a word the probability P(x)=∏i​p(xi​). The tokenizer outputs the most probable segmentation, found by Viterbi over the positions of the word. Training alternates: 1. E-step: given p, compute the expected count of each piece over all segmentations of the corpus. 2. M-step: set p(x)∝ expected count. 3. Prune: remove the 10-20% of pieces whose removal lowers the corpus log-likelihood ∑w​log∑x∈S(w)​P(x) the least, until the target size is reached.

Subword regularisation. Instead of always the best segmentation, sample one with probability ∝P(x)α during training, so the model sees "unbelievable" split several ways and becomes robust to segmentation; α near 0 is close to uniform, large α is close to always-best.

Worked example
Three algorithms, one tiny corpus

Corpus symbol counts after splitting into characters: "h" 10, "u" 12, "g" 6, "q" 2, and pair counts "h u" 8, "u g" 6, "q u" 2.

  • BPE picks the most frequent pair: "h u" (8).
  • WordPiece scores: "h u" =8/(10×12)=0.067; "u g" =6/(12×6)=0.083; "q u" =2/(2×12)=0.083. It picks "u g" or "q u", pairs whose parts rarely occur apart ("g" never appears without a preceding "u" here, nor "q" without a following "u").
  • Unigram, segmenting "hug" with p(hu)=0.05, p(ug)=0.04, p(h)=0.1, p(g)=0.05, p(hug)=0.001: candidates "hu g" =0.05×0.05=0.0025, "h ug" =0.1×0.04=0.004, "hug" =0.001. Viterbi returns "h ug"; sampling with α=1 would return "hu g" about 0.0025/0.0075=33% of the time.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Tokenizers just split on spaces and punctuation."
    ✓ Subword tokenizers split inside words using learned statistics; SentencePiece does not even assume spaces exist, which matters for Chinese, Japanese and Thai.
  • ✗ "The same text always gives the same tokens."
    ✓ Only for a fixed tokenizer, fixed normalisation and fixed whitespace handling. "hello" and " hello" differ, and Unigram can sample segmentations on purpose.
  • ✗ "Bigger vocabularies are always better."
    ✓ They shorten sequences but enlarge the embedding and output layers and leave rare tokens undertrained; the best size grows with model size.

Step 1 · BPE from scratch

See code/main.py. The loop:

def train_bpe(corpus, num_merges):
    vocab = {tuple(word) + ("</w>",): count for word, count in corpus.items()}
    merges = []
    for _ in range(num_merges):
        pairs = Counter()
        for symbols, freq in vocab.items():
            for a, b in zip(symbols, symbols[1:]):
                pairs[(a, b)] += freq
        if not pairs:
            break
        best = pairs.most_common(1)[0][0]
        merges.append(best)
        vocab = apply_merge(vocab, best)
    return merges

Three facts the algorithm encodes. </w> marks word end so "low" (suffix) and "lower" (prefix) stay distinct. Frequency weighting makes high-frequency pairs win early. The merge list is ordered — inference applies merges in training order.

Step 2 · encode with the learned merges

def encode_bpe(word, merges):
    symbols = list(word) + ["</w>"]
    for a, b in merges:
        i = 0
        while i < len(symbols) - 1:
            if symbols[i] == a and symbols[i + 1] == b:
                symbols = symbols[:i] + [a + b] + symbols[i + 2:]
            else:
                i += 1
    return symbols

Naive O(n⋅∣merges∣). Production implementations (tiktoken, HF Tokenizers) use merge-rank lookup with priority queues and run in near-linear time.

Step 3 · SentencePiece in practice

import sentencepiece as spm
import nltk

        model_prefix=f"my_tokenizer_{model_type}",
        vocab_size=4000,           # Emma is small: 8,000 pieces would mostly be whole words
        model_type=model_type,
        character_coverage=1.0,    # all characters kept; use 0.9995 for large multilingual corpora
        normalization_rule_name="nmt_nfkc",
        minloglevel=2,
    )

sp = spm.SentencePieceProcessor(model_file="my_tokenizer_bpe.model")
print(sp.encode("untokenizable", out_type=str))

Pitfalls that still ship in 2026

  • Tokenizer drift. Training on vocab A, deploying against vocab B. Token IDs differ; model outputs garbage. Check tokenizer.json hash in CI.
  • Whitespace ambiguity. BPE "hello" vs " hello" produce different tokens. Always specify add_special_tokens and add_prefix_space explicitly.
  • Multilingual undertraining. English-heavy corpora produce vocabularies that split non-Latin scripts into 5-10x more tokens. With GPT-2's tokenizer the same sentence costs several times more in Arabic or Hindi than in English, and even more for some Indic scripts (measured in Lesson 5.18). OpenAI's o200k_base (GPT-4o, 2024) cut the gap substantially compared with cl100k_base (GPT-3.5 and GPT-4); the experiment under "Use it" measures by how much.
  • Emoji splits. A single emoji can take 5 tokens. Checkpoint emoji handling when budgeting context.

The two algorithms make visibly different choices. BPE keeps frequent words whole ("happiness", "Highbury"), because merges build them up completely, and covers unseen words with arbitrary fragments: "untokenizable" becomes un | t | ok | en | i | z | able. Unigram, choosing the most probable combination of the pieces it kept, prefers reusable parts even for frequent words: happi | ness, High | bury, and un | to | ken | iz | able, which lines up with how a reader would split the word. Neither is wrong; they encode the ordinary sentence in a similar number of pieces (13 and 14). The sampling lines show Unigram's unique trick: at alpha 0.1, 30 samples of "unbelievably" give 28 different segmentations; at alpha 1.0 only 5, all close to the best one. Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Vocabulary sizes grew. Llama 3 (about 128k), GPT-4o (o200k_base, about 200k) and Gemma 3 (about 262k) use much larger vocabularies than GPT-2's 50k, mainly to encode non-English text and code in fewer tokens.
  • Training your own tokenizer. Hugging Face tokenizers (byte-level BPE, as in this lesson) and SentencePiece are the standard tools when a domain-specific model needs one; most teams fine-tune an existing model and keep its tokenizer.
  • Tokenizer quirks reach production. Rare, under-trained vocabulary entries ("glitch tokens") have made deployed models misbehave, one reason providers keep tokenizers fixed across a model family.

Sources: Hugging Face tokenizers, SentencePiece, tiktoken, Fishing for Magikarp: glitch tokens.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

The 2026 stack:

Situation Pick
Training a monolingual model from scratch HF Tokenizers (BPE)
Training a multilingual model SentencePiece (Unigram, character_coverage=0.9995)
Serving an OpenAI-compatible API tiktoken (o200k_base for GPT-4+)
Domain-specific vocab (code, math, protein) Train custom BPE on domain corpus, merge with base vocab
Edge inference, small model A small vocabulary (8k to 32k) of either type: the embedding and output layers shrink with it

Vocabulary size is a scaling decision, not a constant. Rough heuristic: 32k for <1B params, 50-100k for 1-10B, 200k+ for multilingual/frontier. Tao et al. (NeurIPS 2024) fitted scaling laws for vocabulary size and concluded that most LLMs use vocabularies smaller than compute-optimal, and that the optimal vocabulary grows with model size.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • BPE merges the most frequent pair; WordPiece merges the pair with the highest c(a,b)/(c(a)c(b)); Unigram prunes a large vocabulary and picks the most probable segmentation with Viterbi.
  • Byte-level vocabularies never produce [UNK]; SentencePiece trains directly on raw text with spaces as ▁.
  • Unigram can sample segmentations (subword regularisation) to make models robust.
  • The tokenizer must match the model exactly, including whitespace handling and normalisation.
  • Larger vocabularies shorten non-English text and grow with model size, at the cost of bigger embedding layers.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Only partly

"Woodhouse", which occurs hundreds of times, is a single piece in both. But they disagree elsewhere, even on the novel's own words: BPE keeps "Highbury" whole while Unigram splits it into High | bury, because Unigram's pruning keeps pieces that are useful across many words and its segmentation picks the most probable combination. For the unseen "untokenizable", BPE falls back to small fragments (un | t | ok | en | i | z | able) while Unigram finds un | to | ken | iz | able. Same data, same vocabulary size, different inventories and different splits.

SolutionB1 · Pieces never form

With reversed merges, a merge like low + er is tried before lo + w or e + r have been applied, so the symbols it needs do not exist yet and nothing happens; by the time the early merges run, the later ones have already been passed. The word stays split into small pieces, usually single characters plus a few short pairs. Each merge was learned on text that already contained all earlier merges, so encoding must replay them in exactly that order: that is why tokenizers store merges as a ranked list.

Questions from real AI engineering interviews that this lesson prepares you for. Answer out loud first, then open the worked answer.

Explain the mechanismWalk me through one training step of byte-pair encoding on a small corpus, and tell me what the trained artifact actually is.

Level 1 of 4 · about 6 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Start with every word split into base symbols, bytes or characters. Count every adjacent pair across the corpus, weighted by how often each word appears, merge the most frequent pair into one new symbol with the next free id, rewrite every occurrence, and repeat until the vocabulary reaches the target size. The artifact is the ordered list of merges; the vocabulary follows from it, and the same list applied in the same order is what encoding uses later.

A worked step.

Corpus: "lower" 5 times, "lowest" 2 times, "newest" 6 times.

l o w e r      (x5)
l o w e s t    (x2)
n e w e s t    (x6)

pair counts: (w,e): 13   (e,s): 8   (s,t): 8   (l,o): 7   (o,w): 7   (n,e): 6   (e,r): 5
merge (w,e) -> we

l o we r       (x5)
l o we s t     (x2)
n e we s t     (x6)

Recount, and the next step sees (we,s) and (s,t) tied at 8. The lesson's implementation breaks ties by sorting on the pair itself, so two runs over the same corpus produce the same table. After enough steps "west" exists as one symbol.

The three facts the loop encodes.

  1. Frequency weighting makes the pairs of common words win early.
  2. A pre-split on whitespace and punctuation keeps merges inside a word. A word-end marker also keeps "low" as a whole word distinct from "low" as the start of "lower".
  3. The merge list is ordered. Encoding applies merges in training order, so the order is part of the artifact.

Byte level.

With bytes as the base, the first 256 ids are the byte values and merge k gets id 256 plus k. The cost of one step is linear in the corpus as a list of symbol sequences, and the sequences shrink as merges land, so a million words to ten thousand ids runs in seconds.

What you ship.

The merge table and the special-token table. The vocabulary of byte strings can be rebuilt from them.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "BPE picks the pair that makes the most meaningful piece." It picks the most frequent pair; meaning is a side effect, and the likelihood-based variant is a different algorithm.
  • "The vocabulary is the artifact; the merge list can be discarded." Encoding needs the merges in order; the vocabulary alone does not say how to split new text.
  • "All merges are chosen at once from the initial pair counts." Each merge changes the counts, so the loop recounts after every step.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 1) Why weight pairs by word frequency instead of counting each distinct word once? Expected: the merges should serve the text the model will see, and common words appear many times.
  • (level 2) Why must a tie in pair counts be broken deterministically? Expected: two training runs on the same corpus must produce the same table, or the same text would get different ids.
  • (level 2) What happens to the encoded length of a sentence as the target vocabulary grows from 300 to 600? Expected: it falls, because more merges cover longer pieces; that curve is the compression curve.
  • (level 3) Why split the corpus into words before you count pairs? Expected: without it, merges cross word boundaries and the vocabulary fills with whole phrases that do not generalize.

Weigh a trade-offThree subword algorithms dominate: byte-pair encoding, WordPiece and Unigram. Contrast how each builds its vocabulary, tell me which one can give two different segmentations of the same word on purpose, and name the libraries you would reach for.

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Byte-pair encoding merges the most frequent adjacent pair, bottom up. WordPiece also merges bottom up but scores a pair by the count of the merged piece over the product of the counts of its parts, so it prefers pairs that co-occur more than chance, and it marks continuation pieces with a double-hash prefix. Unigram works top down: it starts from a large candidate set, assigns each piece a probability, and prunes the pieces whose removal hurts the corpus likelihood least, which is why it can also sample alternative segmentations for regularization.

The criteria side by side.

BPE merge criterion:        count(A, B)
WordPiece merge criterion:  count(AB) / (count(A) * count(B))
Unigram:                    prune the piece whose removal least lowers the corpus log-likelihood

The first asks which pair appears most. The second asks which pair appears together more often than you would expect from its parts. The third asks which pieces the corpus can best do without.

Who uses what.

Algorithm Direction Marker Used by
Byte-pair encoding merge up leading space kept on word-initial pieces most decoder families, with vocabularies of 50,257, 128,256 and more
WordPiece merge up by likelihood ratio ## for continuation pieces the classic encoder with 30,522 tokens
Unigram prune down ▁ for a space when trained without pre-tokenization an encoder-decoder family with 32,000 tokens

Sampling segmentations.

A Unigram model is probabilistic at inference. It can return the most likely segmentation or sample another one, which gives subword regularization during training: the same word arrives in several forms and the model learns pieces more robustly. The merge-based algorithms are deterministic.

Libraries.

One library trains vocabularies for either the merge or the prune algorithm directly on raw text, encoding the space as a visible symbol and needing no pre-tokenization, which is why it is the default for multilingual work. A second, Rust-backed encoder is fast and matches the vocabularies of a widely used API family, but it only encodes. A third library trains and serves both algorithms in one place.

Picking.

Training a monolingual model: byte-pair encoding. Training multilingual: Unigram with a character coverage near 0.9995. Serving against an existing API vocabulary: the encoder-only library. Edge inference with a small model: Unigram with a smaller vocabulary.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "WordPiece and byte-pair encoding give the same vocabulary on the same corpus." The criteria differ, so the merges differ; the likelihood ratio favors surprising pairs over merely frequent ones.
  • "Unigram is deterministic like the merge algorithms." It is probabilistic and can sample segmentations on purpose.
  • "The library that encodes the space as a symbol is itself an algorithm." It is a library that trains either the merge or the prune algorithm on raw text.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) What is subword regularization? Expected: training on sampled segmentations from a Unigram model so the same word appears in several forms.
  • (level 2) What does the visible-space symbol buy? Expected: the space becomes an ordinary symbol, so the tokenizer needs no pre-tokenization and works for scripts without spaces.
  • (level 3) Which algorithm and setting for a multilingual vocabulary? Expected: Unigram trained on raw text with character coverage near 0.9995, so rare scripts are kept rather than mapped to unknown.
  • (level 3) Why can an encode-only library not train your tokenizer? Expected: it ships pre-built vocabularies and a fast encoder; the training step lives in the other two libraries.

Debug a failureA fine-tuned chat model answers well in the training notebook. The deployed service, with identical weights, returns rambling text that runs until the token limit. Where do you look first, and what do you expect to find?

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. At the flat token sequence the model actually receives, not at the list of messages. A chat model was trained on one exact layout of role markers, special tokens and newlines; a missing newline, a swapped marker or an extra space puts the input outside the training distribution, and the end-of-turn token stops being predicted. Print the token ids of the same request from both paths and diff them, and check that the special tokens arrived as their reserved ids and not as the bytes that spell them.

The model never sees the JSON.

The service holds a list of role and content pairs. The model sees one flat sequence built by the chat template, which inserts markers such as a begin-of-text token, a header for each role and an end-of-turn token after each message, with specific newlines between them. Every model family lays this out differently. The template is part of the model, in the same way the vocabulary is.

The three ways it breaks.

  1. A different template. The service used a generic layout or an older version. Each message now sits in a frame the model never saw, and the end-of-turn marker it learned is absent or misplaced, so it never predicts the stop token.
  2. Special tokens spelled out. Special tokens must be matched exactly before the merge loop and mapped to fixed ids. If the service built the prompt as plain text and encoded it with the allow flag off, the marker string became ordinary bytes and pieces. The model sees text that looks like a marker and is not one.
  3. Stop token mismatch. The service stops on the generic end-of-sequence id while the model emits its end-of-turn id. Generation continues past the real end.

The check that settles it.

ids_notebook = tokenizer(chat_template(messages))
ids_service  = ids the service sent to the engine
diff the two lists; the first difference is the bug

Then decode each id back to its string and look for a marker that decoded as several pieces instead of one reserved id. If the two lists match, the input is fine and the stop token is next: compare the stop ids in the service config with the end-of-turn id that the template puts after each message.

The fix.

Use the model's own template function, pass special tokens through the reserved-id path, and configure the stop token to the id the template ends a turn with. Freeze the tokenizer file and the template together with the weights.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The sampling temperature is too high in the service." Temperature changes which token is picked, not whether the stop token is ever predicted; the ids differ before sampling.
  • "The fine-tune overfit." The same weights behave in the notebook; the difference is in the input layout.
  • "Trim the output on the client." That hides the symptom and the model still wastes compute on text it should not generate.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) What does the end-of-turn token do at inference? Expected: it is the model's signal that the answer is complete; the server must stop on that id, or generation runs to the limit.
  • (level 3) A user types the literal string of a special token into the chat box. What should happen? Expected: with the allow flag off it becomes ordinary bytes, which is the safe default for user text; with it on, the user could inject a control marker.
  • (level 3) How do you test a template implementation? Expected: build the sequence for a fixed message list and compare it id by id with the output of the model's own template code.
  • (level 4) The template is correct and the ids still differ between notebook and service. What else? Expected: a different tokenizer file version, a different normalization step, or a different setting for adding special tokens at the start; hash the tokenizer file and compare the flags.

Defend a designYou are starting a 7B model that must serve many languages and also be strong at code. Design its tokenizer: the algorithm, the library, the vocabulary size, the coverage settings, and the validation plan you would gate the training run on.

Level 4 of 4 · about 15 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Train a byte-fallback subword vocabulary on a corpus that mirrors the target mix of languages and code, with a pre-tokenizer that keeps leading spaces and separates digits and punctuation so that code tokenizes cleanly. For a model in the 1 to 10B range the heuristic is 50 to 100k tokens, and the multilingual requirement pushes to the top of that range or beyond it; set the character coverage near 0.9995 so that rare scripts are kept rather than mapped to unknown. Gate on tokens per word per language, the unknown rate, the compression ratio against a reference encoder and a byte-exact round trip, and freeze the tokenizer file hash in CI before the first training step.

Algorithm and library.

The lesson's table points to the prune-based algorithm trained on raw text for multilingual work, because it needs no language-specific pre-tokenization and handles scripts without spaces, and to the merge-based algorithm for monolingual corpora. For a mixed requirement either works if the corpus is right; the corpus mix decides more than the algorithm. Use a library that trains, not one that only encodes against someone else's vocabulary. Keep byte fallback on in either case so that no input is ever unknown.

Vocabulary size.

Model scale Heuristic
under 1B about 32k
1 to 10B 50 to 100k
multilingual or frontier 200k and above

A 7B model with many scripts sits at the top of the middle row. Each added row is an embedding to learn, and the embedding matrix at 128,000 rows by 4,096 dimensions is about 524M parameters, so the size is a budget decision and not a constant. The payoff is fertility: an English-heavy vocabulary gives other scripts 2 to 3 tokens per word or worse, and non-Latin text can cost 5 to 10 times more tokens; the larger multilingual vocabulary is what repairs that.

Coverage and code.

  • Character coverage near 0.9995 for a mixed corpus; refuse anything under 0.995 when rare scripts are present.
  • Byte fallback for everything the coverage still misses.
  • A pre-tokenizer that keeps the leading space on words, splits digit runs and punctuation, and handles whitespace runs, so that indentation and operators in code become stable pieces. Chinese characters are 3 bytes each and need merges of their own, so the corpus must contain enough of them.
  • Reserve the special tokens and the chat markers at the start, with fixed ids that no merge can produce.

The corpus.

Sample the training text for the tokenizer in the proportions you want the model to be good at. A domain vocabulary can also be trained on the code corpus and merged with the base vocabulary. A tokenizer trained on an English-heavy sample will under-serve every other script for the life of the model.

The validation plan.

  1. Tokens per word, per language and for code, on held-out text; this is the fertility table you publish.
  2. Unknown rate, which must be zero with byte fallback.
  3. Compression ratio against a reference encoder on the same held-out text.
  4. Round trip, encode then decode, on all of it, including emoji and literal special-token strings. Byte-exact when the tokenizer has no normalizer; with a normalizer such as NFKC, compare against the normalized text.
  5. A frozen tokenizer file hash and golden token ids in CI, so that nothing drifts between training and serving.

Flag a monolingual vocabulary under 16k as under-specified, and refuse to ship a vocabulary without the hash check.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Pick 32k because earlier models used it." That size served monolingual models under 1B; a 7B multilingual coding model pays the tax in every language.
  • "Set character coverage to 1.0 to be safe." Full coverage spends vocabulary on characters seen once; near 0.9995 with byte fallback keeps rare scripts without wasting rows.
  • "Train on English now and add the other languages later." The vocabulary cannot change after training without a retrain; the languages must be in the tokenizer corpus from the start.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) Why does the corpus mix matter more than the algorithm choice? Expected: the merges or the pruned pieces follow the frequencies in the training text, so a script that is rare in the sample gets few pieces whatever the algorithm.
  • (level 4) What does whitespace handling do to code? Expected: indentation is runs of spaces or tabs; a pre-tokenizer that keeps whitespace runs as their own chunks lets them become stable tokens instead of being glued to the next word.
  • (level 4) The team wants to change the vocabulary after six months of training. What do you tell them? Expected: the embeddings and every weight were trained against the current ids; a change means a retrain, which is why the tokenizer decision is gated before the first step.
  • (level 4) How do you report the tokenizer with the model? Expected: the fertility table per language, the size and coverage of the vocabulary, the special-token list and the file hash.

Explain the mechanismAt encode time a BPE tokenizer holds the complete merge table. Why does it have to replay the merges in the order they were learned, and what goes wrong when two merges could fire at the same spot?

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Encoding must reproduce what training did to the same text, and training saw the merges one at a time in that order. If merge one created "th" and merge five created "the" from "th" plus "e", then "the" can only form after "th" exists. When two merges could apply at one position, the one with the lower rank, learned earlier, wins; change that rule and the same text yields different ids, so the model sees a distribution of ids it was never trained on.

The naive encoder.

def encode_bpe(word, merges):
    symbols = list(word) + ["</w>"]
    for a, b in merges:
        i = 0
        while i < len(symbols) - 1:
            if symbols[i] == a and symbols[i + 1] == b:
                symbols = symbols[:i] + [a + b] + symbols[i + 2:]
            else:
                i += 1
    return symbols

It walks the whole table for every word, so the cost is the word length times the number of merges. Correct for a merge table trained on characters, and slow.

The rank-based encoder.

The capstone version starts from the byte split and repeats: find the applicable merge with the lowest rank anywhere in the current sequence, apply it, and scan again, until no merge in the table applies. Production encoders keep the same semantics with a rank lookup and a priority queue of candidate pairs, which runs in close to linear time.

Why the rule is the contract.

The training loop and the encoder share the merge table, and that sharing is the whole contract. Training merged the most frequent pair first, so an input that contains both halves of an early merge was always merged that way in the training data. An encoder that prefers a later merge, or the longest piece, produces a segmentation the model never saw. Nothing crashes; the ids are simply different.

What special tokens do here.

Reserved strings such as the end-of-text marker are matched exactly before the merge loop runs and mapped to fixed ids. They never take part in a merge, so no table order can split or join them.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Greedily take the longest token in the vocabulary that matches." That is a different algorithm and gives a different segmentation from the training run.
  • "Order does not matter because merges commute." A later merge depends on the symbols that earlier merges created, so they do not commute.
  • "Sort the merges by token length." Length is not rank; a long piece may have been learned early and a short one late.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why is the naive loop slow? Expected: it scans the full merge table for every word, so the cost grows with the table size times the word length.
  • (level 3) How do production encoders get close to linear time? Expected: a rank lookup for each adjacent pair and a priority queue that always surfaces the lowest-ranked applicable merge.
  • (level 3) Two tokenizers were trained on different corpora to the same vocabulary size. Do they give the same ids? Expected: no; different corpora give different merge tables and different ranks.
  • (level 3) What test pins the order? Expected: encode a held-out word and compare with recorded ids; the capstone's tests also pin the round trip and the special-token reservation.

Where it is taught. The tokenizers lesson explains the ordered table, the BPE capstone implements the rank-based encoder, and the subword lesson gives the naive loop and its cost.

Explain the mechanismA customer in Korea reports that the same prompt uses up far more of the context window than the English version, and the bill is higher too. Why does this happen, and what does a model builder do about it?

Level 1 of 4 · about 6 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. The vocabulary was trained on English-heavy text, so its merges cover English and other scripts fall back to short pieces or raw bytes. Korean averages 2 to 3 tokens per word on an older vocabulary, and non-Latin text can cost 5 to 10 times more tokens than English for the same meaning; a Chinese character is 3 bytes in UTF-8, so without merges each character is several tokens. The builder's fix is a larger vocabulary trained on multilingual data, which is why one model family grew its vocabulary from 32K to 128K.

Where the tokens go.

A byte-level tokenizer never fails, but it only compresses what it has merges for. Each Chinese character is 3 bytes, Japanese characters are 3 to 4 bytes, an emoji is 4 bytes and can take 5 tokens. If the training corpus held little of a script, the merge table has few pieces for it, and the text stays close to one token per byte.

What the customer feels.

  • The context window is a token budget, so the same document fits less often.
  • Pricing is per token, so the same request costs more.
  • Latency is per generated token, so answers in that script are slower.

The lesson's phrase is that a user of such a script effectively has a context window half the size of an English user's.

What the builder does.

  1. Train the vocabulary on a corpus that mirrors the target language mix.
  2. Grow the vocabulary so that more merges serve non-Latin scripts; the move from 32K to 128K in one family was made mainly for this.
  3. Train with a library that works on raw text without pre-tokenization, so scripts without spaces segment well.
  4. Measure fertility per language and report it with the model.

How to measure it.

Take the same 10 meaning-aligned sentences in English, Spanish, Chinese, Korean and Arabic, from one parallel corpus, tokenize each with the candidate vocabulary, and compare the average tokens per character. The ratios are the tax; with sentences that do not match, report them as a spot check only.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Korean is harder for the model to understand." The model sees ids; the extra cost comes from the tokenizer's merges, not from the language.
  • "Use a character-level tokenizer for Korean." Characters make every sequence longer; the fix is merges that cover the script.
  • "The model has a smaller context window for Korean." The window is the same number of tokens; the tokenizer spends more of them per word.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 1) Define fertility in one sentence. Expected: the average number of tokens the tokenizer produces per word, where higher means the model works harder for the same text.
  • (level 2) How do you measure the tax for a new vocabulary? Expected: tokenize matched sentences in several languages and compare tokens per character or per word.
  • (level 2) Why does a larger vocabulary help scripts other than English? Expected: more merges can be spent on their byte patterns, so each character or word needs fewer tokens.
  • (level 3) What does the tax do to a product's cost model? Expected: per-token pricing and per-token latency scale with the token count, so the same feature costs more and runs slower in affected languages, and the context budget shrinks.

Explain the mechanismBefore any merge runs, a production tokenizer cuts the text into chunks with a regular expression. Show me what the classic pattern does to Hello, world! Don't stop. and explain why the cut exists at all, given that the merges could run on the raw stream.

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. The pattern cuts the sentence into eight chunks, Hello, ,, world, !, Don, 't, stop and .: each word after the first keeps the space in front of it, contractions split at the apostrophe, and punctuation stands alone. The merges then run inside each chunk and can never join symbols across a chunk boundary. Without the cut, training learns merges that span words, the vocabulary fills with whole phrases, and the pieces generalize worse to new text.

The pattern.

'(?:[sdmt]|ll|ve|re)| ?\p{L}+| ?\p{N}+| ?[^\s\p{L}\p{N}]+|\s+(?!\S)|\s+

In order: common contraction endings, a word with an optional leading space, a run of digits with an optional leading space, a run of punctuation, and whitespace. The \p{L} and \p{N} classes need the third-party regex module; the standard library falls back to ASCII classes, which is wrong for multilingual text.

Why the leading space travels with the word.

hello and hello are different tokens. Keeping the space on the word means a word-initial piece and a word-internal piece stay distinct, and the decoder can rebuild the spacing from the tokens alone. The same reason forces every caller to be explicit about whether a prefix space is added at the start of a prompt.

Why cut at all.

The merge loop is greedy on frequency. In a raw stream the pair "e" followed by a space followed by "t" is frequent, so the loop would happily learn a piece that ends one word and starts the next. Such pieces do not transfer: the next text has different word pairs. Cutting first keeps every merge inside a word, which is where the regularities that generalize live.

The other design.

A tokenizer that works on raw text treats the space as an ordinary symbol and lets the merge or prune algorithm find the boundaries. It needs no language rules and handles scripts without spaces, and it allows cross-word pieces, which can compress a little better and read a little worse.

The capstone's version.

A small split on whitespace and punctuation. It is enough to keep merges inside words for a small training set, and the rest of the pipeline stays the same.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Pre-tokenization is just splitting on spaces." The pattern also handles contractions, digits, punctuation and runs of whitespace, and it keeps the space attached to the word.
  • "The cut is cosmetic; the merges would find the same pieces." Without the cut the merges cross word boundaries and the vocabulary fills with phrases.
  • "Attach punctuation to the word it follows." Then world! and world are different pieces, and the vocabulary pays for every punctuation variant.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why does a prompt that starts without a space tokenize differently from one that starts with a space? Expected: the space is part of the word-initial token, so the first word maps to a different id; set the prefix-space behavior explicitly.
  • (level 3) Why does the regex module matter for this pattern? Expected: the Unicode letter and number classes cover scripts beyond ASCII, which the standard library's engine lacks.
  • (level 3) What is the trade in skipping the cut and running on raw text? Expected: no language rules and good behavior for scripts without spaces, against cross-word pieces that compress slightly better and are less interpretable.
  • (level 3) A tokenizer file is unchanged but ids differ between two machines. What in this stage can cause it? Expected: one machine fell back to the ASCII pattern because the regex module was missing, so the chunks differ.

Where it is taught. The tokenizer build lesson gives the pattern and its output, the BPE capstone explains why merges stay inside words, and the subword lesson lists the whitespace pitfall.

Debug a failureAfter a routine dependency upgrade, an evaluation score for a deployed model fell sharply. No model weights changed. Give me a diagnosis plan that starts at the tokenizer, and the control you add so it cannot happen again.

Level 3 of 4 · about 10 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. A model trained with one vocabulary and served with another gets different ids for the same text and produces garbage, so first hash the tokenizer file in both environments and compare. If the hashes match, check the settings that change ids without changing the vocabulary: whether special tokens are added, whether a prefix space is added, and the normalization step, because a leading space or a ligature that normalizes differently moves whole tokens. Then freeze the tokenizer hash in CI and keep golden token ids for a fixed corpus so any change fails a test before it ships.

Step one: the file.

The merge table and the vocabulary are the tokenizer. A library upgrade can pull a different file, or resolve a model name to a newer revision. Compare the hash of the tokenizer file in the evaluation environment with the one the model was trained with.

Step two: the settings around the file.

Setting What changes Symptom
Add special tokens a begin-of-text id appears or disappears at the start every position shifts by one
Add prefix space the first word gets a word-initial id or a word-internal id the first token of every prompt differs
Normalization a ligature such as the two-letter "fi" becomes two characters, or a fullwidth letter becomes ASCII the same word gets different pieces in the two environments
Pre-tokenizer engine the Unicode-aware pattern falls back to ASCII classes non-Latin text splits differently

Each of these is a default that a library version can change. None of them touches the vocabulary file, so the hash alone does not catch them.

Step three: prove it.

Take a fixed sample of prompts from the evaluation. Encode them in both environments and diff the id lists. The first position that differs tells you which setting moved. Decode the differing ids to see the pieces.

The controls.

  1. Pin the tokenizer file by hash in the deployment and fail the build when it changes.
  2. Store golden token ids for a fixed corpus that covers English, non-Latin scripts, code, emoji and a prompt with a leading space; the test encodes the corpus and compares.
  3. Assert a round trip, encode then decode, on the same corpus. Compare against the text that the configured normalizer produces, and require byte-exact equality for text that the normalizer leaves unchanged.
  4. Record the tokenizer settings next to the weights, so the serving config cannot drift from the training config.

The validation plan from the lesson.

Average tokens per word on a held-out set, the unknown rate, the compression ratio and the round-trip equality. Those four numbers, tracked over time, show drift before an evaluation does.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The weights did not change, so the model is the same." The model is the weights and the mapping from text to ids; change the mapping and the model sees different inputs.
  • "Retrain the model on the new tokenizer." A multi-week fix for a configuration bug; restore the training-time tokenizer and settings.
  • "Lowercase everything so the normalization does not matter." That changes the ids again and throws away information the model was trained with.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) How does a prefix space change the ids? Expected: the space is part of the word-initial token, so with it the first word gets one id and without it another, and the shift can continue through the merges.
  • (level 3) What does the compatibility normalization do, and when is it harmful? Expected: it maps compatibility characters to their plain forms, such as a ligature to two letters or a fullwidth letter to ASCII; it is lossy, so code or text that depends on the exact form changes meaning.
  • (level 4) The hashes match and the ids still differ on non-Latin text only. What moved? Expected: the pre-tokenizer engine; the environment without the Unicode-aware module fell back to the ASCII pattern.
  • (level 4) Design the CI gate in two sentences. Expected: a test that encodes a fixed multilingual corpus and compares against stored golden ids and a stored tokenizer hash; any difference fails the build and prints the first differing position.

Estimate itA team wants to grow the vocabulary of a new model from 32K to 128K tokens. The embeddings have 4,096 dimensions. Put numbers on what the change costs and on what it buys.

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. The embedding matrix goes from 32,000 by 4,096, about 131 million parameters, to 128,000 by 4,096, about 524 million, so about 400M more parameters from the tokenizer alone, and an untied output head costs the same again. In return the text compresses: an English paragraph of about 100 tokens at 32K takes about 70 tokens at 128K. Generating that same paragraph then needs about 30 percent fewer forward passes, and non-English scripts gain the most. The lesson's rule of thumb is 32k under 1B parameters, 50 to 100k from 1 to 10B, and 200k and above for multilingual or frontier models.

The cost side.

32,000  x 4,096 = about 131M parameters
128,000 x 4,096 = about 524M parameters
difference      = about 400M

Each row is an embedding the model has to learn, and rare rows receive few gradient updates. A small model with a huge vocabulary spends a large share of its parameters in a lookup table.

The benefit side.

Vocabulary Tokens per English word
30,522 about 1.4
50,257 about 1.3
100,256 about 1.2
128,256 about 1.1
200,019 about 1.0

Fewer tokens per word means shorter sequences, so each answer needs fewer forward passes and each context window holds more text. For a model that serves millions of requests, output of that kind needs about 30 percent fewer forward passes.

Who gains the most.

English already compresses well at 32K. Scripts that an English-heavy vocabulary splits into bytes gain far more, which is why one model family quadrupled its vocabulary from 32K to 128K.

The decision.

Vocabulary size scales with the model and the language mix. Under 1B parameters, the embedding would dominate the model, so stay near 32k. Between 1 and 10B, 50 to 100k. Multilingual at any scale pushes toward 200k and beyond. Validate the choice with tokens per word on a held-out set, the unknown rate, the compression ratio and a byte-exact round trip.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "A bigger vocabulary is always better." Under 1B parameters the extra rows dominate the model and rare rows are undertrained.
  • "The embedding is a rounding error next to the model." At 128K by 4,096 it is about 524M parameters.
  • "Vocabulary size does not affect the cost of a request." Tokens per word set the sequence length, and the sequence length sets the forward passes and the context budget.

  • (level 4) How do you validate a vocabulary size before training? Expected: tokens per word per language on held-out text, the unknown rate, the compression ratio against a reference encoder, and a byte-exact round trip.

Explain the mechanismWhy does every current language model read subword tokens instead of whole words or single characters, and why do most of them build the vocabulary on top of raw bytes?

Level 1 of 4 · about 6 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. A word vocabulary cannot hold every word, so each new word becomes an unknown token and the model loses the signal. Characters never fail, but they make sequences several times longer and spend attention on spelling. Subwords keep common words whole and split rare words into meaningful pieces, and starting from the 256 byte values means any input in any script encodes with zero unknown tokens.

The three options.

Level Vocabulary Failure
Word huge, and still incomplete unknown token for anything unseen; English alone has over a million word forms
Character a few hundred a 10-word sentence becomes about 50 tokens; the model relearns that t, h, e spell a word
Subword 30K to 128K none of the above; rare words split into pieces

A rare word such as "unhappiness" is 11 character tokens or 3 subword pieces: un, happi, ness. The pieces carry meaning the model can reuse.

Why bytes at the bottom.

A byte-level vocabulary treats each of the 256 byte values as a base token and learns merges on top. Any string in any encoding is a byte sequence, so nothing is ever unknown, and no language needs special handling. A well-known 50,257-token vocabulary is exactly 256 bytes plus 50,000 merges plus one special token.

Why it is architecture, not preprocessing.

The tokenizer fixes what the model sees for its whole life. A vocabulary that splits a common word into four pieces wastes context and compute on every request, and the choice cannot be undone after training. Pricing is per token and latency is per token, so the tokenizer sits in the cost model too.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Characters are the safest choice because nothing is ever unknown." Bytes give the same guarantee, and characters make every sequence several times longer.
  • "A bigger word list solves the unknown problem." New words, misspellings, code and other languages keep appearing; no word list is complete.
  • "A token is a word." A token can be a byte, a piece of a word, a word, or a short phrase, depending on the merges.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 1) What does fertility measure? Expected: the average number of tokens per word; 1.0 is one token per word and higher means the model works harder for the same text.
  • (level 2) Why does the unknown token disappear with byte-level vocabularies? Expected: every byte is itself a token, so any input decomposes into known tokens before any merge applies.
  • (level 2) What does a tiny vocabulary cost at inference? Expected: longer sequences, so more forward passes per answer and less text per context window.
  • (level 3) Why can you not fix a bad tokenizer after training? Expected: the embedding rows and every weight were trained against those ids; new ids have no learned meaning and old text would map differently.
