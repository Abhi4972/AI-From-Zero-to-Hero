In plain words first
The idea in one line

Every image model, text model, video model, and 3D model fits in one of five buckets. Pick the wrong bucket and you will fight the math for weeks. Pick the right one and the field's last twelve years of progress stacks cleanly in your head.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
Generative model "It makes new stuff" Learns a sampler for $p_{\text{data}}(x)$, optionally exposes $\log p(x)$.
Explicit density "You can evaluate it" Model provides a closed-form or tractable $\log p(x)$.
Implicit density "GAN-style" Only a sampler — no way to evaluate $p(x)$ of a given point.
ELBO "Evidence lower bound" A tractable lower bound on $\log p(x)$; VAEs and diffusion optimize it.
Score "Gradient of log-density" $\nabla_x \log p(x)$; diffusion and SDE models learn this field.
Manifold hypothesis "Data lives on a surface" High-dim data concentrates on a low-dim manifold; why dimensionality reduction works.
Autoregressive "Predict the next piece" Factorize joint as product of conditionals.
Latent "Compressed code" Low-dim representation from which a decoder can reconstruct the input.

The problem

In plain words first

A generative model learns from examples (photos, sentences, recordings) and then produces new ones that could have come from the same collection. There are only a handful of ways to do it. Some models can tell you how likely any given example is and build their output piece by piece (language models). Some learn a compressed code and decode from it (autoencoders). Some learn by a contest between a generator and a judge and produce an output in one shot (GANs). Some start from noise and remove it step by step (diffusion and flow models). Knowing which family a model belongs to tells you how it is trained, how it fails, and how expensive it is to run. This lesson runs the lesson's one-dimensional example of "a model that gives probabilities" against "a model that only gives samples", then times three real models from three families, GPT-2, a diffusion model and BigGAN, making text and images on this page's CPU. Nothing is trained.

A generative model does one job: given training samples drawn from some unknown distribution pdata​(x), output new samples that look like they came from the same distribution. Faces, sentences, MIDI files, protein structures — all the same problem if you squint.

The rub is that pdata​ lives in a space with millions of dimensions (a 512x512 RGB image is ~786k dimensions), the samples sit on a thin manifold inside that space, and you only have maybe 10M examples. Brute-forcing the density is hopeless. Every generative model is a compromise that trades one hard problem for a slightly less hard one.

Five families have survived the last twelve years. Knowing which compromise each family makes tells you why it wins on some tasks and collapses on others.

The idea

1. Explicit density, tractable. Write logp(x) as a sum you can actually evaluate. Autoregressive models (PixelCNN, WaveNet, GPT) factorize p(x)=∏i​p(xi​∣x<i​). Normalizing flows (RealNVP, Glow) build p(x) as an invertible transform of a simple base. Pro: exact likelihood, clean training loss. Con: autoregressive inference is sequential (slow for long sequences), flows need invertible architectures (architecturally restrictive).

2. Explicit density, approximate. Bound logp(x) from below (ELBO) and optimize the bound. VAEs (Kingma 2013) use an encoder-decoder with a variational posterior. Diffusion models (DDPM, Ho 2020) train a denoiser that implicitly optimizes a weighted ELBO. Diffusion is the dominant image, video, and 3D backbone in 2026.

3. Implicit density. Skip density entirely; learn a generator G(z) that produces samples and a discriminator D(x) that tells real from fake. GANs (Goodfellow 2014). Fast at inference (one forward pass) but notoriously unstable during training. StyleGAN 1/2/3 remain state of the art for fixed-domain photorealism (faces, bedrooms) even in 2026.

4. Score-based / continuous-time. Learn the gradient of the log-density ∇x​logp(x) (the score) directly. Song & Ermon (2019) trained score networks at many noise levels, and Song et al. (2021) unified this with diffusion as a stochastic differential equation. Flow matching (Lipman 2023) is the 2024-2026 hotness: simulation-free training, straighter paths, far fewer sampling steps than DDPM. Stable Diffusion 3, FLUX.1 and F5-TTS use flow matching or rectified flow.

5. Token-based autoregressive over discrete codes. Compress high-dim data with a VQ-VAE or residual quantizer into a short sequence of discrete tokens, then use a Transformer to model the token sequence. Parti, AudioLM, VALL-E and MusicGen use this. (Sora, by contrast, is a diffusion transformer over continuous latent patches.) This is bucket 1 plus a learned tokenizer.

The five-question triage

When a new generative model paper drops, answer these five questions before reading the method section.

  1. What is being modeled? Pixels, latents, discrete tokens, 3D Gaussians, meshes, waveforms?
  2. Is the density explicit or implicit? Do they write down logp(x)?
  3. Sampling: one-shot or iterative? Iterative means slower inference; one-shot usually means adversarial or distilled.
  4. Conditioning: unconditional, class, text, image, pose? This determines the loss and architecture scaffolding.
  5. Evaluation: FID, CLIP score, IS, human preference, task accuracy? Each has known failure modes (see Lesson 8.14).

You will re-answer these five for every lesson in this phase. By the end, they will be reflex.

The same idea as a formula

The common goal. Fit a model pθ​ to data from pdata​. Maximum likelihood, maxθ​Ex∼pdata​​[logpθ​(x)], is the same as minimising DKL​(pdata​∥pθ​), since the two differ by the data's own entropy.

How each family gets (or avoids) logpθ​(x):

  • Autoregressive: logpθ​(x)=∑i​logpθ​(xi​∣x<i​), exact, one factor per piece.
  • Normalising flow: with an invertible f mapping data to a simple z, the change of variables gives logpθ​(x)=logpz​(f(x))+log​det∂x∂f​​, exact.
  • VAE: a lower bound, logpθ​(x)≥Eq(z∣x)​[logpθ​(x∣z)]−DKL​(q(z∣x)∥p(z)) (Lesson 8.2).
  • Diffusion: a weighted sum of denoising errors that bounds the likelihood; in practice E∥ε−εθ​(xt​,t)∥2 (Lesson 8.6).
  • Score-based: learn sθ​(x)≈∇x​logp(x) and sample with Langevin steps x←x+ηsθ​(x)+2η​ξ.
  • GAN: no likelihood at all; a generator G and discriminator D play minG​maxD​E[logD(x)]+E[log(1−D(G(z)))] (Lesson 8.3).

Cost of one sample. Autoregressive: one network pass per piece. Diffusion and flow: one pass per sampling step, over the whole output. GAN, VAE decoder and distilled one-step models: one pass in total.

Worked example
One image, five ways, by hand

A 512×512 RGB image has 512×512×3=786,432 numbers.

  • Pixel autoregressive (PixelCNN-style): 786,432 sequential predictions, one per number; impractical at this size.
  • Tokens: a VQ tokenizer with 16× downsampling gives 32×32=1,024 tokens; an autoregressive transformer needs 1,024 sequential steps.
  • Latent diffusion: an 8× VAE gives a 64×64×4 latent (16,384 numbers); 30 denoising steps of a large network, all positions in parallel at each step.
  • Distilled flow model (FLUX.1-schnell style): the same latent in 1–4 steps.
  • GAN: one generator pass.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Diffusion models cannot give likelihoods."
    ✓ They optimise a bound on it and can evaluate likelihoods with extra computation; GANs are the family that has none.
  • ✗ "Language models and image generators are unrelated."
    ✓ An autoregressive transformer over image tokens is a language model over a different vocabulary; several image generators work exactly that way.
  • ✗ "One-shot generators are always worse than iterative ones."
    ✓ BigGAN and StyleGAN are excellent inside narrow domains; distilled diffusion models are one-shot too. Iteration buys flexibility and stable training, not quality by itself.

Build it

The code for this lesson is a lightweight visualization: fit a 1-D mixture-of-Gaussians from samples using three toy approaches (kernel density, discrete histogram, and a nearest-sample "GAN-ish" generator) so you can see the difference between explicit vs implicit density on a problem you can print on one screen.

Run code/main.py. It draws 2000 samples from a two-mode Gaussian mixture, then prints:

explicit density (histogram): p(x in [-0.5, 0.5]) ≈ 0.025
approximate density (KDE):     p(x in [-0.5, 0.5]) ≈ 0.033
implicit (nearest-sample gen): 10 new samples printed, no p(x)

Notice: the first two let you ask "how likely is this point?" The third cannot. This is the explicit vs implicit distinction that will matter for every future lesson.

Reading this output

The histogram and the kernel estimate can both answer "how likely is this point?": about 0.02–0.03 near x=0, the valley between the modes. Their probabilities for [−0.5,0.5], 0.025 and 0.033, bracket the true value for this mixture, about 0.029 (the expected output printed in the source, 0.38 and 0.41, was wrong and has been corrected). The implicit generator produces convincing new samples in both modes but cannot score any point, which is the position GANs are in. Its samples are training points plus a little noise: with less noise it would simply copy the training set, the memorisation risk every generative model must be checked for.

import time, torch, numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from huggingface_hub import hf_hub_download
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer
from diffusers import DDIMScheduler, UNet2DModel
from pytorch_pretrained_biggan import BigGAN, BigGANConfig, one_hot_from_int, truncated_noise_sample

torch.manual_seed(0)
rows = []
# 1. Autoregressive: GPT-2 writes tokens one forward pass at a time.
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("openai-community/gpt2")
gpt = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("openai-community/gpt2").eval()
ids = tok("The castle on the hill", return_tensors="pt").input_ids
for n in (1, 32):
    t = time.perf_counter()
    gpt.generate(ids, max_new_tokens=n, min_new_tokens=n, do_sample=False, pad_token_id=tok.eos_token_id)
    rows.append(("autoregressive", f"GPT-2, {n} token(s)", n, time.perf_counter() - t))

# 2. Diffusion: a DDPM U-Net trained on CIFAR-10 (32 x 32), sampled with 50 DDIM steps instead of 1,000.
unet = UNet2DModel.from_pretrained("google/ddpm-cifar10-32").eval()
sched = DDIMScheduler.from_pretrained("google/ddpm-cifar10-32"); sched.set_timesteps(50)
x = torch.randn(8, 3, 32, 32)
t = time.perf_counter()
with torch.no_grad():
    for step in sched.timesteps:
        x = sched.step(unet(x, step).sample, step, x).prev_sample
rows.append(("diffusion", "DDPM U-Net, 50 DDIM steps, 8 images", 50, time.perf_counter() - t))
cifar = ((x.clamp(-1, 1) + 1) / 2).permute(0, 2, 3, 1).numpy()

# 3. GAN: BigGAN-deep (ImageNet, 128 x 128) maps noise and a class to an image in one pass.
cfg = BigGANConfig.from_json_file(hf_hub_download("osanseviero/BigGAN-deep-128", "config.json"))
gan = BigGAN(cfg); gan.load_state_dict(torch.load(hf_hub_download("osanseviero/BigGAN-deep-128", "pytorch_model.bin"))); gan.eval()
classes = [207, 963, 980, 22]                                # golden retriever, pizza, volcano, bald eagle
z = torch.tensor(truncated_noise_sample(truncation=0.5, batch_size=4, seed=1))
t = time.perf_counter()
with torch.no_grad():
    imgs = gan(z, torch.tensor(one_hot_from_int(classes, batch_size=4)), 0.5)
rows.append(("GAN", "BigGAN-deep, 4 images", 1, time.perf_counter() - t))
big = ((imgs.clamp(-1, 1) + 1) / 2).permute(0, 2, 3, 1).numpy()

print(f"{'family':16s} {'model and job':40s} {'network passes':>15} {'seconds':>8}")
for fam, what, passes, sec in rows:
    print(f"{fam:16s} {what:40s} {passes:15d} {sec:8.2f}")
print(f"parameters: GPT-2 {sum(p.numel() for p in gpt.parameters()) / 1e6:.0f}M, "
      f"DDPM U-Net {sum(p.numel() for p in unet.parameters()) / 1e6:.0f}M, BigGAN-deep {sum(p.numel() for p in gan.parameters()) / 1e6:.0f}M")

fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 4, figsize=(9, 4.8))
for k in range(4):
    axes[0, k].imshow(cifar[k]); axes[1, k].imshow(big[k])
for a in axes.ravel(): a.axis("off")
axes[0, 0].set_title("DDPM (CIFAR-10, 32 px), 50 steps", loc="left", fontsize=9)
axes[1, 0].set_title("BigGAN-deep (128 px), 1 pass: retriever, pizza, volcano, eagle", loc="left", fontsize=9)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

Reading this output

The diffusion model is the slowest by far: more than a minute on this page's CPU for eight tiny 32×32 images, because each of the 50 steps runs the whole U-Net on every pixel of every image. BigGAN makes four 128×128 images, with sixteen times the pixels each, in under a second, in one pass: roughly a hundred times faster. GPT-2's cost grows with the length of its output: one token takes about a tenth of a second, 32 tokens about a second, one pass per token. The pictures show the other side of the trade: the CIFAR-10 diffusion model is a small 2020 research model and its samples are blurry at 32 pixels, while BigGAN's class-conditional samples (retriever, pizza, volcano, eagle) are sharp because a GAN is excellent within its training domain. Modern diffusion systems close the speed gap with latent spaces, fewer steps and distillation, not by giving up iteration.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Diffusion transformers with flow matching are the open-model standard for images and video: FLUX.2, Qwen-Image and Z-Image for images, Wan 2.x, HunyuanVideo and LTX-2 for video, run through Hugging Face diffusers and ComfyUI.
  • Hosted image and video models (OpenAI's GPT Image models, Google's Nano Banana image models and Veo 3.1, Midjourney V8, Runway, Kling, ByteDance's Seedance; in March 2026 OpenAI announced it would discontinue its Sora video app and API) add editing by instruction and generated sound; several are not pure diffusion models, and their architectures are only partly published.
  • One-step and few-step sampling (SDXL-Turbo, FLUX.1-schnell, Z-Image-Turbo, latent consistency models) is how interactive products keep latency low.

Sources: FLUX.2, diffusers, Wan 2.1, Veo, GPT Image 2, Sora shutdown (Axios), SDXL-Turbo.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Five families: autoregressive and flows (exact likelihood), VAEs and diffusion (bounds), GANs (no likelihood), score-based and flow matching (learn a field), token models (autoregressive over learned codes).
  • Ask the five triage questions of every new model: what is modelled, explicit or implicit, one-shot or iterative, what conditioning, what evaluation.
  • Cost per sample: passes per token for autoregressive, steps × full passes for diffusion, one pass for GANs and distilled models.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · The diffusion model, by far

The diffusion model, by a wide margin: more than a minute, against about a second for GPT-2's 32 tokens and under a second for BigGAN's four images, roughly a hundred times less. Diffusion pays one full network pass per step (50 here, 1,000 in the original DDPM sampler), for every image in the batch; GPT-2 pays one pass per token; BigGAN pays one pass in total. That is the "inference shape" of each family in the production note.

SolutionB1 · A copying machine

With zero noise the generator returns training points exactly. Its outputs have the right distribution by construction, so histograms, and even FID in an image model, look perfect, yet it has generated nothing new. For images, the check is a nearest-neighbour search: for each generated sample, find the closest training image (in pixel space or in a feature space such as DINOv2 or CLIP) and look at the closest pairs; near-duplicates reveal memorisation. Studies of large diffusion models have extracted near-copies of training images this way, which is why deduplicating training data matters.

Production note: five families, five inference shapes

Each family maps to a different inference-server cost curve. production-inference literature frames LLM inference as prefill + decode; the same decomposition applies here:

  • Autoregressive (bucket 1 and 5). Sequential decode dominates latency; KV-cache, continuous batching, and speculative decoding all apply directly.
  • VAE / diffusion / flow-matching (buckets 2 and 4). There is no decode in the LLM sense. Cost = num_steps × step_cost, and the step_cost is a transformer or U-Net forward at the full latent resolution. The production knobs are step count (DDIM / DPM-Solver / distillation), batch size, and precision (bf16 / fp8 / int4).
  • GAN (bucket 3). One forward pass. No schedule, no KV-cache. TTFT ≈ total latency. This is why StyleGAN still wins on narrow-domain UX.

When you see "faster than diffusion" in a paper abstract, translate it to "fewer steps × same step cost" or "same steps × cheaper step cost". Everything else is marketing.
