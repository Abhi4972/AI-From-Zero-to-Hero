The problem

In plain words first

This lesson is about the pieces a model reads. Word2Vec gave each whole word one vector, which leaves two gaps: it ignores the big table of "which words appear together" that you could simply count, and it has nothing at all for a word it never saw. Three fixes followed. GloVe counts how often every pair of words appears together and fits vectors to those counts. FastText builds a word's vector from its spelling pieces ("whe", "her", "ere"…), so a new word still gets a vector from pieces it shares with known words. BPE, the method behind GPT-style tokenizers, stops treating words as the unit at all: it learns a vocabulary of frequent chunks, so any text, in any language, splits into chunks it knows.

Word2Vec left three open questions.

First, there was a parallel line of research that factorized the co-occurrence matrix directly (LSA, HAL) rather than doing online skip-gram updates. Was Word2Vec's iterative approach fundamentally better, or was the difference an artifact of how the two methods handled counts? GloVe answered that: factorizing the counts with a carefully chosen loss matched Word2Vec on the benchmarks of 2014, and the counting pass over the corpus happens once rather than every epoch. Later controlled comparisons (Levy, Goldberg and Dagan, 2015) found the two roughly tied once their settings were matched, so neither approach is fundamentally better.

Second, neither method had a story for words it had never seen. Zoomer-approved, dogecoin, any proper noun coined last week, every inflected form of a rare root. FastText fixed this by embedding character n-grams: a word is the sum of its parts, including morphemes, so even out-of-vocabulary words get a sensible vector.

Third, once transformers arrived, the question shifted again. Word-level vocabularies cap out around a million entries; real language is more open than that. Byte-pair encoding (BPE) and its relatives solved this by learning a vocabulary of frequent subword units that covers everything. Practically every modern LLM reads text through a subword tokenizer.

This lesson walks all three, then explains which to reach for when.

The idea

GloVe (Global Vectors). Build the word-word co-occurrence matrix X where X[i][j] is how often word j appears in the context of word i. Train vectors such that v_i · v_j + b_i + b_j ≈ log(X[i][j]). Weight the loss so frequent pairs do not dominate. Done.

FastText. A word is the sum of its character n-grams plus the word itself. With 3-character pieces, where becomes <wh, whe, her, ere, re> plus the whole word <where>; real FastText uses every length from 3 to 6, which gives 14 pieces plus the whole word. The word vector is the sum of those component vectors. Train as Word2Vec. Benefit: unseen words (whereupon) compose from known n-grams.

BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding). Start with a vocabulary of individual bytes (or characters). Count every adjacent pair in the corpus. Merge the most frequent pair into a new token. Repeat for k iterations. Result: a vocabulary of the base symbols plus k merges (k + 256 tokens when you start from bytes) where frequent sequences (ing, tion, the) are single tokens and rare words are broken into familiar pieces. Every sentence tokenizes into something.

Worked example
One GloVe term by hand

Suppose "ice" and "solid" were counted together Xij​=10 times, with the defaults xmax​=100 and α=0.75.

  • Weight: f(10)=(10/100)0.75=0.178. A pair seen 100 times or more would get weight 1; a pair seen once gets 0.032.
  • Target: ln10=2.303.
  • If the current vectors give wi​⋅w~j​+bi​+b~j​=1.5, the error is 1.5−2.303=−0.803.
  • This pair adds f⋅error2=0.178×0.645=0.115 to the loss, and every one of its four pieces gets nudged by lr×0.178×(−0.803) times the matching gradient, pushing the dot product up towards 2.303.

Notice what is missing: a pair that was never seen has Xij​=0, ln0 is undefined, and the loss simply skips it. Keep that in mind for the first experiment under "Use it".

Check your mental model

    ✓ It shares vectors between words that share spelling pieces. Often that tracks meaning ("unfriendliness" and "friendliness"), sometimes it is just coincidence of letters.
  • ✗ "BPE tokens are morphemes like un-, -ness."
    ✓ They are whatever substrings are frequent in the training text. Under "Use it", BPE trained on Emma splits "happiness" into happ · in · ess.
  • ✗ "A tokenizer treats every language the same."
    ✓ A tokenizer trained mostly on English text needs several times more tokens for other scripts, and the model then pays for that in context length and cost.
The same idea as a formula
GloVe, derived from counts

Let Xik​ count how often word k appears near word i, and Pik​=Xik​/∑k​Xik​, the probability of seeing k near i. GloVe's starting observation is that ratios of these probabilities carry meaning. For i = ice and j = steam: Pik​/Pjk​ is large for k = solid, small for k = gas, and near 1 for k = water (related to both) or fashion (related to neither).

So ask for vectors whose dot products reproduce log co-occurrence, so that differences of vectors reproduce log ratios:

wi⊤​w~k​≈logPik​=logXik​−logXi​

The logXi​ term depends only on i, so it becomes a learned bias bi​; adding b~k​ keeps the formula symmetric. Then (wi​−wj​)⊤w~k​≈log(Pik​/Pjk​), the ratio we wanted. Fitting all observed pairs by weighted least squares gives the loss:

J=i,j:Xij​>0∑​f(Xij​)(wi⊤​w~j​+bi​+b~j​−logXij​)2,f(x)={(x/xmax​)α1​x<xmax​otherwise​

The sum runs only over pairs that occurred, since log0 is undefined; f stops very common pairs from dominating and very rare (noisy) pairs from counting much. The paper uses xmax​=100, α=3/4, and the final vector wi​+w~i​. Levy and Goldberg (2014) showed skip-gram with negative sampling implicitly factorises a closely related matrix (shifted pointwise mutual information), which is why the two methods end up so similar.

The same idea as a formula
FastText and BPE as formulas

FastText keeps Word2Vec's skip-gram objective and changes only how a word is scored against a context word c. With Gw​ the set of character n-grams of w (lengths 3 to 6, plus the whole word in angle brackets) and zg​ one vector per n-gram:

s(w,c)=g∈Gw​∑​zg⊤​vc​,loss=−logσ(s(w,c))−n∈negatives∑​logσ(−s(w,n))

A word's vector is ∑g∈Gw​​zg​, so any string, seen or not, has one. To bound memory, n-grams are hashed into a fixed number of buckets (2 million in the paper), so unrelated n-grams occasionally share a vector.

BPE is a greedy compression algorithm, not a learned model. Start from base symbols (256 bytes in byte-level BPE). Repeat k times: find the adjacent pair (a,b) with the highest count in the corpus, add the new symbol ab, and replace every occurrence. The vocabulary ends with 256+k symbols, and the ordered merge list is the tokenizer: encoding a new word replays the merges in learned order (rank). Each merge reduces the corpus length by the count of the merged pair, which is why the first merges compress most.

Build · GloVe: factorize the co-occurrence matrix

import numpy as np
from collections import Counter


def build_cooccurrence(docs, window=5):
    pair_counts = Counter()
    vocab = {}
    for doc in docs:
        for token in doc:
            if token not in vocab:
                vocab[token] = len(vocab)
    for doc in docs:
        indexed = [vocab[t] for t in doc]
        for i, center in enumerate(indexed):
            for j in range(max(0, i - window), min(len(indexed), i + window + 1)):
                if i != j:
                    distance = abs(i - j)
                    pair_counts[(center, indexed[j])] += 1.0 / distance
    return vocab, pair_counts


def glove_train(vocab, pair_counts, dim=16, epochs=100, lr=0.05, x_max=100, alpha=0.75, seed=0):
    n = len(vocab)
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    W = rng.normal(0, 0.1, size=(n, dim))
    W_tilde = rng.normal(0, 0.1, size=(n, dim))
    b = np.zeros(n)
    b_tilde = np.zeros(n)

    for epoch in range(epochs):
        for (i, j), x_ij in pair_counts.items():
            weight = (x_ij / x_max) ** alpha if x_ij < x_max else 1.0
            diff = W[i] @ W_tilde[j] + b[i] + b_tilde[j] - np.log(x_ij)
            coef = weight * diff

            grad_W_i = coef * W_tilde[j]
            grad_W_tilde_j = coef * W[i]
            W[i] -= lr * grad_W_i
            W_tilde[j] -= lr * grad_W_tilde_j
            b[i] -= lr * coef
            b_tilde[j] -= lr * coef

    return W + W_tilde

Two moving pieces worth naming. The weighting function f(x) = (x/x_max)^alpha downweights very frequent pairs (like (the, and)) so they do not dominate the loss. The final embedding is the sum of W (center) and W_tilde (context) tables. Summing both is a published trick that tends to outperform using just one.

Build · FastText: subword-aware embeddings

def char_ngrams(word, n_min=3, n_max=6):
    wrapped = f"<{word}>"
    grams = {wrapped}
    for n in range(n_min, n_max + 1):
        for i in range(len(wrapped) - n + 1):
            grams.add(wrapped[i:i + n])
    return grams

Each word is represented by its set of n-grams (typically 3 to 6 characters). The word embedding is the sum of its n-gram embeddings. For skip-gram training, plug this in where Word2Vec used a single vector.

def fasttext_vector(word, ngram_table):
    grams = char_ngrams(word)
    vecs = [ngram_table[g] for g in grams if g in ngram_table]
    if not vecs:
        return None
    return np.sum(vecs, axis=0)

For an unseen word, you still get a vector as long as some of its n-grams are known. whereupon shares <wh, her, ere, and <where with where, so the two land near each other.

Build · BPE: learned subword vocabulary

def learn_bpe(corpus, k_merges):
    vocab = Counter()
    for word, freq in corpus.items():
        tokens = tuple(word) + ("</w>",)
        vocab[tokens] = freq

    merges = []
    for _ in range(k_merges):
        pair_freq = Counter()
        for tokens, freq in vocab.items():
            for a, b in zip(tokens, tokens[1:]):
                pair_freq[(a, b)] += freq
        if not pair_freq:
            break
        best = pair_freq.most_common(1)[0][0]
        merges.append(best)

        new_vocab = Counter()
        for tokens, freq in vocab.items():
            new_tokens = []
            i = 0
            while i < len(tokens):
                if i + 1 < len(tokens) and (tokens[i], tokens[i + 1]) == best:
                    new_tokens.append(tokens[i] + tokens[i + 1])
                    i += 2
                else:
                    new_tokens.append(tokens[i])
                    i += 1
            new_vocab[tuple(new_tokens)] = freq
        vocab = new_vocab
    return merges


def apply_bpe(word, merges):
    tokens = list(word) + ["</w>"]
    for a, b in merges:
        new_tokens = []
        i = 0
        while i < len(tokens):
            if i + 1 < len(tokens) and tokens[i] == a and tokens[i + 1] == b:
                new_tokens.append(a + b)
                i += 2
            else:
                new_tokens.append(tokens[i])
                i += 1
        tokens = new_tokens
    return tokens

>>> corpus = Counter({"low": 5, "lower": 2, "newest": 6, "widest": 3})
>>> merges = learn_bpe(corpus, k_merges=10)
>>> apply_bpe("lowest", merges)
['low', 'est</w>']

First iteration merges the most common adjacent pair. After enough iterations, frequent substrings (low, est, tion) become single tokens and rare words break cleanly.

Real tokenizers have vocabularies of 30k to 260k entries: about 50k for GPT-2, 30k for BERT, 32k for T5, 128k for Llama 3 and 256k for Gemma. When the base symbols are the 256 possible bytes (byte-level BPE, as in GPT-2), any text at all tokenizes into known IDs: no word is ever out of vocabulary.

Worked example
The toy run, merge by merge

On {"low": 5, "lower": 2, "newest": 6, "widest": 3}, the first pass counts adjacent pairs, weighted by word frequency:

Pair Count From
e s, s t, t </w> 9 each newest (6) + widest (3)
w e 8 lower (2) + newest (6)
l o, o w 7 each low (5) + lower (2)

Three pairs tie at 9; Counter.most_common returns the one it counted first, e s. The ten merges come out as es, est, est</w>, lo, low, ne, new, newest</w>, low</w>, wi. "lowest" was never in the corpus, but replaying those merges in order turns l o w e s t </w> into low · est</w>: two pieces, both learned from other words.

Predict before you run

def cos_matrix(M):
    M = M / np.linalg.norm(M, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    return M @ M.T

def in_group_rate(S):              # of each topic word's 4 nearest topic words, how many share its group?
    group = [g for g, ws in groups.items() for _ in ws]
    hits = [sum(group[j] == group[i] for j in np.argsort(-S[i])[1:5]) for i in range(len(topic))]
    return sum(hits) / (4 * len(topic))

ids = [vocab[w] for w in topic]
S_count, S_glove = cos_matrix(np.log1p(X[ids])), cos_matrix(E[ids])
print(f"nearest 4 in the same group: count rows {in_group_rate(S_count):.0%}, GloVe vectors {in_group_rate(S_glove):.0%}")

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(12, 4), gridspec_kw={"width_ratios": [1.9, 1, 1]})
axes[0].imshow(np.log1p(X[ids][:, [vocab[c] for c in context]]), cmap="Blues", aspect="auto")

FastText: vectors for words it never saw

First, the lesson's char_ngrams and fasttext_vector with random n-gram vectors, to show how much similarity comes from shared spelling alone. import time

Even with random, untrained n-gram vectors, "where" and "whereupon" come out similar, purely because they share many pieces; "cat" and "dog" share none and come out near zero. Training then adds meaning on top of spelling. None of the five test words is in the trained model's vocabulary, so Word2Vec would raise a KeyError for each, yet FastText gives every one a vector, and the neighbours mostly make sense: whale words for "whaleship", "friendliness" for "unfriendliness", the right word for the misspelling "shipp". Look at the neighbour lists closely, though, and some entries are related only by letters. That is the method's character: a strong guess from spelling, not understanding.

    print(f"   {w:13s} {emma[w]:5d}x  ->", " · ".join(apply_bpe(w, emma_merges)))

seqs = {w: tuple(w) + ("</w>",) for w in emma}     # replay the merges once, counting tokens after each
curve = [sum(len(s) * emma[w] for w, s in seqs.items())]
for a, b in emma_merges:
    for w, s in seqs.items():
        if a in s and b in s:
            out, i = [], 0
            while i < len(s):
                if i + 1 < len(s) and s[i] == a and s[i + 1] == b:
                    out.append(a + b); i += 2
                else:
                    out.append(s[i]); i += 1
            seqs[w] = tuple(out)
    curve.append(sum(len(s) * emma[w] for w, s in seqs.items()))
print(f"tokens per word: {curve[0] / len(words):.2f} before any merge, {curve[100] / len(words):.2f} after 100, "
      f"{curve[-1] / len(words):.2f} after 600")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 3.4))
ax.plot(range(len(curve)), [c / len(words) for c in curve], color="#474d9a")
ax.set_xlabel("merges learned"); ax.set_ylabel("tokens per word (end marker included)")
ax.set_title("BPE on Emma: tokens per word fall fast, then slowly", fontsize=9)
ax.grid(alpha=0.3); plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

Reading this output

The first merges glue common word endings to the end-of-word marker (e+</w>, t+</w>, d+</w>) and build "th", "er", "in": the most frequent pairs in English. Frequent words like "the" and "emma" quickly become single tokens, while rare and unseen words ("tokenization", "zoomer") break into smaller familiar pieces, never into an unknown symbol. The pieces follow frequency, not grammar: "happiness" splits as happ · in · ess, not happi · ness. The curve falls steeply and then flattens: the first hundred merges do most of the compression, and each later merge helps a rarer pair. Production tokenizers keep going to tens of thousands of merges over far more text.

Reading these outputs

All three tokenizers need 7 tokens for the English greeting. Outside English they part ways. GPT-2 needs about five times as many tokens for Hindi and almost three times as many for Chinese as for English, because its merges were learned on English text: those characters fall back to their raw UTF-8 bytes, shown as strings like à¤. Qwen3's much larger multilingual vocabulary brings French close to English and Chinese below it, and cuts Hindi by a third, though it still costs about three times English. MiniLM's count for Hindi looks short, but the round trip shows why: its lowercasing step also strips accents and combining marks, which deletes the Hindi vowel signs and turns "Café naïve" into "cafe naive". The model never sees the original text. Token counts set context length and API cost, so this is a real cost difference between languages, and it comes from the tokenizer, not the model.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Bigger vocabularies in production models. GPT-4o's o200k_base tokenizer has about 200k entries, Llama 3's about 128k and Gemma 3's about 262k, up from GPT-2's 50k, largely to make non-English text and code cheaper; the token-count chart above shows why that matters.
  • Count tokens with the provider's tool. OpenAI's tiktoken, Anthropic's token-counting endpoint and Hugging Face tokenizers give exact counts for cost and context-window planning; the same text can differ by tens of percent between models.
  • What has not changed. Every production LLM you can call today ships a fixed subword tokenizer, so the rule in the table below holds: use the model's own tokenizer.

Sources: tiktoken, Anthropic token counting, Hugging Face tokenizers, Gemma 3 report.

When to pick which

Situation Pick
Pretrained general-purpose word vectors, no OOV tolerance needed GloVe 300d
Pretrained general-purpose word vectors, must handle misspellings / neologisms / morphologically rich languages FastText
Anything going into a transformer (training or inference) Whatever tokenizer the model shipped with. Never swap.
Training your own language model from scratch Train a BPE or SentencePiece tokenizer on your corpus first
Production text classification with a linear model Still TF-IDF. Lesson 02.
Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • GloVe fits vectors to log co-occurrence counts, weighted so frequent pairs do not dominate; pairs never seen are skipped, so it needs a lot of text.
  • FastText sums character n-gram vectors, so misspellings, inflections and new words still get sensible vectors.
  • BPE learns merges of frequent adjacent symbols; replaying them in order splits any word into known pieces, and byte-level BPE never meets an unknown token.
  • Subword pieces follow frequency, not morphology.
  • Tokenizers are tied to their models and are uneven across languages: the same sentence can cost several times more tokens.

Exercises

  1. Easy. Run char_ngrams("playing") and char_ngrams("played"). Compute the Jaccard overlap of the two n-gram sets. You should see substantial shared pieces (pla, lay, play), which is why FastText transfers well across morphological variants.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Many more

36 tokens with GPT-2, about five times the English count, for a sentence with fewer characters. GPT-2's merges were learned on mostly English text, so very few Devanagari byte sequences were ever merged; each Devanagari character falls back to its three UTF-8 bytes. Qwen3's multilingual vocabulary needs 23, still about three times English. The bar chart shows the same pattern for Chinese, less extreme.

SolutionB1 · Five pieces instead of two

['lo', 'w', 'es', 't', '</w>']. Reversed, the list starts with merges like wi, low</w> and newest</w>, which need pieces that do not exist yet, so they do nothing; by the time es and lo get their turn, the merges that would have built on them (est, est</w>, low) have already been passed. Each merge was learned on a vocabulary that already contained every earlier merge, so apply_bpe must replay them in exactly the learned order. Real tokenizers store the merge list in order, with its rank, for this reason.
