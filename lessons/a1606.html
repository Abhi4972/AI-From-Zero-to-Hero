In plain words first
The idea in one line

Hierarchical is supervisor nested. Manager agents over sub-managers over workers. CrewAI Process.hierarchical is the textbook version: a manager_llm dynamically delegates tasks and validates outputs. The LangGraph equivalent was create_supervisor(create_supervisor(...)) with the older langgraph-supervisor helper; LangChain now recommends building supervisors from tool calls (Lesson 16.5). It is the natural pattern when the task is a real org chart. It is also the pattern most likely to collapse into managerial looping — manager agents assign work poorly, misinterpret sub-outputs, or fail to reach consensus. Sequential often beats it.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
Hierarchical "Org chart pattern" Supervisors over supervisors; only leaves do work.
Manager LLM "The boss" The LLM that decomposes, assigns, and validates at an internal node.
Decomposition drift "The boss lost the plot" Top manager's split no longer covers the original question.
Reconciliation loop "Endless meetings" Sub-managers disagree; top re-delegates; workers re-run; loop until budget exhausted.
Depth-2 ceiling "Don't go deeper than 2 levels" Empirical guardrail: 3+ levels collapses observability.
Canary question "Ground truth at every level" A worker that is always asked the original query unchanged, to detect drift.
Provenance chain "Who said what" Trace from each synthesis back to the leaf outputs that produced it.

Problem

In plain words first

A hierarchy nests supervisors: a top manager delegates to sub-managers, who delegate to workers, and summaries flow back up. It fits work that really has separate teams. Each extra level is another place to misassign work or distort a summary, and errors surface only at the top. LangGraph subgraphs and multi-agent patterns are covered in Lesson 14.85 (linked above). This lesson adds a coverage check to the lesson's hierarchy, works out how reliability falls with depth, and lets a small model decide which teams a request needs.

Once the supervisor pattern clicks, the natural next step is "what if the workers are themselves supervisors?" Teams have sub-teams; companies have departments of departments. Hierarchical architectures mirror that.

The issue: LLM managers are not the same as human managers. A human manager has stable priors about what their reports know. An LLM manager re-reasons the org every turn from whatever is in its context. Tiny drift in that context, and the whole tree misallocates work.

Concept

The shape

                 Manager
                 ┌─────┐
                 └──┬──┘
           ┌────────┴────────┐
           ▼                 ▼
       Sub-Mgr A         Sub-Mgr B
       ┌─────┐           ┌─────┐
       └──┬──┘           └──┬──┘
         ┌┴──┬──┐          ┌┴──┐
         ▼   ▼  ▼          ▼   ▼
       W1  W2  W3         W4  W5

Every internal node plans, delegates, and synthesizes. Only leaves do work.

Where it shines

  • Clear org mapping. If the real task is departmental ("legal review the doc, finance review the doc, engineering review the doc, then summarize for exec"), the hierarchy is explicit.
  • Local summarization. Each sub-manager synthesizes its team's output before the top manager sees it. Top manager sees three sub-manager summaries, not fifteen worker outputs.

Where it breaks

Three failure modes that studies of failed multi-agent runs, such as the MAST taxonomy (Cemri et al., 2025), keep finding:

  1. Task assignment error. The manager reads the goal, hallucinates a decomposition, and delegates to the wrong sub-manager. Because the sub-manager obediently works on what it was given, the error only surfaces at the top synthesis — one level removed from where a human could have caught it.
  2. Output misinterpretation. Sub-manager returns "unable to verify claim X." Top manager summarizes as "claim X not confirmed." Meaning drifts at every level.
  3. Consensus loops. Two sub-managers disagree; top manager asks them to reconcile; they re-delegate down; workers re-run; sub-managers return slightly different answers; loop. CrewAI's Process.hierarchical guards against this with step limits, but the limit itself is now a hyperparameter.

The deciding question

Sequential (linear pipeline) vs hierarchical: does your task actually have independent sub-teams, or is it one linear flow pretending to be a tree? If the latter, use sequential. If the former, use hierarchical but budget explicit reconciliation rules.

Role-framework implementation

CrewAI's Process.hierarchical wires a manager LLM over specialist crews. The manager:

  • receives the top-level task,
  • assigns subtasks to crews,
  • evaluates crew outputs,
  • decides whether to accept, re-delegate, or iterate.

Documentation: https://docs.crewai.com/en/introduction (look for "Hierarchical Process" under Core Concepts).

Graph-framework implementation

LangGraph uses nested create_supervisor calls. The inner supervisor has its own graph; the outer supervisor treats the inner graph as an opaque node. This is cleaner than CrewAI for debugging (you can step through each graph separately) but harder to express dynamic reshaping of the tree.

Reference: https://reference.langchain.com/python/langgraph-supervisor.

The same idea as a formula

Reliability multiplies. If each delegation or summary step is right with probability p, a hierarchy of depth d has d steps down and d−1 summaries up, so the top answer is right with probability

P(correct)=p2d−1.
At p=0.9 that is 90% at depth 1, 73% at depth 2 and 59% at depth 3.

Coverage is a set check. Write down the set A of things the request needs at intake. After the run, compare it with the set B of branches that actually answered: report A∖B (asked, never answered) and B∖A (answered, never asked). A non-empty A∖B means the synthesis must not ship.

Worked example
Catching one mislabel
  • The request needs engineering and legal: A={engineering,legal}.
  • The top manager delegates to engineering and finance, so B={engineering,finance}.
  • A∖B={legal}, so the check stops: the legal question was never answered. B∖A={finance} is extra work nobody asked for.
  • The lesson's TopManager returns a 192-character synthesis either way. Only the check notices.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "The top manager will notice if a branch is wrong."
    ✓ It synthesises whatever comes back. The lesson's run returned a full synthesis with the legal question unanswered.
  • ✗ "An unknown branch name stops the run."
    ✓ The lesson records MISSING[legl] as text and synthesises anyway.
  • ✗ "Adding a level adds one small risk."
    ✓ It adds two steps that must both go right. At 95% per step, depth 3 is right 77% of the time.
  • ✗ "A model asked 'does this need legal?' decides well."
    ✓ The small model said yes to every team for every request; calibrated, it mostly said no.

Checking coverage and counting depth

The lesson's build_hierarchy() and TopManager.run() on the right labels, a mislabel and a typo, each followed by a coverage check against what the request needs. Then p2d−1 for four reliabilities and four depths. Runs in under a second.

# The lesson's TopManager (imported from main.py), with two checks it lacks: does the set of branches cover what the
# user asked for, and does an unknown branch stop the run? Then the arithmetic of depth: if each level gets its step
# right with probability p, how often is the top answer right? Runs in under a second.
from main import build_hierarchy

top = build_hierarchy()
task = "Ship the premium tier feature to production."
asked = {"engineering", "legal"}                         # what the user's request needs, written down at intake
def covered(synth):
    done = {b.sub_manager.replace("-manager", "") for b in synth.branches if not b.sub_manager.startswith("MISSING")}
    done = {"engineering" if d == "eng" else d for d in done}
    return asked - done, done - asked
print("1. coverage of what was asked")
for labels in (["engineering", "legal"], ["engineering", "finance"], ["engineering", "legl"]):
    synth = top.run(task, branch_labels=labels)
    missing, extra = covered(synth)
    flags = [b.sub_manager for b in synth.branches if b.sub_manager.startswith("MISSING")]
    verdict = "ok" if not missing else f"STOP: {sorted(missing)} never answered" + (f"; extra {sorted(extra)}" if extra else "")
    print(f"   labels {labels!s:<28} lesson returns a synthesis anyway ({len(synth.synthesis)} chars){'; ' + flags[0] if flags else ''}")
    print(f"   {'':<35} coverage check: {verdict}")

print("\n2. reliability compounds with depth")
print(f"   {'per-step reliability p':<24}" + "".join(f"{f'depth {d}':>10}" for d in (1, 2, 3, 4)))
for p in (0.99, 0.95, 0.90, 0.80):
    print(f"   {p:<24}" + "".join(f"{p ** (2 * d - 1):>10.0%}" for d in (1, 2, 3, 4)))
print("   (depth d has d delegation steps down and d - 1 summaries up, so 2d - 1 steps must all go right)")

Reading this output

The lesson's hierarchy always returns a synthesis. With the right labels, a mislabel (finance instead of legal) and a typo (legl), it produces 184, 192 and 169 characters of synthesis. The typo shows up only as MISSING[legl] text inside the summary.

A coverage check catches both errors. Written down at intake, "this needs engineering and legal" turns the mislabel into "legal never answered; finance was extra", and the typo into "legal never answered". Either should stop the synthesis from shipping.

Depth is expensive. With 2d − 1 steps that must all go right, 99% per step still gives 95% at depth 3, but 90% per step gives 59%, and 80% gives 33%.

A small model as the decomposer

This block continues from the one above. For eight requests, Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct (CPU) decides which of engineering, legal and finance each needs, from the likelihood of "Yes" against "No", raw and calibrated against a request that needs no team. About 1 minute.

# This block continues from the one above: a model as the top manager's decomposer. For eight requests, Qwen2.5-
# 0.5B-Instruct (CPU) decides which of the three sub-teams (engineering, legal, finance) each request needs, by
# comparing the likelihood of "yes" and "no" for each team, raw and calibrated against a request that needs no
# team. Its choice is compared with the teams each request really needs. About 1 minute.
import os, warnings
os.environ["HF_HUB_OFFLINE"] = "1"
warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")
import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM, logging as hf_logging
hf_logging.set_verbosity_error(); hf_logging.disable_progress_bar()
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct")
llm = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct").eval()

TEAMS = {"engineering": "builds and reviews the code", "legal": "checks contracts, privacy and compliance",
         "finance": "checks cost and budget"}
REQUESTS = [("Ship the premium tier feature; it changes the terms of service.", {"engineering", "legal"}),
            ("Our cloud bill doubled; find out which service is responsible.", {"engineering", "finance"}),
            ("Can we sign this vendor contract? It sets a $200k yearly fee.", {"legal", "finance"}),
            ("Fix the login bug before Friday's release.", {"engineering"}),
            ("A customer asked us to delete all their personal data under GDPR.", {"engineering", "legal"}),
            ("Approve next quarter's hiring budget.", {"finance"}),
            ("Review the open-source licence of a library we want to bundle.", {"legal"}),
            ("Migrate the database to a cheaper hosting plan without downtime.", {"engineering", "finance"})]
def margin(req, team):
    """log P(Yes) - log P(No) for the first token of the answer."""
    ids = tok.apply_chat_template([{"role": "user", "content": f"Request: {req}\nThe {team} team {TEAMS[team]}. "
                                    f"Does this request need the {team} team? Answer yes or no."}],
                                  add_generation_prompt=True, return_tensors="pt", return_dict=True)["input_ids"]
    with torch.no_grad():
        logits = llm(ids).logits[0, -1]
    return (logits[tok("Yes")["input_ids"][0]] - logits[tok("No")["input_ids"][0]]).item()
BASE = {t: margin("Say good morning to everyone.", t) for t in TEAMS}     # how much it says yes to nothing at all
score = {"raw yes/no": [0, 0, 0], "calibrated": [0, 0, 0]}
print(f"{'request':<46} {'needs':<24} {'raw yes/no':<26} calibrated")
for req, truth in REQUESTS:
    m = {t: margin(req, t) for t in TEAMS}
    picks = {"raw yes/no": {t for t in TEAMS if m[t] > 0},
             "calibrated": {t for t in TEAMS if m[t] - BASE[t] > 0}}
    for k, p in picks.items():
        score[k][0] += p == truth; score[k][1] += len(truth - p); score[k][2] += len(p - truth)
    print(f"{req[:44]:<46} {','.join(sorted(truth)):<24} {','.join(sorted(picks['raw yes/no'])):<26} "
          f"{','.join(sorted(picks['calibrated'])) or '-'}")
for k, (ex, mi, ad) in score.items():
    print(f"{k:<11} exact team set {ex}/8; teams missed {mi}; teams added that were not needed {ad}")

Reading this output

Raw, the model says yes to everything. Every request was sent to all three teams: no team was missed, but 11 unneeded team calls were added across 8 requests, and not one request got exactly the right set.

Calibrated, it says no to nearly everything. Subtracting how strongly it says yes to "Say good morning" leaves teams only where the request pushes harder than that. It got 1 request exactly right (the licence review, legal only), missed 9 needed teams and added 1.

Neither is a decomposer you can trust. A small model cannot separate "needs legal" from "might involve legal". Write the needed teams into the request schema at intake, or use a much stronger model and still check coverage after.

Build it

code/main.py runs a 3-level hierarchy:

  • top manager: splits a task into "engineering" and "legal" branches,
  • engineering sub-manager: splits into "frontend" and "backend" workers,
  • legal sub-manager: one worker.

Demo contrasts happy path (everyone agrees) against a perturbed path where the top manager's decomposition mislabels "legal" as "finance" and watches the error cascade — the sub-manager obediently does finance work, the top synthesizer reports finance findings, the original legal question goes unanswered.

Part 1 of 3 · LeafOutput, SubSummary, TopSynthesis, Worker, SubManager

"""Hierarchical multi-agent with decomposition-drift demo.

3-level hierarchy: top manager -> sub-managers -> workers.
Run happy path and a perturbed path where the top manager mislabels one branch.
Watch the error cascade.
"""
from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass, field


@dataclass
class LeafOutput:
    worker: str
    question: str
    answer: str


@dataclass
class SubSummary:
    sub_manager: str
    leaves: list[LeafOutput]
    summary: str


@dataclass
class TopSynthesis:
    top_manager: str
    branches: list[SubSummary]
    synthesis: str


class Worker:
    def __init__(self, name: str, canned: dict[str, str]) -> None:
        self.name = name
        self.canned = canned

    def run(self, question: str) -> LeafOutput:
        key = self._match_key(question)
        ans = self.canned.get(key, f"[no canned answer for '{question}']")
        return LeafOutput(worker=self.name, question=question, answer=ans)

    def _match_key(self, q: str) -> str:
        ql = q.lower()
        for k in self.canned:
            if k in ql:
                return k
        return "default"


class SubManager:
    def __init__(self, name: str, workers: list[Worker], split: dict[str, str]) -> None:
        self.name = name
        self.workers = workers
        self.split = split

    def run(self, task: str) -> SubSummary:
        leaves = []
        for w in self.workers:
            sub_q = self.split.get(w.name, task)
            leaves.append(w.run(sub_q))
        summary = f"[{self.name}] aggregated: " + " | ".join(l.answer for l in leaves)
        return SubSummary(sub_manager=self.name, leaves=leaves, summary=summary)

Part 2 of 3 · TopManager, build_hierarchy, render

class TopManager:
    def __init__(self, name: str, subs: dict[str, SubManager]) -> None:
        self.name = name
        self.subs = subs

    def run(self, task: str, branch_labels: list[str]) -> TopSynthesis:
        summaries: list[SubSummary] = []
        for label in branch_labels:
            if label not in self.subs:
                summaries.append(
                    SubSummary(
                        sub_manager=f"MISSING[{label}]",
                        leaves=[],
                        summary=f"[top] tried to delegate to '{label}' -- no such sub-manager",
                    )
                )
                continue
            summaries.append(self.subs[label].run(f"{task} -- branch: {label}"))
        synth = "top synthesis: " + " || ".join(s.summary for s in summaries)
        return TopSynthesis(top_manager=self.name, branches=summaries, synthesis=synth)


def build_hierarchy() -> TopManager:
    fe = Worker("fe", {"frontend": "React component audited; 2 issues."})
    be = Worker("be", {"backend": "API endpoints audited; 1 issue."})
    eng = SubManager(
        "eng-manager",
        [fe, be],
        {"fe": "frontend review of the feature", "be": "backend review of the feature"},
    )
    lw = Worker("lawyer", {"legal": "Contract clauses A and B are non-compliant."})
    legal = SubManager("legal-manager", [lw], {"lawyer": "legal review of the feature"})
    fw = Worker(
        "finance",
        {"finance": "Projected cost $42k/month; exceeds budget by 12%."},
    )
    finance = SubManager("finance-manager", [fw], {"finance": "finance review of the feature"})
    return TopManager("vp-eng", {"engineering": eng, "legal": legal, "finance": finance})


def render(label: str, synth: TopSynthesis) -> None:
    print(f"\n=== {label} ===")
    for branch in synth.branches:
        print(f"  [sub] {branch.sub_manager}")
        for leaf in branch.leaves:
            print(f"    [leaf] {leaf.worker:8s} asked: {leaf.question}")
            print(f"           answered: {leaf.answer}")
        print(f"    [summary] {branch.summary}")
    print(f"  [top] {synth.synthesis}")


def main() -> None:
    print("Hierarchical multi-agent with decomposition-drift demo")
    print("-" * 60)

    top = build_hierarchy()
    task = "Ship the premium tier feature to production."

    happy = top.run(task, branch_labels=["engineering", "legal"])
    render("Happy path (correct branches)", happy)

    perturbed = top.run(task, branch_labels=["engineering", "finance"])
    render("Perturbed path (top manager mislabels 'legal' as 'finance')", perturbed)

    print("\nUser asked about legal/engineering review.")
    print("Happy path: both legal and engineering answer truthfully.")
    print("Perturbed path: finance dutifully answers, legal question goes unanswered.")
    print("The error appears at TOP synthesis -- one level removed from where a human could catch it.")

Break it on purpose

Your compliance assistant runs a CrewAI hierarchy: a manager over security, privacy and legal crews. An audit finds that privacy questions were sometimes routed to security, whose summaries said "no issues found", and the manager's reports said "privacy reviewed, no issues". Using this lesson, what was missing? (Solution B1.)

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • CrewAI's hierarchical process puts a manager LLM over crews that delegates and validates; LangGraph builds nested supervisors as subgraphs; the Claude Agent SDK lets subagents spawn subagents, 3 levels deep by default.
  • Failure studies name the patterns. The MAST taxonomy (Cemri et al., 2025) sorts failed multi-agent runs into specification, inter-agent misalignment and task verification failures, and finds each category common.
  • Shallow beats deep in practice. Production systems such as Anthropic's Research feature use one lead over workers rather than several management layers.
  • On your laptop: keep hierarchies to two levels with a small model, and write the coverage set at intake so a script can check the result.

Sources: CrewAI, Hierarchical process, Cemri et al., Why Do Multi-Agent LLM Systems Fail? (arXiv:2503.13657), Claude Agent SDK, Subagents, Anthropic, Multi-agent research system.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Hierarchies fit tasks with genuinely separate teams; otherwise use a pipeline or a single supervisor.
  • Reliability falls as p2d−1; every level costs two chances to go wrong.
  • Write down what the request needs at intake and check coverage before the synthesis ships.
  • Treat an unknown or missing branch as a failure, not text in the summary.
  • A small model is a poor decomposer: it said yes to everything, or no to nearly everything.

Ship it

If you ship hierarchical:

  • Cap tree depth at 2. Three levels already hides most errors from observability.
  • Explicit reconciliation budget. Set max rounds before the top manager must commit. Usually 2.
  • Provenance on every synthesis. Each node's summary must cite which leaf outputs produced it.
  • Alert on decomposition drift. Log the manager's decomposition per step; diff against the user query. If the decomposition no longer covers the query, fire an alert.

outputs/skill-hierarchy-fitness.md

---
name: hierarchy-fitness
description: Decide whether a multi-agent task fits hierarchical, flat supervisor, or sequential. Surface the failure modes that matter.
version: 1.0.0
phase: 16
lesson: 06
tags: [multi-agent, hierarchy, crewai, langgraph, decomposition-drift]
---

Given a task description and an optional org structure, recommend the coordination pattern (flat supervisor, hierarchical, sequential) and list the specific failure modes to guard against.

Produce:

1. **Task shape analysis.** Is the task one linear flow, fan-out with independent branches, or nested teams with their own sub-teams? Justify.
2. **Pattern verdict.** Sequential, flat supervisor, or hierarchical. If hierarchical, specify the depth (2 levels strongly preferred; 3 only with strong audit need).
3. **Decomposition plan.** The exact split the top manager should make. For each branch, name the sub-manager and the bounded scope.
4. **Reconciliation budget.** Number of rounds allowed before the top manager must commit. Default 2.
5. **Guardrails.** Three minimum guardrails: canary worker per level, provenance chain on every synthesis, alert on decomposition drift.
6. **Failure-mode checklist.** Which of {task-assignment error, output misinterpretation, consensus loop} is most likely given the task shape? Describe one concrete symptom and one mitigation per mode.

Hard rejects:

- Any recommendation that proposes depth > 2 without naming a concrete audit or org requirement that demands it.
- Hierarchical for single-linear-flow tasks. Those should be sequential pipelines.
- Designs without an explicit reconciliation budget.

Refusal rules:

- If the task is simple enough to fit one agent (under ~10 tool calls), refuse hierarchy and recommend single-agent.
- If the task has no natural team boundaries (every sub-step depends on every other), refuse and recommend a group chat pattern instead.
- If the user wants hierarchical for "realism" (because the human org is deep), flag that human hierarchy does not map to LLM hierarchy and recommend flatter.

Output: one-page brief. Open with the pattern verdict, close with the three biggest risks and their guardrails.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Nothing raised, and 59% at depth 3

Nothing is raised. The finance branch answers its own question, the synthesis includes it, and the legal question is simply never asked. The error is visible only if someone compares what was asked with what was answered.

At 90% per step, depth 3 means 5 steps (3 down, 2 summaries up), so 0.9⁵ ≈ 59% of top-level answers are right.

SolutionB1 · Coverage nobody checked

Two things were missing.

  • A coverage check. The request needed privacy, and no privacy crew answered. Written down at intake, that gap would have stopped the report.
  • Faithful summaries. "Security found no issues" became "privacy reviewed, no issues" one level up, which is the output-misinterpretation failure. Require each summary to name the branch that produced it and to keep claims within that branch's scope.

Also consider flattening: three crews under one manager is already deep enough. A single supervisor with a routing table for privacy, security and legal questions is easier to check.

Questions from real AI engineering interviews that this lesson prepares you for. Answer out loud first, then open the worked answer.

Debug a failureA three-level agent hierarchy reviews a contract. The top manager splits the work into an engineering branch and a legal branch, each with its own sub-manager and workers. The final report is complete and confident, and it contains finance findings where the legal review should be. Nobody asked a finance question. Trace the failure and say what you change in the design.

Level 3 of 4 · about 12 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. The top manager mislabeled the legal branch as finance when it decomposed the task. The sub-manager did what it was given, the workers did finance work well, and the top synthesizer reported finance findings. The error surfaced only at the top, one level away from any point where a human could have caught it. The design fixes are a depth cap of two, provenance on every synthesis, a logged decomposition that is checked against the original question, and a canary worker that is always asked the user's question unchanged.

Why hierarchy hides this.

A human manager has stable knowledge of what each team does. A model manager re-derives the whole structure every turn from whatever is in its context. A small drift in that context changes the decomposition, and every level below works on the drifted version in good faith. The lesson names three failure modes, and this incident is the first:

Failure Mechanism
Task assignment error The manager hallucinates a decomposition and sends work to the wrong branch
Output misinterpretation A sub-manager returns "unable to verify claim X" and the top reports "claim X not confirmed", and meaning drifts at every level
Consensus loop Two sub-managers disagree, the top asks them to reconcile, they re-delegate, workers re-run, and the loop continues until a step limit

Trace it.

  1. Read the top manager's decomposition log. The branch label is "finance", not "legal".
  2. Read the sub-manager's input. It received a finance task and had no way to know it was wrong.
  3. Read the synthesis. It cites finance findings. The original legal question appears nowhere.

The side-by-side of what was asked and what was delivered is the whole diagnosis.

The design changes.

  • Cap the depth at two. Three levels already hide most errors from observability.
  • Provenance on every synthesis. Each node's summary cites the leaf outputs that produced it, so a report can be traced to the workers who wrote it.
  • Alert on decomposition drift. Log the manager's decomposition at each step and compare it with the user's question. When the decomposition no longer covers the question, fire an alert before the workers start.
  • A canary worker per sub-manager. One worker is always asked the original question unchanged. When the canary's answer disagrees with the synthesized answer, the manager stops and escalates instead of merging.
  • An explicit reconciliation budget. Set the rounds before the top must commit, usually two.

The deciding question.

Before you keep the hierarchy, ask whether the task has independent sub-teams or is a linear flow that looks like a tree. A contract review with legal and engineering branches is a real tree. A task where step two needs step one is a pipeline, and sequential beats hierarchical there.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Add a fourth level to check the third." More levels hide more errors from observability, and the fix is fewer levels with provenance.
  • "Tell the sub-manager to double-check the task it receives." The sub-manager had no access to the original question, and a canary worker is the mechanism that gives it one.
  • "Use a bigger model for the top manager." A bigger manager still re-derives the structure each turn from its context, and the mislabel is a context drift, not a capability gap.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) How many levels did the error travel before the output diverged from the question? Expected: one, because the mislabel happened at the top and every level below executed it faithfully, which is why the depth cap is two.
  • (level 3) The canary's answer and the merged answer differ. What does the manager do? Expected: stop, record both answers with provenance, and escalate to a human or re-decompose, never merge the two.
  • (level 4) How do you detect meaning drift between levels? Expected: compare each node's summary with the leaf outputs it cites, and flag a summary that changes the modality of a claim, such as unverified becoming false.
  • (level 4) The reconciliation loop keeps hitting its step limit. What does that tell you? Expected: the limit is now a hyperparameter that hides a disagreement, so either the decomposition is wrong or the question has no single answer, and the top must report the disagreement.
