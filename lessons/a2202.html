In plain words first
The idea in one line

Before any model, turn a messy market report into a clean table of prices per bushel, then look at it from a few angles.

The problem

The US Department of Agriculture publishes wholesale pumpkin prices from city markets. The raw file mixes package sizes (bushels, half bushels, bins, cartons) and gives a low and a high price per sale. To ask anything about price you first need one comparable price per row.

Step 1 · Load the market report and keep bushel sales

Only rows priced by the bushel are kept, so the units are comparable.

Step 2 · Build a clean table of monthly prices

The average of low and high becomes the price, the date becomes a month, and fractional bushels are scaled to a full bushel.

# A set of new columns for a new dataframe. Filter out nonmatching columns
columns_to_select = ['Package', 'Low Price', 'High Price', 'Date']
pumpkins = pumpkins.loc[:, columns_to_select]

# Get an average between low and high price for the base pumpkin price
price = (pumpkins['Low Price'] + pumpkins['High Price']) / 2

# Convert the date to its month only
month = pd.DatetimeIndex(pumpkins['Date']).month

# Create a new dataframe with this basic data
new_pumpkins = pd.DataFrame({'Month': month, 'Package': pumpkins['Package'], 'Low Price': pumpkins['Low Price'],'High Price': pumpkins['High Price'], 'Price': price})

# Convert the price if the Package contains fractional bushel values
new_pumpkins.loc[new_pumpkins['Package'].str.contains('1 1/9'), 'Price'] = price/(1 + 1/9)

new_pumpkins.loc[new_pumpkins['Package'].str.contains('1/2'), 'Price'] = price/(1/2)

print(new_pumpkins)

Step 4 · The same questions with Seaborn

Seaborn works directly on a DataFrame and adds statistics such as confidence intervals to bar charts.

Visualizing with Seaborn

Seaborn is built on top of Matplotlib and works directly with dataframes, making it quick to create attractive statistical plots with very little code.

Scatter plots to show relationships

sns.relplot(x="Price", y="Month", data=new_pumpkins)

Exercises

  1. Which month has the highest average price? Read it off the bar chart, then confirm it with groupby.
  2. Add a City column to the clean table and draw one box plot of price per city. Which market is most expensive?
  3. The heatmap shows Low and High Price almost perfectly correlated with Price. Why is that expected, and why would using them as features be a mistake when predicting price?
Recap
What to keep
  • Clean before you model: one unit, one price per row.
  • Plots are cheap checks for structure and mistakes.
  • A feature built from the target leaks the answer.
