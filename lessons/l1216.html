Humans in charge: approvals and handoffs

Lesson goals
What you will be able to do

  • Design approvals people actually read, and detect when they have become a rubber stamp
  • Hand work to a person with a complete packet, so the customer never repeats themselves

Why this came about

As agents took consequential actions, organizations needed people to approve, review and take over. Human-in-the-loop design moved from a debugging feature to a product and compliance requirement, with lessons about reviewer fatigue and escalation.

In plain words first
Deciding who signs, and how

Some actions need a person: approve before, review after, or take over the conversation. Designing this well means deciding by risk which actions need approval, giving reviewers the context to decide quickly, handling timeouts and absent reviewers, recording who approved what, and watching for reviewer fatigue, where people approve without reading. Human-in-the-loop blocks until a person decides; human-on-the-loop lets work continue under supervision.

"Human in the loop" is easy to say and hard to do well. Put a person in front of every action and they will approve hundreds a day without reading them; put nobody there and one bad decision reaches the world. The design question is where people add judgment, what they need to see to exercise it, and what happens when they are not there. Lesson 3.5 introduced approval levels and Lesson 8.4 the protocols (AG-UI, interrupts) that carry them; this lesson is about designing the human side.

Approval tiers by risk

Give every action an approval tier, set by its risk, before the agent can take it.

Tier When What the person does Example
Automatic Low value, reversible, within policy Nothing; it is logged and sampled for review Order status, small refunds on known customers
Notify after Reversible, moderate value Can undo within a window A refund of a few thousand rupees, a calendar change
Approve before Irreversible, high value, new counterparty or outside normal policy Reads a summary with evidence and approves or rejects A large refund, a payment to a new account, an email to a customer list
Two-person approval Irreversible and very high value Two people approve independently Moving large sums, deleting production data

Hands-on · Approval routing, and approvals that are real

The script routes a day of actions to tiers, then checks reviewer behavior. An approval only protects you if the reviewer actually decides; a reviewer who approves almost everything in two seconds is a sign that the queue is badly designed, not that the reviewer is careless.

import random, statistics
from collections import Counter

def tier(action):
    """Route an action to the lightest review that matches its risk."""
    if action["irreversible"] and action["amount"] > 50_000:
        return "two-person approval"
    if action["irreversible"] or action["amount"] > 5_000 or action["new_counterparty"]:
        return "approval"
    if action["amount"] > 1_000:
        return "notify after"
    return "auto"

rng = random.Random(8)
actions = [{"amount": rng.choices([200, 499, 1200, 2500, 4999, 7999, 25_000, 90_000], [30, 25, 20, 12, 6, 4, 2, 1])[0],
            "irreversible": rng.random() < 0.08, "new_counterparty": rng.random() < 0.05}
           for _ in range(2_000)]
mix = Counter(tier(a) for a in actions)
print("one day, 2,000 actions:", dict(sorted(mix.items(), key=lambda kv: -kv[1])))
print(f"reviews needed: {mix['approval'] + 2 * mix['two-person approval']} "
      f"(vs 2,000 if every action needed approval)")

# Are the approvals real? Flag reviewers who approve almost everything, almost instantly.
reviews = []
for r in ["asha", "ben", "chen"]:
    for _ in range(150):
        fast = r == "ben"
        reviews.append({"reviewer": r, "seconds": rng.uniform(1, 4) if fast else rng.uniform(20, 240),
                        "approved": rng.random() < (0.995 if fast else 0.86)})
for r in ["asha", "ben", "chen"]:
    mine = [x for x in reviews if x["reviewer"] == r]
    rate = sum(x["approved"] for x in mine) / len(mine)
    med = statistics.median(x["seconds"] for x in mine)
    flag = "  <-- rubber-stamping risk: review the queue design" if rate > 0.98 and med < 10 else ""
    print(f"  {r:5} approved {rate:6.1%} | median review {med:5.0f} s{flag}")
What you should see
one day, 2,000 actions: {'auto': 977, 'notify after': 658, 'approval': 361, 'two-person approval': 4}
reviews needed: 369 (vs 2,000 if every action needed approval)
  asha  approved  91.3% | median review   124 s
  ben   approved  99.3% | median review     3 s  <-- rubber-stamping risk: review the queue design
  chen  approved  82.7% | median review   122 s

Routing by risk cuts reviews from 2,000 to a few hundred, which is what makes careful review possible at all. The second check flags one reviewer as a rubber stamp. The fix is usually the queue, not the person: too many low-risk items, summaries that bury the one thing that matters, or targets that reward speed. Make approvals rarer, show the deciding fact first ("new account, first payment, INR 90,000"), and sample approved items for audit.

From the field
Every control "worked"

In 2020 Citibank accidentally paid about USD 900 million to Revlon's lenders instead of an interest payment of about USD 8 million. Two Sigma's John Feminella uses the case to show that the procedure, the warning popup and three human approvers all functioned as designed, and the payment still went out, because the interface did not make the consequence clear to the people approving it. His recommendations for agents: a registry of agents, review proportional to risk, tool access that follows each agent's declared purpose, shared evaluations across the firm, and classifiers that route only the approvals that matter to people (What a $900M Bank Error Teaches Us About AI Agents, 19 min).

What an approval screen must show

  • The decision in one line, with the consequence: "Refund INR 7,999 to Priya's original card; cannot be reversed after 24 hours."
  • Why the agent proposes it, with evidence links (order, photo, policy section), not the agent's whole transcript.
  • What is unusual: above limit, new counterparty, outside business hours, different from the customer's history.
  • The alternatives: approve, edit (change the amount), reject with a reason, or take over.
  • A deadline and what happens if nobody decides.

Rejections and edits are training data: log the reason, and review them weekly to improve the agent or the policy (Lesson 11.9).

Hands-on · A handoff packet nobody has to re-ask

When the agent escalates, the person who takes over should not need to read the transcript or ask the customer again. The packet below has a fixed set of fields, and the function reports any that are missing so the escalation can be blocked until it is complete.

REQUIRED = ["customer", "goal", "what_was_done", "evidence", "open_question", "suggested_next_step", "trace_id"]

def handoff(run):
    """Build the packet a person needs to take over without re-asking the customer anything."""
    packet = {
        "customer": run["customer"],
        "goal": run["goal"],
        "what_was_done": [f"{s['tool']}: {s['summary']}" for s in run["steps"]],
        "evidence": [s["ref"] for s in run["steps"] if s.get("ref")],
        "open_question": run.get("blocker"),
        "suggested_next_step": run.get("proposal"),
        "trace_id": run["trace_id"],
    }
    missing = [k for k in REQUIRED if not packet.get(k)]
    return packet, missing

run = {"customer": "C-2231 (Priya)", "goal": "refund for damaged item on A-1077",
       "steps": [{"tool": "get_order", "summary": "delivered 2 Nov, INR 7,999", "ref": "order A-1077"},
                 {"tool": "check_photos", "summary": "photo shows cracked screen", "ref": "upload U-88"},
                 {"tool": "policy", "summary": "damage refunds above INR 5,000 need approval", "ref": "kb refunds#3"}],
       "blocker": "amount is above the agent's refund limit",
       "proposal": "approve full refund of INR 7,999 and a return pickup",
       "trace_id": "r-0517"}
packet, missing = handoff(run)
for k in REQUIRED:
    v = packet[k]
    print(f"{k:20} {'; '.join(v) if isinstance(v, list) else v}")
print("missing fields:", missing or "none")
What you should see

goal                 refund for damaged item on A-1077
what_was_done        get_order: delivered 2 Nov, INR 7,999; check_photos: photo shows cracked screen; policy: damage refunds above INR 5,000 need approval
evidence             order A-1077; upload U-88; kb refunds#3

suggested_next_step  approve full refund of INR 7,999 and a return pickup

missing fields: none

The colleague can approve in one step, with the evidence attached and the trace one click away. Make the packet a contract: an escalation without it is a bug, counted on the dashboard (Lesson 12.14).

When the human is not there

Situation Bad default Better design
Approval not given in time Work waits forever, or proceeds silently A deadline per tier; then escalate to a backup approver, or cancel and tell the user
Reviewer queue grows Reviewers approve faster and read less Admission control on actions needing approval; raise thresholds only with evidence
Out of hours Agent acts anyway, or stops all work Business-hours policy per action; queue high-risk actions, keep low-risk ones running
Customer asks for a person Agent keeps trying Always honor it, with the handoff packet
Person takes over mid-task Agent and person both act Explicit ownership change: the agent stops acting on that case and records why

Design for oversight in the interface

Users stay in charge when they can see what the agent did (an activity log in plain words, with links), correct it (edit a draft, undo an action within its window) and stop it (a visible pause or take-over control). Show progress for long runs, and say honestly when something is queued or waiting for a person. These are product requirements, not polish: they are what lets people trust an agent with more autonomy over time.

Hands-on lab
Lab P9 · Put people where they matter
  1. List every action your agent can take and assign each a tier with a written reason.
  2. Count how many approvals a normal day would create. If it is more than a reviewer can read properly, change the thresholds or the design.
  3. Design the approval screen for your riskiest action: one line, evidence, what is unusual, alternatives and deadline.
  4. Make the handoff packet a required contract and add a check that blocks incomplete escalations.
  5. Write what happens for each tier when no reviewer responds in time.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "More approvals mean more safety."
    ✓ Too many approvals train people to click without reading.
  • ✗ "A pending approval can wait forever."
    ✓ Set timeouts with a safe default, such as deny or escalate.
  • ✗ "Approval is a UI feature."
    ✓ It needs durable state, audit records and permission checks behind it.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Rubber-stamping

Reviewers are not really reviewing; approval fatigue makes the control ineffective. Raise the threshold, show reviewers what matters, sample-audit decisions and automate safe cases.

SolutionB1 · Handoffs need context

Repeating everything to the person. Pass a summary, the conversation, the facts gathered and the attempted actions.

SolutionD1 · Approval SLAs

Deadlines, reminders and escalation for pending approvals, and a default action (decline with a message) when they expire.

SolutionM1 · An approval screen

Order, amount, policy check result, customer history, the agent's reasoning summary; approve, edit amount, reject with reason; log decision, reviewer, time and outcome.

Check your understanding

Explain the mechanismHow do you design human oversight for agents?

Worked answer. Decide which actions need approval by risk, design reviewer tools with the right context and decisions, set SLAs and escalation, measure reviewer quality and load to avoid rubber-stamping, design smooth handoffs to people, and use decisions as training and eval data.

A plausible wrong answer. "Add an approve button." Oversight that is not designed becomes a formality.

Follow-ups. How do you prevent approval fatigue?

Debug a failureAfter adding approvals, customer satisfaction fell. Why?

Worked answer. Waiting: approvals added delays without communication. Show status to customers, set SLAs, and auto-approve low-risk cases.

A plausible wrong answer. "Customers dislike AI." They dislike waiting without updates.

Follow-ups. Which cases would you auto-approve?

Defend a designWhen should a human be in the loop, on the loop, or out of the loop?

Worked answer. In the loop (approve each action) for high-risk, irreversible actions; on the loop (monitor and intervene) for medium risk at volume; out of the loop for low-risk, reversible tasks with audits. Revisit as evals and incident history build trust.

A plausible wrong answer. "Always in the loop for safety." It does not scale and becomes rubber-stamping.

Follow-ups. What evidence would move a task out of the loop?
