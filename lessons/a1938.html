In plain words first
The idea in one line

Track B's first capstone. A pretrained language model is a stack of self-attention blocks ending in a token-prediction head. When you want spam vs ham, the head is wrong but the body is mostly right. This lesson rips the head off, glues a two-class linear layer onto the pooled representation, and trains the classifier two different ways: final-layer only, and full fine-tuning. The eval is precision, recall, and F1 on a held-out split. You learn what each strategy buys you and what it costs.

The problem

You pre-trained a small transformer on a generic corpus. The output head projects the last hidden state to a 1000-token vocabulary. You now have 800 SMS messages labelled spam or ham and you want a binary classifier. Three options exist.

The wrong option is to train a fresh classifier from scratch on 800 examples. The body of the pretrained model already encodes useful structure: word identity, position, simple co-occurrence. Throwing it away wastes the compute that built it.

The two right options are head swap with the body frozen, and head swap with the body trainable. Head-only training is fast, almost free in memory, and rarely overfits with this little data. Full fine-tuning is slower, can overfit on small data, but reaches higher accuracy when the downstream domain drifts from the pretraining corpus.

This lesson builds both, so you can compare them on the same fixture.

The idea

The model is a function f_theta(tokens) -> hidden_states. The head is a function g_phi(hidden) -> logits. Swapping heads means keeping theta and replacing g_phi. The body's parameters are the expensive part. The head is a single linear layer.

Two trainable parameter sets matter:

  • theta (the body): tens of thousands of weights per attention block.
  • phi (the head): hidden_dim * num_classes weights plus a bias.

In head-only training you compute gradients against phi and zero them against theta. PyTorch lets you do this by setting requires_grad=False on body parameters. The optimiser then sees only the head and the body stays frozen.

In full fine-tuning you let gradients flow back through the whole stack. The body's weights drift to fit the classification objective. The risk is catastrophic forgetting on small data: the body's pretraining gets washed out by overfitting noise.

The Pooling Question

A classifier needs one vector per sequence, not one vector per token. Three common choices:

  • Mean pool: average the hidden states across the sequence, weighted by the attention mask.
  • CLS pool: prepend a special token and use only its output. This is what BERT does.
  • Last-token pool: use the last non-padding token. This is what GPT-class classifiers do.

This lesson uses mean pooling with explicit attention-mask weighting. It is the simplest, gives a stable signal across sequence lengths, and does not require pretraining a CLS token.

The Data

Eight hundred SMS messages, balanced 400 spam and 400 ham, are generated deterministically in code/main.py. The generator uses a fixed seed, picks templates and substitutes slot fillers, and emits messages between 5 and 25 tokens long. Real datasets have noise this fixture does not. The point of the fixture is reproducibility.

The data splits 80/20: 640 train, 160 test. Splits are stratified so the test set keeps the 50/50 balance. A held-out set with a known balance lets precision and recall be read as honest numbers.

The Metrics

Binary classification with class 1 as the positive class (spam). Counts are:

  • TP: predicted spam, was spam.
  • FP: predicted spam, was ham.
  • FN: predicted ham, was spam.
  • TN: predicted ham, was ham.

The three headline metrics:

  • precision = TP / (TP + FP). Of the messages flagged spam, what fraction actually are?
  • recall = TP / (TP + FN). Of the actual spam, what fraction did the model flag?
  • F1 = 2 * P * R / (P + R). The harmonic mean of the two.

A confusion matrix prints the four counts as a 2x2 grid. The demo writes this to stdout for both training regimes.

Architecture

The body is a deliberately tiny transformer: vocab 260, hidden 64, 4 heads, 2 blocks, max sequence 32. It is small enough to train both regimes to convergence inside ninety seconds on CPU. It is not pretrained in the lesson; instead, the pretrain_quick helper does five epochs of LM training on the same fixture's text to give the body a non-trivial starting point. This keeps the lesson self-contained.

What you will build

The implementation is one main.py plus one test module (code/tests/test_main.py).

  1. ByteTokenizer: maps bytes to ids, reserves a pad id.
  2. Block: a transformer block with multi-head attention and a feed-forward layer. Pre-norm.
  3. LMBody: token + position embeddings plus a stack of blocks. Returns hidden states.
  4. MeanPool: mask-weighted average over the sequence axis.
  5. Classifier: body, pool, linear head. The body is the same instance across regimes.
  6. freeze_body and unfreeze_body: toggle requires_grad on body parameters.
  7. train_classifier: one shared loop. Accepts the model and an optimiser configured for whichever parameter group is trainable.
  8. evaluate: runs the test set and returns Metrics(precision, recall, f1, confusion).
  9. run_demo: pretrains the body briefly, then trains and evaluates head-only, then full, prints both reports, and exits zero.

Why the comparison matters

The head-only regime usually trains faster and underfits more gracefully. On this fixture you typically see precision near 0.9 and recall near 0.85 after twenty epochs of head-only training. Full fine-tuning takes about three times longer and lands within a couple of points either way, depending on the random seed.

The lesson does not pick a winner. It teaches you to read the numbers and the cost. On 800 examples and a tiny body, head-only is the right call. On 80,000 examples and a bigger body, full fine-tuning starts to pay off. The contract you take from this lesson is the API: the same train_classifier function handles both, and the toggle is one call.

Stretch goals

  • Add a third regime that unfreezes only the last block. This is sometimes called partial fine-tuning. It costs less than full FT and learns more than head-only.
  • Add a learning-rate scheduler. A cosine schedule on the head plus a smaller constant rate on the body is a common production setup.
  • Replace mean pooling with a learned attention pool: a small attention layer with one learned query. This often beats mean pool on longer sequences.

The implementation gives you the hooks. The tests pin the contract. The numbers are yours to push.

Weigh a trade-offYou have a small pretrained transformer and 800 labelled text messages, half of them spam. You need a binary classifier by tomorrow. Do you train only a new classification head, or do you update every weight? Defend the choice.

Level 1 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Swap the language-model head for a two-class linear layer and start with the body frozen. Head-only training is fast, costs almost no memory, and rarely overfits on this little data. Unfreeze the body only when the frozen run leaves accuracy on the table and the domain is far from the pretraining text.

Two regimes, one loop.

The body maps tokens to hidden states and stays as it is. The head maps a pooled hidden state to two logits and is new. Head-only training sets requires_grad=False on the body, so the optimizer sees only the head. Full fine-tuning lets the gradient flow through the whole stack, and the body drifts toward the classification objective.

What each one buys.

On the fixture in the capstone lesson, head-only training reached a precision near 0.9 and a recall near 0.85 after twenty epochs. Full fine-tuning took about three times longer and landed within a couple of points either way, depending on the seed. With 800 examples the body has little to learn from the labels, and a lot to forget.

Pooling.

A classifier needs one vector per sequence. Mean pooling weighted by the attention mask is the simplest choice and gives a stable signal across lengths. A CLS token needs pretraining of that token. Last-token pooling is what decoder-style classifiers use.

Read the numbers, not one accuracy.

Report precision, recall, F1 and the confusion matrix on a held-out split with a known balance. The lesson splits 80/20 into 640 training and 160 test messages, stratified so that the test set keeps its balance.

On 80,000 examples and a larger body, the body has enough signal to move without overfitting, and a domain shift can justify the cost. A middle path unfreezes only the last block.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Train a fresh classifier from scratch, the model is small anyway." The pretrained body already encodes word identity, position and co-occurrence; a new model from 800 examples throws that away.
  • "Updating every weight is always more accurate, so do it." On 800 examples it lands within a couple of points of the frozen run and costs about three times the training time, with a real risk of overfitting.
  • "Report accuracy, the classes are balanced." Precision and recall tell you which error you make, and the confusion matrix shows the counts behind the score.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 1) What does requires_grad=False on the body do to the optimizer? Expected: the optimizer receives only the head parameters, so the body weights never change.
  • (level 2) Why mean pooling and not the last hidden state? Expected: mean pooling with the mask is stable across lengths and needs no special token; last-token pooling suits decoder models that saw that pattern.
  • (level 2) Why keep the test split stratified? Expected: a known class balance lets you read precision and recall as honest numbers.
  • (level 3) The full update scores lower than the frozen run on this data. What happened? Expected: the body overfit the small set and lost pretrained structure; lower the body learning rate, unfreeze fewer blocks, or stay with the head.

Weigh a trade-offYou have a small pretrained language model and 800 labeled text messages, half spam and half not. Design the classifier: what you keep, what you replace, which of two training regimes you choose, how you split and evaluate, and when the other regime would win.

Level 2 of 4 · about 12 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Keep the pretrained body and replace the vocabulary head with a linear layer to two classes over a mean-pooled, mask-weighted sequence vector. With 800 examples, train the head only with the body frozen: it is fast, light on memory and rarely overfits, and on this fixture it reaches about 0.9 precision and 0.85 recall after twenty epochs. Full fine-tuning takes about three times longer and lands within a couple of points either way; it starts to pay off at the scale of 80,000 examples or when the domain drifts far from the pretraining text.

What to keep and replace.

The body already encodes word identity, position and co-occurrence. Training a fresh classifier from scratch on 800 examples throws that away. The head is the wrong part: it projects to the vocabulary. Replace it with one linear layer from the hidden width to two logits over a pooled vector, and train with cross-entropy against the label.

Pooling.

A classifier needs one vector per message. Mean pooling weighted by the attention mask is the simplest stable choice across lengths and needs no special token. The alternatives are a leading class token, which the body would have to be pretrained with, and the last non-padding token, which decoder-style classifiers use.

Two regimes, one loop.

Regime Trainable set Cost Risk
Head only The linear head Fast, almost free in memory Underfits when the domain is far from pretraining
Full fine-tuning Head and body About three times longer Overfits and forgets on small data

Both run through the same training function; the toggle flips requires_grad on the body parameters, so the optimizer sees only the head in the first regime.

Data and evaluation.

Split 80/20, stratified so the test set keeps the 50/50 balance: 640 train, 160 test. Report precision, recall and F1 for the spam class with the confusion matrix, so the numbers can be read as honest estimates. The fixture is balanced and generated, so real data will be noisier.

The decision.

On 800 examples and a tiny body, head only is the right call. On 80,000 examples and a bigger body, full fine-tuning starts to win. A middle path unfreezes only the last block, which costs less than full tuning and learns more than the head alone. The contract is the API, not the winner: one training function, one toggle.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Train a small classifier from scratch; the model is tiny anyway." That wastes the pretraining and 800 examples are too few to learn the language from zero.
  • "Always fine-tune everything for the best accuracy." On small data full tuning overfits and forgets, for a result within a couple of points of the head alone at three times the cost.
  • "Use the last token's hidden state without a mask." Padded sequences then pool a padding position; the mask must select the last real token or weight the mean.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why does the frozen body rarely overfit here? Expected: only the head's few parameters move, so the model cannot memorize 640 examples through the body.
  • (level 2) Why stratify the split? Expected: so the test set keeps the class balance and precision and recall mean what they say.
  • (level 3) Precision matters more than recall for this product. What changes in the design? Expected: the decision threshold on the spam logit, chosen on a validation split held out from the 640 training messages to trade recall for precision, so the 160 test messages stay untouched for the final numbers; the training regime stays the same.
  • (level 3) The messages are in a domain the body never saw. Which regime and why? Expected: full or partial fine-tuning, because the frozen features do not carry the domain and the head alone underfits.
