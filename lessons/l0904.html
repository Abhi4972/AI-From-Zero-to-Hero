Why this came about

Until about 2013, neural networks were used to recognise: a label for an image, a score for a sentence. Generating a new, realistic image seemed much harder, because there is no single right answer to compare with. Three ideas changed that within a few years. The variational autoencoder (Kingma and Welling, 2013) learned a compact space of codes from which new images could be decoded. The generative adversarial network (Goodfellow and colleagues, 2014) trained a generator against a critic and produced the first truly sharp synthetic faces. Diffusion models (proposed in 2015, made to work well in 2020) learned to turn noise into data by removing it in small steps, and by 2021 they produced better images than GANs and were far easier to train. In 2022, latent diffusion ran that process inside a VAE's compressed space, which made it cheap enough to release publicly as Stable Diffusion. Almost every image and video generator since is a descendant of that design.

In plain words first
Three ways to make something new

Suppose you want a machine that draws new handwritten digits. One way: teach it to squeeze each digit into a short description and to redraw the digit from the description; then hand it made-up descriptions (autoencoder). A second way: train a forger and a detective together; the forger improves until the detective cannot tell its digits from real ones (GAN). A third way: take real digits, smudge them with more and more random noise, and train a restorer to remove a little noise at a time; then give the restorer pure noise and let it "restore" a digit that never existed (diffusion). Modern generators combine the first and third: the restorer works on the short descriptions, and a text prompt tells it what to restore towards.

Step 1 · The data, and a judge

The examples use the same 8 × 8 handwritten digits as Lesson 3.21, drawn as characters so that you can look at what each model produces. To put a number on quality, train a simple classifier (Lesson 2.6) on the real digits and use it as a judge: a generated image counts as convincing if the judge is at least 90% sure which digit it is.

import numpy as np, torch, torch.nn as nn
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

torch.manual_seed(0); np.random.seed(0)
digits = load_digits()
X = torch.tensor(digits.data / 16.0, dtype=torch.float32)        # 1,797 images, each 64 numbers between 0 and 1
y = torch.tensor(digits.target)

def show(images, title=""):
    """Print up to eight 8 x 8 images side by side as characters."""
    if title: print(title)
    images = [im.reshape(8, 8) for im in images[:8]]
    for r in range(8):
        print("   ".join("".join(" .:-=+*#%@"[int(max(0.0, min(1.0, float(v))) * 9)] for v in im[r]) for im in images))

judge = LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000).fit(digits.data / 16.0, digits.target)    # a simple classifier, used to judge generated digits

def convincing(images):
    """Share of images that the judge recognises as some digit with at least 90% confidence."""
    return float((judge.predict_proba(images.numpy()).max(1) > 0.9).mean())

show(X[:8], "eight real digits:")

Step 2 · An autoencoder: compress, then rebuild

An autoencoder is two networks trained together. The encoder squeezes the 64 pixel values into a few numbers, called the latent code. The decoder rebuilds the image from that code. The only training signal is "make the output equal the input". Because everything must pass through 8 numbers, the network is forced to find a compact description of what digits look like.

class AutoEncoder(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, latent=8):
        super().__init__()
        self.encoder = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(64, 128), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(128, latent))     # 64 numbers -> 8 numbers
        self.decoder = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(latent, 128), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(128, 64), nn.Sigmoid())   # 8 numbers -> 64 numbers
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.decoder(self.encoder(x))

ae = AutoEncoder()
opt = torch.optim.Adam(ae.parameters(), lr=3e-3)
for epoch in range(600):
    loss = ((ae(X) - X) ** 2).mean()                      # how different is the rebuilt image from the original?
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
print(f"reconstruction error after training: {loss.item():.4f}")
with torch.no_grad():
    show(X[:8], "originals:")
    show(ae(X[:8]), "rebuilt from just 8 numbers each:")
    print("\nthe 8 numbers for the first image:", [round(v, 1) for v in ae.encoder(X[:1])[0].tolist()])
    made = ae.decoder(torch.randn(500, 8))                # decode 500 random points
    show(made, "decoding random points:")
    print(f"convincing digits among 500 random decodes: {convincing(made):.0%}")

Rebuilding works well: eight numbers are enough to redraw each digit recognisably. So the decoder knows how to draw digits. Step 3 · A variational autoencoder: tidy the space

A variational autoencoder (VAE) makes two changes so that the latent space can be sampled:

  • The encoder outputs not a point but a small cloud for each image (a mean and a spread per dimension), and the decoder is trained on points drawn from that cloud. Neighbouring points must therefore decode to similar images, which fills the gaps.
  • An extra loss term, the KL divergence, pulls every cloud towards a standard bell curve centred on zero. All the codes are pushed into one compact, overlapping region with a known shape.
class VAE(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, latent=8):
        super().__init__()
        self.body = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(64, 128), nn.ReLU())
        self.mean, self.log_var = nn.Linear(128, latent), nn.Linear(128, latent)      # the encoder outputs a small cloud, not a point
        self.decoder = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(latent, 128), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(128, 64), nn.Sigmoid())
    def encode(self, x):
        h = self.body(x)
        return self.mean(h), self.log_var(h)
    def forward(self, x):
        mean, log_var = self.encode(x)
        z = mean + torch.exp(0.5 * log_var) * torch.randn_like(mean)                  # sample a point from the cloud
        return self.decoder(z), mean, log_var

torch.manual_seed(0)
vae = VAE()
opt = torch.optim.Adam(vae.parameters(), lr=3e-3)
for epoch in range(1000):
    rebuilt, mean, log_var = vae(X)
    reconstruction = ((rebuilt - X) ** 2).sum(1).mean()
    kl = (-0.5 * (1 + log_var - mean ** 2 - log_var.exp()).sum(1)).mean()             # pull every cloud towards a standard bell curve
    loss = reconstruction + 0.25 * kl
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
print(f"reconstruction {reconstruction.item():.2f}   KL {kl.item():.2f}")
with torch.no_grad():
    made = vae.decoder(torch.randn(500, 8))                                           # decode 500 random points
    show(made, "decoding random points:")
    print(f"convincing digits among 500 random decodes: {convincing(made):.0%}")
    print("how many of each digit 0-9:", np.bincount(judge.predict(made.numpy()), minlength=10).tolist())

The same decoder architecture, the same 8 numbers, and now random points decode to recognisable digits several times as often, across all ten classes. The cost is visible too: the samples are soft and blurry. A VAE is trained to produce an image that is good on average for a whole cloud of codes, and the average of several plausible digits is a blurry digit.

Reading this output
Reading the VAE code
  • mean + torch.exp(0.5 * log_var) * torch.randn_like(mean) draws a random point from the cloud in a way that gradients can flow through. This is the reparameterisation trick.
  • reconstruction is the rebuilding error. kl measures how far each cloud is from a standard bell curve. The weight 0.25 balances them: too low and the space has gaps again; too high and the model ignores its input and every image decodes to the same blur (posterior collapse).
  • To generate, you no longer need the encoder at all: draw torch.randn(8) and decode.

Step 4 · A GAN: a forger against a detective

A generative adversarial network has no encoder. The generator turns random numbers directly into an image. The discriminator sees a mix of real and generated images and outputs one number: real or fake. They are trained in turns. The discriminator is rewarded for telling them apart; the generator is rewarded when the discriminator is fooled.

torch.manual_seed(0)
G = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(16, 128), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(128, 64), nn.Sigmoid())          # generator: 16 random numbers -> an image
D = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(64, 128), nn.LeakyReLU(0.2), nn.Linear(128, 1))                 # discriminator: an image -> real or fake?
opt_g = torch.optim.Adam(G.parameters(), lr=2e-3, betas=(0.5, 0.999))
opt_d = torch.optim.Adam(D.parameters(), lr=2e-3, betas=(0.5, 0.999))
bce = nn.BCEWithLogitsLoss()
for step in range(1, 3001):
    real = X[torch.randint(0, len(X), (64,))]
    fake = G(torch.randn(64, 16))
    d_loss = bce(D(real), torch.ones(64, 1)) + bce(D(fake.detach()), torch.zeros(64, 1))     # D: call real "1" and fake "0"
    opt_d.zero_grad(); d_loss.backward(); opt_d.step()
    g_loss = bce(D(fake), torch.ones(64, 1))                                                 # G: make D say "1" for fakes
    opt_g.zero_grad(); g_loss.backward(); opt_g.step()
    if step % 1000 == 0:
        print(f"step {step}: discriminator loss {d_loss.item():.2f}, generator loss {g_loss.item():.2f}")
with torch.no_grad():
    made = G(torch.randn(500, 16))
    show(made, "eight digits from the generator:")
    print(f"convincing digits among 500 generated: {convincing(made):.0%}")
    print("how many of each digit 0-9:", np.bincount(judge.predict(made.numpy()), minlength=10).tolist())

The strokes are sharp and high-contrast, not blurry: the generator is never asked to average anything, only to be convincing. But many samples are malformed, and the two losses did not go down. They hover, because every improvement by one network is a setback for the other. That is normal for a GAN, and it is why they are hard to train: there is no single number that says training is going well.

Reading this output
Why GANs gave way to diffusion

GANs produced the first photorealistic faces (StyleGAN, 2018) and are still used where speed matters, because they generate in one step. Three problems limited them. Training is unstable: small changes in settings make it diverge. They suffer mode collapse: the generator finds a few outputs that fool the discriminator and stops producing the rest of the variety. And steering them with text never worked well. Diffusion models train with a plain, stable regression loss, cover the variety of the data, and accept conditions naturally.

Step 5 · Diffusion in the latent space

Lesson 10.34 trained a diffusion model on points in a plane. The same recipe works on anything that is a list of numbers, including the VAE's 8-number codes. That is latent diffusion:

  1. Encode every training image to its latent code with the VAE (done once).
  2. Take a code, add a random amount of noise, and train a denoiser network to predict the noise that was added.
  3. Tell the denoiser which digit the code belongs to, so that it learns to denoise towards a requested digit. In a text-to-image model this condition is the encoded prompt.
torch.manual_seed(0)
with torch.no_grad():
    latents = vae.encode(X)[0]                                   # every training image as 8 numbers
scale = latents.std()
latents = latents / scale
T = 200
betas = torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.05, T)
alphas_bar = torch.cumprod(1 - betas, 0)                         # how much of the original survives after t noising steps

class Denoiser(nn.Module):
    """Given a noisy latent, the step number and (optionally) the wanted digit, predict the noise that was added."""
    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__()
        self.label = nn.Embedding(11, 32)                        # labels 0-9, plus 10 meaning "no label given"
        self.net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(8 + 1 + 32, 256), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(256, 256), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(256, 8))
    def forward(self, z, t, label):
        return self.net(torch.cat([z, t.float().unsqueeze(1) / T, self.label(label)], dim=1))

denoiser = Denoiser()
opt = torch.optim.Adam(denoiser.parameters(), lr=2e-3)
for step in range(1, 4001):
    idx = torch.randint(0, len(latents), (256,))
    z0, labels = latents[idx], y[idx].clone()
    labels[torch.rand(256) < 0.15] = 10                          # sometimes hide the label, so the model also learns to work without it
    t = torch.randint(0, T, (256,))
    noise = torch.randn_like(z0)
    a = alphas_bar[t].unsqueeze(1)
    zt = a.sqrt() * z0 + (1 - a).sqrt() * noise                  # the latent after t steps of noise
    loss = ((denoiser(zt, t, labels) - noise) ** 2).mean()
    opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    if step % 1000 == 0:
        print(f"step {step}: loss {loss.item():.3f}")

The loss settles around 0.25 and stays there. Unlike a GAN's losses, this is a plain prediction error, and it cannot reach zero: when a code is almost entirely noise, the exact noise cannot be recovered. What matters is how the model generates.

Reading this output
Reading the training loop
  • alphas_bar[t] says how much of the original code is left after t noising steps. zt is the noisy code: a mix of the original and fresh noise.
  • The denoiser sees the noisy code, the step number t and the label, and predicts noise. The loss is the squared difference. That is the whole training objective of a diffusion model.
  • For 15% of the examples the label is replaced by a special "no label" value. The model therefore learns both to denoise towards a specific digit and to denoise with no instruction. The next step uses both.

Step 6 · Generate, and steer with guidance

To generate, start from pure noise in the latent space and repeatedly remove the predicted noise. At each step, ask the denoiser twice: once with the wanted digit and once with no label. The difference between the two answers is the direction that makes the result "more like a 3". Classifier-free guidance pushes further along that direction by a chosen factor:

def generate(digit, n=100, guidance=2.0, seed=0):
    """Start from noise in the latent space, denoise step by step towards `digit`, then decode to pixels."""
    torch.manual_seed(seed)
    z = torch.randn(n, 8)
    want, none = torch.full((n,), digit), torch.full((n,), 10)
    with torch.no_grad():
        for t in reversed(range(T)):
            tt = torch.full((n,), t)
            with_label, without = denoiser(z, tt, want), denoiser(z, tt, none)
            noise_pred = without + guidance * (with_label - without)            # classifier-free guidance
            z = (z - betas[t] / (1 - alphas_bar[t]).sqrt() * noise_pred) / (1 - betas[t]).sqrt()
            if t > 0:
                z = z + betas[t].sqrt() * torch.randn_like(z)
        return vae.decoder(z * scale)

for digit in (3, 7):
    show(generate(digit), f"asked for a {digit}:")
print()
print(f"{'guidance':>9}{'is the digit asked for':>24}{'convincing':>12}{'variety':>9}")

These digits were generated from noise, in the VAE's latent space, steered by a label, and decoded to pixels. Read the table from the top:

  • Guidance 0 ignores the label. The digits are random (right 10% of the time, which is chance), often unclear, and very varied.
  • Guidance 1 uses the label plainly. Almost every sample is the requested digit.
  • Guidance 2 to 4 exaggerates the label's direction. Every sample is the requested digit and more of them are convincing than the real handwriting was. But the variety column has halved: the samples of each digit now look alike, as the rows of 3s and 7s above show.
  • Guidance 8 pushes too far, and quality falls again.

This is the trade-off behind the "guidance scale" or "CFG" setting in every image generator: low values give variety and loose prompt-following, high values give faithful but samey and eventually distorted images.

Ideas built on this design that you will meet in products:

  • Image-to-image and inpainting: start from a partly noised version of an existing image, or denoise only a masked region.
  • ControlNet and adapters: an extra network feeds a pose, an edge map or a depth map into the denoiser, so that composition can be controlled exactly.
  • LoRAlater · 3.24 for images: the low-rank adapters of Lesson 3.24, trained on a few dozen pictures to teach a style, a product or a face.
  • Video: the latent gains a time dimension, and the denoiser attends across frames as well as within them. Compression matters even more.
  • Audio and music: the same recipe on compressed audio latents.
  • Autoregressive image generation: the alternative family. An image is turned into a sequence of tokens and a transformer predicts them one after another, as with text. Multimodal language models that generate images natively work this way, sometimes with a small diffusion decoder at the end.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "A generative model stores its training images and stitches them together."
    ✓ It learns a function from random numbers to data. The digits above came from 8 random numbers each. (Very large models can still memorise specific training images that were repeated many times, which is a real legal and privacy concern.)
  • ✗ "Diffusion works on the pixels of the final image."
    ✓ Most systems denoise a small latent and decode to pixels once at the end. That is what made them affordable.
  • ✗ "A higher guidance scale always gives a better image."
    ✓ It trades variety for faithfulness, and too much distorts the result.
  • ✗ "GANs are obsolete."
    ✓ They are less used for open-ended generation, but still valued for one-step speed, and adversarial losses are used inside VAEs and distilled diffusion models.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Negative guidance

The update moves away from "3": the samples are digits, but almost never a 3. This is the mechanism behind negative prompts in image generators: the "without" branch is replaced by a prompt describing what you do not want, and guidance pushes away from it.

SolutionM1 · The KL weight

With weight 2.0 the KL term wins: KL falls to nearly zero, the reconstruction error rises, and every random point decodes to much the same grey blur. The encoder has stopped passing information (posterior collapse). With 0.01 reconstruction is excellent but the KL value is large: the codes have spread out and left gaps, so random decodes are again mostly unconvincing, as with the plain autoencoder. A usable VAE sits between the two.

SolutionX1 · Style, placement, volume

Style: a LoRA trained on a few dozen brand images, applied to a base model. Placement: ControlNet (an edge or depth map of the layout), or inpainting the product into a fixed scene. Volume: a distilled few-step model, which cuts the cost of each image by a factor of ten or more. Lesson 4.13 adds the questions this lesson leaves out: rights to the training data and outputs, labelling of generated media, and review before publication.

Recap
  • An autoencoder compresses data to a short latent code and rebuilds it. A VAE also organises the latent space so that random codes decode to plausible data, at the price of blur.
  • A GAN trains a generator against a discriminator: sharp results, unstable training, limited variety.
  • A diffusion model is trained to predict the noise added to data, and generates by removing noise step by step. Running it in a VAE's latent space makes it cheap: latent diffusion.
  • A condition (a label here, a text embedding in practice) steers generation. Classifier-free guidance amplifies the condition, trading variety for faithfulness.
  • Real image and video generators are this design at scale, with better samplers, distillation, ControlNet, LoRA and, increasingly, transformers and flow matching.

Check your understanding

Q1Why do random codes decode badly with a plain autoencoder but much better with a VAE?

The KL term and the sampling during training organise the latent space so that random points land where the decoder has learned to draw.

Q2What does a diffusion model's denoiser learn to predict?

Training adds a known amount of noise and asks the network to recover it; generation runs that in reverse from pure noise.

Q3You raise the guidance scale of an image generator from 3 to 12. What should you expect?

Guidance amplifies the condition's direction: more faithfulness, less variety, and eventually artefacts.
