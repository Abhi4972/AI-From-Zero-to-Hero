In plain words first
The idea in one line

Ho, Jain, Abbeel (2020) gave the field a recipe it could not quit. Destroy the data with noise over a thousand small steps. Train one neural net to predict the noise. Reverse the process at inference. Today every mainstream image, video, 3D, and music model runs on this loop, possibly with flow matching or consistency tricks on top.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
Forward process "Adding noise" Fixed Markov chain $q(x_t \mid x_{t-1})$ that destroys the data.
Reverse process "Denoising" Learned chain $p_\theta(x_{t-1} \mid x_t)$ that reconstructs the data.
β schedule "The noise ladder" Per-step variance; linear, cosine, or sigmoid.
α̅ "Alpha bar" Cumulative product $\prod (1 - \beta)$; gives closed-form $x_t$ from $x_0$.
Simple loss "MSE on noise" $\lVert \epsilon - \epsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \rVert^2$; all variational derivations collapse to this.
ε-prediction "Predict noise" Output is the noise added; standard DDPM.
V-prediction "Predict velocity" Output is $\alpha \cdot \epsilon - \sigma \cdot x$; better conditioning across t.
DDPM "The paper" Ho et al. 2020; linear β, 1000 steps, U-Net.
DDIM "Deterministic sampler" Non-Markov sampler, 20-50 steps, same training objective.
Classifier-free guidance "CFG" Mix conditional and unconditional noise predictions to amplify conditioning.

The problem

In plain words first

A diffusion model learns to generate by learning to clean. Take a training image and add a little random noise, then a little more, over many steps, until nothing but noise is left; this direction needs no learning, just a formula. Then train a network to look at a noisy image and estimate the noise in it. To generate, start from pure noise and repeatedly subtract the network's noise estimate, a small step at a time, until an image appears. The training loss is a plain squared error, which makes diffusion far more stable to train than a GAN; the price is that generating takes many network passes. This lesson checks the noising formula against step-by-step noising, runs the lesson's reverse sampler with a noise predictor worked out exactly instead of trained (and finds a bug in the lesson's settings), then runs the original 2020 DDPM model to see what it believes at each noise level and how an image emerges. Nothing is trained.

You want a sampler for pdata​(x). GANs play a minimax game that often diverges. VAEs produce blurry samples from a Gaussian decoder. What you really want is a training objective that is (a) a single stable loss (no saddle point, no minimax), (b) a lower bound on logp(x) (so you have likelihoods), and (c) samples that match SOTA quality.

Sohl-Dickstein et al. (2015) had a theoretical answer: define a Markov chain q(xt​∣xt−1​) that gradually adds Gaussian noise, and train a reverse chain pθ​(xt−1​∣xt​) to denoise. Ho, Jain, Abbeel (2020) showed the loss could be simplified to one line — predict the noise — and cleaned up the math. In 2020 this was a curiosity. In 2021 it produced state-of-the-art samples. In 2022 it became Stable Diffusion. In 2026 it is the substrate.

The idea

Forward process q. Add Gaussian noise in T small steps. The closed form — the reason the math is tractable — is that the cumulative step is also Gaussian:

q(xt​∣x0​)=N(αˉt​​⋅x0​,(1−αˉt​)⋅I)

where αˉt​=∏s=1t​(1−βs​) for a schedule of βt​. Pick βt​ from 1e-4 to 0.02 linearly over T=1000 steps and xT​ is approximately N(0,I).

Reverse process pθ​. Learn a neural net ϵθ​(xt​,t) that predicts the noise that was added. Given xt​, denoise by:

xt−1​=αt​​1​⋅(xt​−1−αˉt​​βt​​⋅ϵθ​(xt​,t))+σt​⋅z

where σt​ is either βt​​ or a learned variance. The expression is ugly but it is just algebra — solving for xt−1​ given the posterior q(xt−1​∣xt​,x0​) and substituting x0​ with its noise-predicted estimate.

Training loss.

Lsimple​=Ex0​,t,ϵ​[​ϵ−ϵθ​(αˉt​​⋅x0​+1−αˉt​​⋅ϵ,t)​2]

Sample x0​ from data, pick a random t, sample ϵ∼N(0,I), compute the noisy xt​ in one shot via the closed form, and regress on the noise. One loss, no minimax, no KL, no reparameterization tricks.

Sampling. Start xT​∼N(0,I). Iterate the reverse step from t=T to 1. Done.

Why it works

Three intuitions:

  1. Denoising is easy; generating is hard. At t=T, the data is pure noise — the net has to solve a trivial problem. At t=0, the net only has to clean up a few pixels. At intermediate t, the problem is hard but the net has many gradients flowing through the same weights from every noise level.

  2. Score matching in disguise. Vincent (2011) proved that predicting the noise is equivalent to estimating ∇x​logq(xt​∣x0​), the score. The reverse SDE uses this score to walk up the density gradient — a guided random walk toward high-probability regions.

  3. The ELBO reduces to simple MSE. The full variational lower bound has a KL term per timestep. With DDPM's parameterization those KL terms simplify to MSE on noise prediction with specific coefficients; Ho dropped the coefficients (calling it "simple" loss) and quality improved.

The same idea as a formula

Forward process. q(xt​∣xt−1​)=N(1−βt​​xt−1​,βt​I). Composing t Gaussian steps gives one Gaussian:

q(xt​∣x0​)=N(αˉt​​x0​,(1−αˉt​)I),αˉt​=s=1∏t​(1−βs​)

so xt​=αˉt​​x0​+1−αˉt​​ε in one shot. The signal-to-noise ratio is SNR(t)=αˉt​/(1−αˉt​).

The reverse step's target. Given x0​, the previous step is Gaussian too:

q(xt−1​∣xt​,x0​)=N(μ~​t​,β~​t​I),μ~​t​=αt​​1​(xt​−1−αˉt​​βt​​ε),β~​t​=1−αˉt​1−αˉt−1​​βt​

The model replaces the unknown ε with εθ​(xt​,t), which gives the reverse update in the idea above.

What the best noise predictor is. The squared error E∥ε−εθ​(xt​,t)∥2 is minimised by the conditional mean, so

ε∗(xt​,t)=E[ε∣xt​]=1−αˉt​​xt​−αˉt​​E[x0​∣xt​]​,x^0​=αˉt​​xt​−1−αˉt​​εθ​​

A trained network approximates E[x0​∣xt​], the average clean image consistent with what it sees.

Score. Tweedie's formula links this to the score of the noisy distribution: ∇xt​​logpt​(xt​)=−ε∗(xt​,t)/1−αˉt​​. Predicting noise is estimating the score.

The ELBO is a sum of KL terms, one per step, each equal to a weighted ∥ε−εθ​∥2; Lsimple​ drops the weights.

Worked example
The schedule by hand

Linear β from 10−4 to 0.02 over T=1,000 steps:

  • ∑t​βt​≈1,000×0.01=10, and αˉT​=∏(1−βt​)≈e−10=4.5×10−5: the signal is scaled by αˉT​​≈0.007. xT​ is essentially pure noise, so starting the sampler from N(0,I) is correct.
  • The same β range over T=40 steps: ∑βt​≈40×0.01=0.4, αˉT​≈e−0.4=0.67, and the signal is still scaled by 0.67​=0.82. A sampler that starts from N(0,I) starts from the wrong distribution.

One noising step by hand. x0​=2, t with αˉt​=0.5, ε=−1: xt​=0.5​×2+0.5​×(−1)=1.414−0.707=0.707. A perfect predictor returning ε=−1 recovers x^0​=(0.707+0.707)/0.707=2.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Noising must be simulated step by step during training."
    ✓ The closed form jumps straight to any t; training picks a random t per example (the run after Step 4 checks the closed form against 1,000 real steps).
  • ✗ "The network predicts the clean image directly from pure noise."
    ✓ At high noise its best guess is a blurry average of plausible images; detail is added gradually as the noise falls (the runs under "Use it").
  • ✗ "More steps always help, whatever the schedule."
    ✓ The schedule must actually reach pure noise at T; with too few steps for its β range, sampling starts from the wrong distribution and stays biased.

Step 1 · the forward schedule (closed form)

code/main.py implements a 1-D DDPM. Data is a two-mode mixture. The "net" is a tiny MLP that takes (xt​,t) and outputs predicted noise. Training is the one-line loss. Sampling iterates the reverse chain.

T = 1000
betas = [1e-4 + (0.02 - 1e-4) * t / (T - 1) for t in range(T)]
alphas = [1 - b for b in betas]
alpha_bars = []
cum = 1.0
for a in alphas:
    cum *= a
    alpha_bars.append(cum)
print(f"alpha_bar after 1, 100, 500, 1000 steps: {alpha_bars[0]:.4f}, {alpha_bars[99]:.4f}, {alpha_bars[499]:.4f}, {alpha_bars[999]:.6f}")

Step 2 · sample x_t in one shot

def forward_sample(x0, t, alpha_bars, rng):
    a_bar = alpha_bars[t]
    eps = rng.gauss(0, 1)
    x_t = math.sqrt(a_bar) * x0 + math.sqrt(1 - a_bar) * eps
    return x_t, eps

Step 3 · one training step

def train_step(x0, model, alpha_bars, rng):
    t = rng.randrange(T)
    x_t, eps = forward_sample(x0, t, alpha_bars, rng)
    eps_hat = model_forward(model, x_t, t)
    loss = (eps - eps_hat) ** 2
    return loss, gradient_step(model, ...)

Step 4 · reverse sampling

def sample(model, alpha_bars, T, rng):
    x = rng.gauss(0, 1)
    for t in range(T - 1, -1, -1):
        eps_hat = model_forward(model, x, t)
        beta_t = 1 - alphas[t]
        x = (x - beta_t / math.sqrt(1 - alpha_bars[t]) * eps_hat) / math.sqrt(alphas[t])
        if t > 0:
            x += math.sqrt(beta_t) * rng.gauss(0, 1)
    return x

For a 1-D problem, main.py trains a 24-unit MLP with 40 timesteps on 4,000 samples. Its 40-step schedule keeps the β range meant for 1,000 steps, so the last noisy sample is far from pure noise; the run after this step shows what that costs.

The lesson's chain, with an exact noise predictor

main.py's schedule and histogram (its main() trains a network, which this page does not run). First, the closed form against 1,000 actual noising steps on the lesson's two-mode data. Then, because the data is a known Gaussian mixture, the best possible noise predictor can be computed exactly with Bayes' rule; Step 4's reverse chain runs with it for T=1,000 and for the lesson's T=40.

import math, random, runpy, numpy as np
m = runpy.run_path("main.py")            # the lesson's schedule and histogram; main() is not run, because it trains

# 1. The closed form q(x_t | x_0) against 1,000 actual noising steps.
betas, alphas, abar = m["make_schedule"](1000)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x0 = np.where(rng.random(20_000) < 0.5, rng.normal(-2, 0.4, 20_000), rng.normal(2, 0.4, 20_000))
x = x0.copy()
for t in range(1000):
    x = math.sqrt(1 - betas[t]) * x + math.sqrt(betas[t]) * rng.normal(size=x.size)
    if t in (99, 499, 999):
        pred = np.sqrt(abar[t]) * 2.0                               # closed-form mean for the +2 mode
        print(f"t = {t + 1:4d}: alpha_bar {abar[t]:.5f} | +2 mode after stepping: mean {x[x0 > 0].mean():.3f}, "
              f"closed form {pred:.3f} | overall std {x.std():.3f}, closed form "
              f"{math.sqrt(abar[t] * x0.var() + 1 - abar[t]):.3f}")

# 2. The exact noise predictor for this data, worked out by Bayes' rule instead of trained.
def eps_star(xt, ab):
    v = ab * 0.4 ** 2 + 1 - ab                                       # variance of x_t given the mode
    r = np.stack([np.exp(-(xt - math.sqrt(ab) * mu) ** 2 / (2 * v)) for mu in (-2, 2)])
    r = r / r.sum(0)                                                 # which mode x_t probably came from
    e_x0 = sum(r[k] * (mu + math.sqrt(ab) * 0.4 ** 2 / v * (xt - math.sqrt(ab) * mu)) for k, mu in enumerate((-2, 2)))
    return (xt - math.sqrt(ab) * e_x0) / math.sqrt(1 - ab)           # epsilon = (x_t - sqrt(ab) E[x_0 | x_t]) / sqrt(1 - ab)

def reverse(T, n=2000, seed=1):                                       # Step 4's reverse chain, vectorised
    _, al, ab = m["make_schedule"](T)
    g = np.random.default_rng(seed); x = g.normal(size=n)
    for t in range(T - 1, -1, -1):
        b = 1 - al[t]
        x = (x - b / math.sqrt(1 - ab[t]) * eps_star(x, ab[t])) / math.sqrt(al[t])
        if t > 0: x = x + math.sqrt(b) * g.normal(size=n)
    return x
for T in (1000, 40):
    s = reverse(T)
    print(f"\nreverse chain with the exact noise predictor, T = {T} (alpha_bar_T = {m['make_schedule'](T)[2][-1]:.5f}):")
    print(m["histogram"](s.tolist()))
    print(f"mode means {s[s < 0].mean():+.2f} / {s[s > 0].mean():+.2f} (data: -2 / +2), "
          f"mode stds {s[s < 0].std():.2f} / {s[s > 0].std():.2f} (data: 0.40), share above 0: {(s > 0).mean():.2f}")

Reading this output

Stepping 20,000 points through 1,000 real noising steps lands where the closed form says: after 100 steps the +2 mode has moved to 1.892 against a predicted 1.894, and after 1,000 the spread is 0.997 against 1.000 and the data is gone. The exact noise predictor needs no training: by Bayes' rule it estimates which mode xt​ came from and what clean value it most likely had. With T=1,000, Step 4's reverse chain turns 2,000 standard-normal draws into the data distribution exactly, modes at −1.99 and +2.02 with spread 0.40, so the sampling formula is right. With the lesson's T=40 and the same β range, αˉT​ is 0.67, not 0: real noisy samples at step 40 still hold most of their signal, but the chain starts from N(0,I), which carries none. Even with the perfect predictor, the modes come out at ±1.74 instead of ±2. A trained network cannot do better than the exact one, so main.py's samples inherit this bias; with 40 steps, β must be raised (for example to 0.2 at the end) or a cosine schedule used.

Time conditioning

The net needs to know which timestep it is denoising. Two standard options:

  • Sinusoidal embedding. Like Transformer positional encoding. embed(t)=[sin(t/ω0​),cos(t/ω0​),sin(t/ω1​),…]. Pass through an MLP, broadcast into the net.
  • Film / group-norm conditioning. Project embedding to per-channel scale/bias (FiLM) at each block.

Our toy code uses sinusoidal → concat. Production U-Nets use FiLM.

Pitfalls

  • Schedule matters a lot. Linear β is the DDPM default but cosine schedule (Nichol & Dhariwal, 2021) gives better FID for the same compute. Switch schedules if quality plateaus.
  • Timestep embedding is fragile. Passing raw t as a float works for toy 1-D but fails for images; always use a proper embedding.
  • V-prediction vs ε-prediction. For narrow regimes (very small or very large t), ϵ has poor signal-to-noise. V-prediction (v=α⋅ϵ−σ⋅x) is more stable; Stable Diffusion 2.x (768-pixel models) uses it. SDXL predicts the noise, and SD3 and FLUX.1 predict the closely related rectified-flow velocity (Lesson 8.13).
  • Classifier-free guidance. At inference, compute both conditional and unconditional ϵ, then ϵcfg​=(1+w)⋅ϵcond​−w⋅ϵuncond​ with w≈3-7. Covered in Lesson 08.
  • 1000 steps is a lot. Production uses DDIM (20-50 steps), DPM-Solver (10-20 steps), or distillation (1-4 steps). See Lesson 12.

import io, numpy as np, torch, matplotlib.pyplot as plt, pyarrow.parquet as pq
from PIL import Image
from huggingface_hub import hf_hub_download
from diffusers import UNet2DModel, DDPMScheduler

torch.set_num_threads(2); torch.manual_seed(0)
# Google's DDPM U-Net trained on CIFAR-10 (32 x 32), the model from Ho et al. 2020, with its 1,000-step linear schedule.
unet = UNet2DModel.from_pretrained("google/ddpm-cifar10-32").eval()
sched = DDPMScheduler.from_pretrained("google/ddpm-cifar10-32")
abar = sched.alphas_cumprod
print(f"U-Net: {sum(p.numel() for p in unet.parameters()) / 1e6:.1f}M parameters; schedule: {sched.config.num_train_timesteps} steps, "
      f"beta {sched.config.beta_start} to {sched.config.beta_end} ({sched.config.beta_schedule})")
table = pq.read_table(hf_hub_download("Multimodal-Fatima/Imagenette_validation",
                      "data/validation-00000-of-00001-b518380f1876c09a.parquet", repo_type="dataset"), columns=["image"])
def photo(row):
    im = Image.open(io.BytesIO(table.column("image")[row].as_py()["bytes"])).convert("RGB")
    s = min(im.size); im = im.crop(((im.width - s) // 2, (im.height - s) // 2, (im.width + s) // 2, (im.height + s) // 2))
    return torch.tensor(np.asarray(im.resize((32, 32), Image.LANCZOS)), dtype=torch.float32).permute(2, 0, 1) / 127.5 - 1
x0 = torch.stack([photo(r) for r in (100, 1500, 2600, 3500)])       # photos it has never seen, shrunk to 32 x 32

ts = [20, 100, 250, 500, 750, 999]
print("   t   alpha_bar   SNR = abar/(1-abar)   noise-prediction MSE   x0 estimate MSE")
row_noisy, row_x0 = [], []
with torch.no_grad():
    for t in ts:
        eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
        xt = abar[t].sqrt() * x0 + (1 - abar[t]).sqrt() * eps            # one-shot noising (Step 2)
        eps_hat = unet(xt, t).sample
        x0_hat = (xt - (1 - abar[t]).sqrt() * eps_hat) / abar[t].sqrt()  # the network's guess of the clean image
        print(f"{t:4d}   {abar[t]:9.5f}   {abar[t] / (1 - abar[t]):19.4g}"
              f"   {(eps_hat - eps).pow(2).mean():20.3f}   {(x0_hat.clamp(-1, 1) - x0).pow(2).mean():15.3f}")
        row_noisy.append(xt[0]); row_x0.append(x0_hat[0])

show = lambda im: ((im.clamp(-1, 1) + 1) / 2).permute(1, 2, 0).numpy()
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 7, figsize=(9, 2.9))
axes[0, 0].imshow(show(x0[0])); axes[0, 0].set_title("photo", fontsize=8)
for j, t in enumerate(ts):
    axes[0, j + 1].imshow(show(row_noisy[j])); axes[0, j + 1].set_title(f"x_t, t = {t}", fontsize=8)
    axes[1, j + 1].imshow(show(row_x0[j])); axes[1, j + 1].set_title("its x0 guess", fontsize=8)
for a in axes.ravel(): a.axis("off")
plt.subplots_adjust(left=0.01, right=0.99, top=0.9, bottom=0.01, wspace=0.05, hspace=0.25)
plt.savefig("v2.png", dpi=60); plt.show()

Reading this output

Noise-prediction error is largest at low noise (0.22 at t=20) and almost zero at t=999: at high noise the input is nearly all noise, so "the noise is the input" is almost right, while at low noise telling fine detail from noise is hard. The clean-image guess behaves the opposite way: nearly perfect at t=20 and 100, increasingly blurry beyond, and at t=999 its error explodes because the guess divides by αˉt​​=0.006. The pictures show the formula box's conditional mean: at t=250 (signal-to-noise ratio about 1) the model is unsure, and its guess drifts toward what it knows; at 500 and 750 the guess is a smooth, average-looking blob; at 999 nothing of the photo remains. This is why sampling works coarse to fine.

import time, torch, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from diffusers import UNet2DModel, DDIMScheduler

torch.set_num_threads(2)
unet = UNet2DModel.from_pretrained("google/ddpm-cifar10-32").eval()
sched = DDIMScheduler.from_pretrained("google/ddpm-cifar10-32")
sched.set_timesteps(25)                                  # 25 deterministic DDIM steps instead of 1,000
x = torch.randn(6, 3, 32, 32, generator=torch.Generator().manual_seed(7))
snaps = []
t0 = time.perf_counter()
with torch.no_grad():
    for i, t in enumerate(sched.timesteps):
        out = sched.step(unet(x, t).sample, t, x)
        if i % 5 == 0 or i == len(sched.timesteps) - 1:
            snaps.append((int(t), out.pred_original_sample[0].clone()))     # the current guess of the final image
        x = out.prev_sample
dt = time.perf_counter() - t0
print(f"25 steps for 6 images: {dt:.1f} s, {dt / 25:.2f} s per step (one full U-Net pass over all 6 images each step)")
print(f"the original sampler's 1,000 steps would take about {dt / 25 * 1000 / 60:.0f} minutes here")

show = lambda im: ((im.clamp(-1, 1) + 1) / 2).permute(1, 2, 0).numpy()
fig, axes = plt.subplots(2, 6, figsize=(9, 3.2))
for j, (t, im) in enumerate(snaps[:6]):
    axes[0, j].imshow(show(im)); axes[0, j].set_title(f"guess at t = {t}", fontsize=8)
for j in range(6):
    axes[1, j].imshow(show(x[j]))
axes[1, 0].set_title("final samples", loc="left", fontsize=8)
for a in axes.ravel(): a.axis("off")

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Noise in one shot: xt​=αˉt​​x0​+1−αˉt​​ε; the schedule must reach αˉT​≈0.
  • Train with ∥ε−εθ​(xt​,t)∥2 at a random t; the optimum is E[ε∣xt​], equivalently the score.

Practice problem · DDPM noise schedule, closed form noising and posterior

From the source repository: write the function, then press "Run the checks". They run as real Python in your browser (the first run loads Python, about 10 MB) and tell you which part is wrong and why. Hints open one at a time; the reference solution opens when every check passes.

medium30 minresearch

A diffusion model learns to remove noise. To train it, you add a known amount of Gaussian noise to clean data. DDPM fixes this forward process with a schedule of small noise steps. You can jump to any step in closed form, and you can compute the exact posterior of one reverse step when you know the clean data.

Task

Write three functions. Timesteps start at 0: t=0,1,…,T−1.

  1. make_schedule(T, beta_start=1e-4, beta_end=0.02) returns (betas, alphas, alpha_bars).
  2. q_sample(x0, t, alpha_bars, noise) returns a sample of xt​ given x0​.
  3. posterior_mean_variance(x0, x_t, t, betas, alpha_bars) returns the mean and the variance of q(xt−1​∣xt​,x0​).

Schedule. The betas are linear: βt​=βstart​+(βend​−βstart​)⋅t/(T−1). So β0​=βstart​ and βT−1​=βend​. Then αt​=1−βt​ and αˉt​=∏s=0t​αs​.

Closed form sample. With the given noise ϵ:

xt​=αˉt​​x0​+1−αˉt​​ϵ

Posterior. Define αˉt−1​=1 when t=0. Then

μ~​t​=1−αˉt​αˉt−1​​βt​​x0​+1−αˉt​αt​​(1−αˉt−1​)​xt​,β~​t​=1−αˉt​1−αˉt−1​​βt​

Timesteps. t is an int or an int array of shape (B,). When t is an array, item b of the batch is x0[b] and it uses timestep t[b]. The coefficients then broadcast over all the axes after the first.

Input and output

Name Type Shape Meaning
T int the number of steps
beta_start, beta_end float the first and the last beta
x0 float array (B, ...) or any shape when t is an int the clean data
x_t float array same as x0 the noisy data at step t
noise float array same as x0 the Gaussian noise ϵ
t int or int array scalar or (B,) the timestep of each item
betas, alpha_bars float array (T,) the schedule from make_schedule
  • make_schedule returns three float arrays of shape (T,).
  • q_sample returns a float array with the shape of x0.
  • posterior_mean_variance returns a tuple (mean, variance). mean has the shape of x0. variance is a float when t is an int. When t is an array, variance has shape (B, 1, ...) and broadcasts against x0.

Constraints

  • 2 <= T <= 1000 and 0 < beta_start < beta_end < 1.
  • 0 <= t <= T - 1.
  • Do not change the input arrays.
  • Use numpy only.

Your code
Common mistakes the checks catch
  • Does not reshape the coefficients for an array of timesteps, so they do not broadcast over the batch.
  • Uses alpha_bar and 1 - alpha_bar as the coefficients of x_t, without the square roots.
  • Uses alpha_bar_t in place of alpha_bar_{t-1} in the posterior.
  • Builds alpha_bar_{t-1} with np.roll, so t = 0 reads the last step, not 1.
  • Divides by T, not by T - 1, so the last beta is below beta_end.
  • Returns beta_t as the posterior variance, not the tilde beta of the paper.
Hint 1

np.arange(T) / (T - 1) goes from 0 to 1 in equal steps. Scale and shift it to get the betas. np.cumprod gives the running product for αˉ.

Hint 2

For an int t, alpha_bars[t] is a scalar and broadcasts over any shape. For an array t, alpha_bars[t] has shape (B,). Reshape it to (B, 1, ...) with one 1 for each axis of x0 after the first.

Hint 3

The posterior needs αˉt−1​. Build an array of the previous values with a 1 in front: [1, alpha_bars[0], ..., alpha_bars[T-2]]. Then index it with t like the other arrays.

Hint 4

At t=0 the previous value is 1, so the coefficient of xt​ is 0 and the coefficient of x0​ is β0​/(1−αˉ0​)=1. Python reads alpha_bars[-1] as the last step, so a plain t - 1 index gives a wrong answer at t=0.

Reference solution and notes
import numpy as np


def make_schedule(T, beta_start=1e-4, beta_end=0.02):
    betas = beta_start + (beta_end - beta_start) * np.arange(T) / (T - 1)
    alphas = 1.0 - betas
    alpha_bars = np.cumprod(alphas)
    return betas, alphas, alpha_bars


def extract(values, t, ndim):
    out = np.asarray(values)[t]
    if np.ndim(t) == 0:
        return out
    return out.reshape((-1,) + (1,) * (ndim - 1))


def q_sample(x0, t, alpha_bars, noise):
    a_bar = extract(alpha_bars, t, np.ndim(x0))
    return np.sqrt(a_bar) * x0 + np.sqrt(1.0 - a_bar) * noise


def posterior_mean_variance(x0, x_t, t, betas, alpha_bars):
    alpha_bars_prev = np.concatenate([[1.0], alpha_bars[:-1]])
    ndim = np.ndim(x0)
    beta_t = extract(betas, t, ndim)
    a_bar = extract(alpha_bars, t, ndim)
    a_bar_prev = extract(alpha_bars_prev, t, ndim)
    coef_x0 = np.sqrt(a_bar_prev) * beta_t / (1.0 - a_bar)
    coef_xt = np.sqrt(1.0 - beta_t) * (1.0 - a_bar_prev) / (1.0 - a_bar)
    mean = coef_x0 * x0 + coef_xt * x_t
    variance = (1.0 - a_bar_prev) / (1.0 - a_bar) * beta_t
    return mean, variance

Why a closed form exists

Each forward step scales the data by αt​​ and adds Gaussian noise with variance βt​. A sum of independent Gaussians is Gaussian. After t steps the scale is αˉt​​ and the noise variance is 1−αˉt​. The squares of the two coefficients sum to 1, so data with unit variance keeps unit variance. Training uses this to sample any t in one step.

Why the posterior matters

The reverse step q(xt−1​∣xt​) is unknown, but q(xt−1​∣xt​,x0​) is a Gaussian with the mean and the variance of this problem. The model does not know x0​. It predicts the noise ϵ, and the paper substitutes x0​=(xt​−1−αˉt​​ϵ)/αˉt​​. The posterior mean then becomes (xt​−βt​ϵ/1−αˉt​​)/αt​​. That is the update in the sampler of the lesson.

The indexing trap

With timesteps that start at 0, the step before t=0 is the clean data, with αˉ=1. A negative index in numpy reads the last entry, so the code still runs but gives the wrong posterior. The paper counts from 1. Choose one convention and keep it in the schedule, the sampler and the loss.

Where this goes next

The lesson trains a small network to predict ϵ with a mean squared error and runs the reverse chain. DDIM, latent diffusion and classifier-free guidance all reuse this schedule. The flow matching lesson replaces it with a straight line between data and noise.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Something else: a cat

Something else: at t=250 the guess looks like a cat's face, with only the cassette player's pale colours left (the third guess in the figure). With signal and noise at equal power, many clean images are consistent with what the network sees, and its guess is their average under its idea of what images look like, which is CIFAR-10's ten classes. A cassette player is not among them, so the most plausible explanation it finds is a cat. At t=20 and 100 the photo dominates the noise and the guess is accurate. The same mechanism explains how a diffusion model generates: from pure noise, its guesses are pulled toward the training distribution.

SolutionB1 · Too narrow and pulled inward

Without the fresh noise, each step moves x to the mean of the reverse step and nothing replaces the variance the noise was supposed to supply. With the exact noise predictor from the run after Step 4 and T=1,000, the two clusters come out at about ±1.65 with a spread of 0.09, instead of ±2 with 0.4: every sample is drawn toward a "typical" point and diversity collapses. The ancestral sampler needs σt​z to stay on the right distribution. Deterministic sampling is possible, but it needs a different update rule: DDIM's, which is derived so that no noise is required (the run under "Use it").

Production note: diffusion inference is a step-count problem

The DDPM paper runs T=1000 reverse steps. Nobody ships that in production. Every real inference stack picks one of three strategies — and each maps cleanly to production framing of "where is the latency coming from":

  1. Faster sampler, same model. DDIM (20-50 steps), DPM-Solver++ (10-20), UniPC (8-16). Drop-in replacement of the reverse loop; the trained ϵθ​ weights are untouched. Cuts latency 20-50×.
  2. Distillation. Train a student to match the teacher in fewer steps: Progressive Distillation (2 → 1), Consistency Models (arbitrary → 1-4), LCM, SDXL-Turbo, SD3.5 Large Turbo. Cuts latency another 5-10×, requires retraining.
  3. Caching and compilation. torch.compile(unet, mode="reduce-overhead"), TensorRT engines, SDPA/FlashAttention, bf16 or 8-bit weights, and step caching (DeepCache, TeaCache) that reuses features between neighbouring steps. Cuts per-step latency roughly 2×. Stacks with (1) and (2).

For a production diffusion server the budget conversation is the same as production literature describes for LLMs: latency is num_steps × step_cost + VAE_decode, throughput is batch_size × (num_steps × step_cost)^-1. TTFT is small (one step); TPOT-equivalent is the full response time because image generation is "all-at-once" from the user's perspective.
