Why this came about

In 1957 the linguist J. R. Firth wrote, "You shall know a word by the company it keeps." The idea that a word's meaning shows in the words around it led to counting methods in the 1990s (latent semantic analysis), but the vectors were large and slow to compute. In 2013 Tomas Mikolov and colleagues at Google released word2vec, which learned compact vectors for millions of words in hours on ordinary computers, and showed a striking side effect: arithmetic on the vectors captured relations, as in king − man + woman ≈ queen. Word vectors quickly became the standard first layer of NLP systems. Today's language models still begin with an embedding table learned the same way, as part of predicting the next token.

In plain words first
Judging a word by its neighbours

Suppose you meet a new word, "chai", in sentences like "I drink hot chai in the morning" and "she ordered a cup of chai". Without a dictionary you can guess it is a drink, a bit like tea or coffee, because it turns up in the same places they do. Word vectors make that guess mathematical: describe each word by the words that appear around it, and words that keep the same company end up with similar descriptions, and so similar vectors.

Step 1 · Counting the company words keep

Here is a tiny text of 14 sentences. For each word, count which other words appear within two positions of it:

import numpy as np

corpus = """i drink hot coffee in the morning
i drink hot tea in the morning
she drinks a cup of coffee after lunch
he drinks a cup of tea after lunch
the coffee is too hot to drink
the tea is too hot to drink
the dog barks at the cat
the cat runs from the dog
my dog sleeps on the sofa
my cat sleeps on the sofa
the courier delivered the parcel on monday
the courier delivered the parcel on friday
we close early on friday
we close early on monday"""

sentences = [s.split() for s in corpus.splitlines()]
vocab = sorted({w for s in sentences for w in s})
idx = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}

window = 2
counts = np.zeros((len(vocab), len(vocab)))       # row: a word, column: a neighbour
for s in sentences:
    for i, w in enumerate(s):
        for j in range(max(0, i - window), min(len(s), i + window + 1)):
            if j != i:
                counts[idx[w], idx[s[j]]] += 1

print(len(vocab), "words; the row for 'coffee':")
print({vocab[j]: int(counts[idx["coffee"], j]) for j in np.nonzero(counts[idx["coffee"]])[0]})
What you should see
37 words; the row for 'coffee':
{'after': 1, 'cup': 1, 'drink': 1, 'hot': 1, 'in': 1, 'is': 1, 'lunch': 1, 'of': 1, 'the': 2, 'too': 1}

Each row of counts is now a vector describing a word by its neighbours. Compare rows with cosine similarity:

def cosine(a, b):
    return a @ b / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))

for a, b in [("coffee", "tea"), ("dog", "cat"), ("monday", "friday"), ("coffee", "dog"), ("tea", "friday")]:
    print(f"{a:>7} ~ {b:<7} {cosine(counts[idx[a]], counts[idx[b]]):.2f}")
What you should see
 coffee ~ tea     1.00
    dog ~ cat     0.90
 monday ~ friday  1.00
 coffee ~ dog     0.35
    tea ~ friday  0.00

Coffee and tea have identical neighbours in this text, so their vectors are identical (1.00); dog and cat nearly so; coffee and dog share only common words like "the"; tea and friday share nothing. Nobody told the program what any word means. Similar usage alone produced similar vectors.

On real text, these count vectors are huge (one column per vocabulary word) and dominated by common words. That is where learning comes in.

Step 2 · word2vec: learning short vectors

word2vec turns the counting into a training task, the same one you ran on shopping baskets in Lesson 3.19. Slide a window along the text and collect (word, neighbour) pairs:

s = "the courier delivered the parcel on monday".split()
pairs = [(s[i], s[j]) for i in range(len(s))
         for j in range(max(0, i - window), min(len(s), i + window + 1)) if j != i]
print(len(pairs), "pairs from one sentence, e.g.", pairs[:6])
What you should see
22 pairs from one sentence, e.g. [('the', 'courier'), ('the', 'delivered'), ('courier', 'the'), ('courier', 'delivered'), ('courier', 'the'), ('delivered', 'the')]

Then train an embedding table so that the dot product of a word's vector with its real neighbours' vectors is high, and with randomly chosen words (negative samples) low. The code is exactly Lesson 3.19's training loop with these pairs in place of basket pairs. Trained on billions of words, word2vec produced vectors of 100 to 300 numbers per word that capture remarkably fine relationships. The variant described here is called skip-gram with negative sampling; a cousin, GloVe (Stanford, 2014), learns similar vectors directly from the co-occurrence counts.

Step 3 · Real word vectors: GPT-2's embedding table

You do not need to train your own. GPT-2's first layer is a table of 50,257 token vectors with 768 numbers each, learned while predicting the next token (Lesson 3.19). Words with a leading space, like " coffee", are single tokens, so their rows are word vectors.

import torch
from transformers import GPT2Model, GPT2TokenizerFast

tokenizer = GPT2TokenizerFast.from_pretrained("gpt2")
table = GPT2Model.from_pretrained("gpt2").wte.weight.detach()      # wte = word token embeddings
unit = table / table.norm(dim=1, keepdim=True)                     # length 1, so dot product = cosine
print("embedding table:", tuple(table.shape))

def token_id(word):
    ids = tokenizer.encode(word)
    assert len(ids) == 1, f"{word!r} is {len(ids)} tokens"           # only single-token words, to keep it simple
    return ids[0]

def nearest(vector, k=5, exclude=()):
    sims = unit @ (vector / vector.norm())                           # cosine with all 50,257 tokens at once
    sims[list(exclude)] = -1                                         # do not return the query words themselves
    best = sims.topk(k)
    return [(tokenizer.decode([i]), round(float(s), 2)) for s, i in zip(best.values, best.indices)]

for w in [" coffee", " Monday", " refund", " happy", " seven"]:
    print(f"{w!r:<10} -> {nearest(table[token_id(w)], exclude=[token_id(w)])}")
What you should see
embedding table: (50257, 768)
' coffee'  -> [(' Coffee', 0.77), (' espresso', 0.67), (' tea', 0.62), (' caffeine', 0.62), (' Starbucks', 0.61)]
' Monday'  -> [(' Tuesday', 0.89), (' Thursday', 0.85), (' Wednesday', 0.84), (' Friday', 0.82), (' Sunday', 0.81)]
' refund'  -> [(' refunds', 0.78), (' reimb', 0.57), (' reimbursement', 0.57), (' reimburse', 0.56), (' rebate', 0.56)]
' happy'   -> [('happy', 0.7), (' Happy', 0.69), (' happier', 0.67), (' unhappy', 0.66), ('Happy', 0.66)]
' seven'   -> [(' six', 0.87), (' eight', 0.86), (' five', 0.85), (' nine', 0.85), (' four', 0.8)]
Reading this output
  • The first time, from_pretrained("gpt2") downloads the model (about 500 MB) from Hugging Face and caches it; later runs load it from disk. Lesson 7.23 explores the rest of the model.
  • Days sit next to days, numbers next to numbers, " refund" next to " refunds", " reimbursement" and " rebate". These relationships were learned only from predicting text.
  • Capitalised and space-less variants (" Coffee", "happy") appear as separate neighbours: they are different tokens with their own rows, but nearly the same meaning.
  • " unhappy" is one of the closest words to " happy". Opposites are used in very similar contexts ("I am ___ with the service"), so embeddings learned from context put them close together. Similar vectors mean "used similarly", not "means the same".

Step 4 · Directions in the space mean something

Differences between vectors can capture relationships. The classic test is an analogy: king is to man as ? is to woman. Compute king − man + woman and look for the nearest word:

for a, b, c in [(" king", " man", " woman"), (" Paris", " France", " Japan"),
                (" walking", " walk", " swim"), (" big", " bigger", " small")]:
    v = table[token_id(a)] - table[token_id(b)] + table[token_id(c)]
    answer = nearest(v, k=3, exclude=[token_id(a), token_id(b), token_id(c)])
    print(f"{a} - {b} + {c} = {answer}")
What you should see
 king -  man +  woman = [(' queen', 0.71), (' princess', 0.6), (' Queen', 0.6)]
 Paris -  France +  Japan = [(' Tokyo', 0.69), ('Japan', 0.61), (' Japanese', 0.6)]
 walking -  walk +  swim = [(' swimming', 0.73), (' Swim', 0.61), (' bathing', 0.56)]
 big -  bigger +  small = [(' large', 0.59), (' tiny', 0.48), (' Small', 0.46)]
Reading this output
  • king − man + woman lands nearest " queen"; Paris − France + Japan nearest " Tokyo"; walking − walk + swim nearest " swimming". The difference vectors "male to female", "country to capital" and "verb to -ing form" are roughly consistent directions in the space.
  • The last one fails: big − bigger + small should give "smaller", but gives " large" and " tiny". Analogies work for some relationships and not others, and the famous examples were partly chosen because they work. Treat them as a demonstration, not a guarantee.

Explore the space yourself. The map shows 32 words placed along the main directions of variation (found by PCA, Lesson 2.27). Click any word to see its nearest neighbours among all 50,257 tokens.

Notice that each PCA direction tends to pick out one kind of word: one separates the days of the week, another places, another numbers from food, another feelings. The 768 numbers encode many such directions at once.

Step 5 · Embeddings absorb what the text says, including its biases

Word vectors learn associations from the text they were trained on, and text written by people contains stereotypes. One way to see it: take the direction from " she" to " he" and measure where occupation words fall along it.

direction = table[token_id(" he")] - table[token_id(" she")]
direction = direction / direction.norm()
for w in [" nurse", " dancer", " secretary", " teacher", " programmer", " soldier", " doctor", " engineer"]:
    print(f"{w:<12} {float(unit[token_id(w)] @ direction):+.3f}")
What you should see
 nurse       -0.170
 dancer      -0.126
 secretary   -0.062
 teacher     -0.056
 programmer  -0.057
 soldier     -0.035
 doctor      -0.019
 engineer    -0.014

All the values are slightly negative (the direction is not perfectly centred), so compare them with each other: " nurse" and " dancer" sit noticeably further towards " she" than " engineer" and " doctor". The model was never told anything about gender and jobs; it absorbed the pattern from how people write. Research in 2016 (Bolukbasi and colleagues) found the same in word2vec, and showed that systems built on such vectors can repeat the bias, for example when ranking CVs. Large language models inherit associations like these too, which is one reason they are tested and tuned for fairness (Lesson 10.41 and the Safety, security and governance section).

Step 6 · One vector per word is not enough

Static word vectors give each word one vector, whatever the sentence. But many words have several meanings:

print(nearest(table[token_id(" bank")], k=8, exclude=[token_id(" bank")]))
What you should see
[(' banks', 0.76), (' Bank', 0.73), (' banking', 0.66), ('bank', 0.65), ('Bank', 0.63), (' banker', 0.61), (' bankers', 0.59), (' Banks', 0.55)]

The neighbours are forms of the word itself and its financial meaning (" banks", " banking", " banker"); nothing about rivers. The river sense of "bank" is much rarer in text, so the single vector is dominated by the financial meaning; a word's one vector is an average of all its uses, weighted by how often each occurs. In "we sat on the river bank", that is the wrong vector. The fix is contextual embeddings: start from this table, then let every word's vector be adjusted by the words around it. That adjustment is what attention does (Lesson 5.36 and the Transformers, step by step section), and it is why a transformer's upper layers know which "bank" you mean.

The same idea as a formula
  • co-occurrence vector for word w: c_w with entry j = number of times word j appears within the window around w
  • skip-gram with negative sampling: maximise log σ(v_w · u_c) + Σ log σ(−v_w · u_n) over (word, context) pairs (w, c) and random words n
  • analogy: answer = argmax over words x of cos(v_x, v_king − v_man + v_woman), excluding the three input words
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Words with similar vectors have the same meaning."
    ✓ They are used in similar contexts. Opposites ("happy", "unhappy") and related-but-different words ("tea", "coffee") are close too.
  • ✗ "Word vectors are neutral facts about language."
    ✓ They reflect the text they were trained on, including stereotypes, and systems built on them can repeat those biases.
  • ✗ "A word has one meaning, so one vector is enough."
    ✓ Many words have several senses; static vectors blur them together, which contextual embeddings fix.

Practice
Apply it

Try two analogies of your own with GPT-2's vectors, one that you expect to work and one that you expect to fail, for example Delhi − India + France and good − better + bad. Use only words that are single tokens (the token_id check will tell you). (Solution X1.)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Sleeps and drink

0.00. "sleeps" appears only in "my dog sleeps on the sofa" and "my cat sleeps on the sofa", with neighbours dog, cat, my, on, the. "drink" appears with hot, coffee, tea, i, to, too. They share no neighbours within two words, so their vectors have no overlap.

SolutionM1 · Learning a new word

For example: "i drink hot chai in the morning", "he drinks a cup of chai after lunch", "the chai is too hot to drink". After rebuilding, chai's row matches tea's almost exactly (cosine close to 1), and its similarity to dog stays low (about 0.35, from shared words like "the"). This is the "chai" thought experiment from the start of the lesson, done in code.

SolutionX1 · Your own analogies

Delhi − India + France gives " Paris" in second place, just behind the space-less token "France": capitals are a strong, consistent relationship in text, though the answer list needs reading with care. Comparative forms such as good − better + bad fail here (the top answers are " Good", "bad" and " Bad", not " worse"), as big − bigger + small did. Relationships that are written about often and consistently are captured better.

Recap
  • A word's meaning shows in the company it keeps; describing words by their neighbours gives similar vectors to similar words.
  • word2vec learns short vectors by predicting neighbours with negative sampling, the same training as Lesson 3.19.
  • A language model's embedding table holds real word vectors: nearest neighbours and some analogies work by vector arithmetic.
  • Similar vectors mean similar usage, which includes opposites; embeddings also absorb biases from their training text.
  • Static vectors give one vector per word; contextual embeddings from attention adapt each word to its sentence.
