In plain words first
The idea in one line

GPT-4o ships a product most open models cannot replicate: an agent that hears voice, sees video, and speaks back in real time. The open-ecosystem answer by late 2024 was MIO (Wang et al., September 2024). MIO tokenizes text, image, speech, and music, trains one causal transformer over the interleaved sequences, and generates any modality to any modality. AnyGPT (Zhan et al., February 2024) was the proof of concept; MIO is the scale-up; Unified-IO 2 (Allen AI, December 2023) is the cousin with vision + action grounding. This lesson reads the a

The problem

In plain words first

An "any-to-any" model takes any mix of text, images and speech as input and can answer in any of them. MIO (September 2024) builds one by turning everything into tokens. Text uses its normal tokenizer. Each image becomes 32 codes from an image tokenizer, and speech becomes codes from a speech tokenizer that stacks several codebooks, coarse to fine (residual vector quantisation). All these codes get their own number ranges in one shared vocabulary, and one transformer, initialised from the Yi-6B language model, is trained to predict the next token whatever it is. This lesson covers the tokenizers, the training curriculum, streaming speech and the latency budget a voice product must meet. It measures residual quantisation on a real audio codec, EnCodec (93 MB), and computes MIO's token budgets. No model is trained.

A unified multimodal model is easy to claim and hard to build at scale. Most "any-to-any" systems until 2024 were pipelined: vision model → text representation → speech model → audio. Each hop loses information, adds latency, and complicates training. GPT-4o's demo video showed a single-model alternative with subsecond response; open systems trailed by months.

The engineering challenges:

  • Tokenizers must exist for every modality, compress losslessly-enough for reconstruction, and produce tokens at rates the transformer can consume.
  • A single vocabulary must allocate space for text (32k+), image (8k+), speech (4k+), music (8k+). Fifty-thousand-plus entries minimum.
  • Training data must cover every input-output pair (text→image, image→speech, speech→image, etc.) or the model must compose.
  • Inference must stream output tokens fast enough for conversational latency (<500ms time-to-first-audio-byte).

Idea 1 · Four tokenizers for four modalities

MIO's tokenizer stack:

  • Text: the BPE tokenizer of its base model, Yi-6B (64,000 entries). The toy below uses a 32,000-entry text range.
  • Image: SEED-Tokenizer (2023), a discrete image tokenizer with an 8192-entry codebook that emits 32 tokens per 224x224 image.
  • Speech: SpeechTokenizer residual-VQ (2023), which encodes speech into 8 hierarchical codebooks at 50 frames per second; the first level is coarse content, later levels add prosody and speaker identity. MIO keeps the content level plus the first three timbre levels, 1024 codes each, so 4096 speech IDs and 200 speech tokens per second.
  • Music: similar residual-VQ (Meta's MusicGen / Encodec family), 4-8 codebooks. MIO itself does not ship a music tokenizer (its fourth modality is video, as frames through the image tokenizer); this lesson's toy adds music to show how a new modality claims an ID range.

Each modality produces integer tokens. The tokens get disjoint ID ranges in the shared vocabulary:

text:   0..31999
image:  32000..40191  (8192 image tokens)
speech: 40192..44287  (4 codebooks x 1024 speech tokens)
music:  44288..52479  (8192 music tokens, the toy's extension)
sep:    52480..52485  (<image>, <speech>, <music>, </...>)

Total: ~52k vocabulary. The input embedding and output projection span all of it.

Idea 2 · Streaming decode

Speech generation uses residual-VQ. The transformer predicts speech tokens for all kept levels (in MIO, interleaved in the sequence; in designs like Moshi, a small extra transformer predicts the deeper levels for each frame). Each frame is 20 ms of audio at 50 frames per second. The MIO paper makes no streaming or latency claims; the pattern below is how speech LLMs are made to stream in general.

The streaming pattern:

  1. User speaks into mic; real-time audio tokenizer emits speech tokens every 50ms.
  2. MIO consumes tokens as they arrive (prompt prefill + incremental forward).
  3. Output tokens stream out as generated; a parallel speech decoder converts them to audio samples with ~50-150ms latency.
  4. Time-to-first-audio-byte: the MIO paper reports no latency number. The budget below is an estimate for this design, against GPT-4o's reported 232 ms minimum and 320 ms average response time to audio.

Mini-Omni (arXiv:2408.16725), GLM-4-Voice (arXiv:2412.02612), and Moshi (arXiv:2410.00037) are complementary streaming speech-LLM designs. Moshi reports a theoretical latency of 160 ms and about 200 ms in practice on a single GPU.

The same idea as a formula

Residual vector quantisation. With codebooks C1​,…,CQ​, the audio frame's feature z is quantised level by level on what is left over:

r0​=z,cq​=argkmin​∥rq−1​−Cq​[k]∥,rq​=rq−1​−Cq​[cq​],z^=q=1∑Q​Cq​[cq​].
Each level refines the previous approximation, so the first levels carry the most.

Token rate. At F frames per second with Q kept levels: F⋅Q tokens per second (MIO: 50×4=200).

Shared vocabulary. Modality m with Km​ codes occupies ids [om​,om​+Km​), offsets stacked after the text vocabulary.

Worked example
A 30-second spoken reply
  • MIO: 30 s × 200 tokens/s = 6,000 speech tokens, against about 75 text tokens for the same words.
  • EnCodec at 6 kbps (8 levels × 75 frames/s): 600 tokens/s, 18,000 for 30 s.
  • If the model emits the 4 levels of each frame one after another at 12 ms per token, it needs 48 ms per 20 ms frame, slower than real time. Streaming designs therefore predict the deeper levels in parallel or with a small separate model (Moshi's "depth transformer").
  • A 5-minute voice conversation at 200 tokens/s is 60,000 tokens of context.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Speech tokens are like words."
    ✓ They are short acoustic frames (20 ms here), many per word, at several levels each.
  • ✗ "More codebook levels only add bitrate."
    ✓ They add detail: each level quantises the error left by the previous ones (measured below).
  • ✗ "MIO streams speech in real time."
    ✓ The MIO paper makes no streaming or latency claims; streaming is a system design on top.

Residual quantisation on a real audio codec

Meta's EnCodec 24 kHz (15M parameters, 93 MB) encodes 3 seconds of a synthetic voice (a gliding pitch with harmonics) at its five bandwidths. The run reports codebooks, tokens and the signal-to-noise ratio of the decoded audio. About 10 seconds.

import math, torch
from transformers import AutoProcessor, EncodecModel, logging
logging.set_verbosity_error()
torch.manual_seed(0)
# Residual vector quantization (RVQ), the speech-and-music tokenizer idea behind MIO's SpeechTokenizer, measured on
# Meta's EnCodec 24 kHz (a real 15M-parameter audio codec, 93 MB). More codebooks = finer detail = more tokens.
model = EncodecModel.from_pretrained("facebook/encodec_24khz").eval()
proc = AutoProcessor.from_pretrained("facebook/encodec_24khz")
sr = model.config.sampling_rate
t = torch.arange(3 * sr) / sr                      # 3 seconds of a synthetic "voice": a gliding pitch with harmonics
f0 = 140 + 40 * torch.sin(2 * math.pi * 0.7 * t)
phase = 2 * math.pi * torch.cumsum(f0, 0) / sr
wave = sum(torch.sin(k * phase) / k for k in range(1, 8)) * (0.6 + 0.4 * torch.sin(2 * math.pi * 3 * t)) * 0.3
wave = wave + 0.003 * torch.randn_like(wave)
x = proc(raw_audio=wave.numpy(), sampling_rate=sr, return_tensors="pt")

print(f"EnCodec: {sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters()) / 1e6:.0f}M parameters, {model.config.frame_rate} frames/s, "
      f"codebooks of {model.config.codebook_size}")
print(f"{'bandwidth':>10}{'codebooks':>11}{'tokens / s':>12}{'tokens (3 s)':>14}{'reconstruction SNR':>21}")
for bw in model.config.target_bandwidths:
    with torch.no_grad():
        enc = model.encode(x.input_values, x.padding_mask, bandwidth=bw)
        rec = model.decode(enc.audio_codes, enc.audio_scales, x.padding_mask)[0][0, 0, : wave.numel()]
    snr = 10 * torch.log10(wave.pow(2).sum() / (wave - rec).pow(2).sum())
    n_q = enc.audio_codes.shape[2]
    print(f"{bw:>8} kbps{n_q:>11}{n_q * model.config.frame_rate:>12,}{enc.audio_codes[0, 0].numel():>14,}{snr:>18.1f} dB")
codes = enc.audio_codes[0, 0]
print(f"\ncodes at 24 kbps: shape {tuple(codes.shape)} (codebook levels x frames); first frame, levels 1-8: {codes[:8, 0].tolist()}")

Reading this output

Each doubling of levels doubles the tokens. EnCodec runs at 75 frames per second. With 2 codebooks that is 150 tokens per second (450 for 3 s), and with 32 it is 2,400 per second (7,200). Bandwidth grows with it, from 1.5 to 24 kbps.

Quality rises, by smaller steps each time. The signal-to-noise ratio of the decoded audio goes 7.0 → 10.0 → 14.3 → 18.7 → 21.8 dB. Each level quantises what the previous levels left over, so the first levels capture the most and later levels add finer detail. This coarse-to-fine structure is why MIO can keep SpeechTokenizer's first 4 of 8 levels: the content and much of the voice are in the early ones.

What the codes look like. At 24 kbps, 3 seconds is a 32 × 225 grid: 32 levels for each of 225 frames. The first frame's codes (521, 793, 843, …) are each one of 1,024 entries. An any-to-any model must predict grids like this, and must decide whether to flatten the levels into the sequence (more tokens) or predict them in parallel (more machinery).

About the test signal: it is synthetic (a gliding pitch with harmonics), not real speech, so the absolute SNR values do not transfer to voices. The trend does.

MIO's vocabulary and token budgets

MIO's shared vocabulary laid out from the paper's numbers (Yi's 64,000 text tokens, 8,192 SEED image codes, 4 × 1,024 speech codes, separators). Then the token costs of typical requests, and an estimated time-to-first-audio budget. Instant.

# MIO's shared vocabulary and token budget, from the numbers in the MIO paper
TEXT = 64_000                                        # Yi-6B's text vocabulary
ranges, start = {}, 0
for name, size in [("text", TEXT), ("image (SEED, 8,192 codes)", 8192),
                   ("speech (4 SpeechTokenizer levels x 1,024)", 4 * 1024), ("separators", 4)]:
    ranges[name] = (start, start + size - 1); start += size
for name, (a, b) in ranges.items():
    print(f"  {name:<44} ids {a:>6,} .. {b:>6,}")
print(f"  total vocabulary {start:,} ids (the paper adds 12,292 to the text model's embedding and output layers)\n")

SPEECH_TPS, IMG = 200, 32                            # 4 codebooks x 50 tokens/s; 32 tokens per 224-px image
def budget(speech_s=0, images=0, text=0):
    return speech_s * SPEECH_TPS + images * IMG + text
for label, kw in [("a 10-second spoken question", dict(speech_s=10)),
                  ("a 30-second spoken answer", dict(speech_s=30)),
                  ("one image + a 20-word text question", dict(images=1, text=30)),
                  ("a 60-second video at 1 frame/s, no audio", dict(images=60)),
                  ("a 5-minute voice conversation", dict(speech_s=300))]:
    print(f"  {label:<44} {budget(**kw):>7,} tokens")

         "decode tokens to waveform": 30}
print()

Reading this output

The vocabulary is mostly text. Yi's 64,000 text ids come first, then 8,192 image codes, 4,096 speech codes (4 levels × 1,024) and 4 separators: 76,292 ids. The 12,292 new ids are the rows MIO adds to the embedding and output layers.

Speech is by far the most expensive modality. A 10-second spoken question is 2,000 tokens, while an image with a text question is only 62, thanks to SEED's 32 codes per image. A 5-minute conversation is 60,000 tokens of speech, enough to fill a long context window. A one-minute video at one frame per second is 1,920 tokens.

The latency budget is dominated by generation. With assumed component times, emitting 200 ms of speech as 40 sequential tokens takes 480 ms, more than everything else together. The estimate comes to 650 ms against GPT-4o's 232–320 ms and Moshi's 160–200 ms. That gap is why streaming speech models predict the deeper levels in parallel, use lower frame rates, and start speaking after a few frames. These numbers are assumptions for illustration, not MIO measurements.

Idea 3 · Four-stage curriculum

MIO's training curriculum:

  1. Stage 1 — alignment. Large-scale modality-pair corpora: text-image, text-speech (and video frames with text). Trains the new vocabulary entries.
  2. Stage 2 — interleaved. Multi-modality interleaved documents (blogs with images + video, podcasts with transcripts, etc.). Trains cross-modality context.
  3. Stage 3 — speech-enhanced. Speech's share of training tokens rises across the three pretraining stages, from 12.5% to 37.5% to 75%, to lift speech quality without losing text capability.
  4. Stage 4 — SFT. Instruction tuning across modalities: VQA, captioning, narration, speech-to-speech dialogue.

Missing a stage degrades specific capabilities: skip stage 2 and the model loses cross-modality context; skip stage 3 and speech is poor.

Idea 4 · Chain-of-visual-thought

MIO introduces chain-of-visual-thought: the model emits intermediate image tokens as a reasoning step. For "is the cat climbing a tree?" the model:

  1. Emits <image> tokens rendering the scene (from the input image or a sketch).
  2. Emits text analyzing the sketch.
  3. Emits the final answer.

The rendered intermediate image serves as a scratchpad. The MIO paper presents this as an emergent ability with qualitative examples, not benchmark results. The idea mirrors chain-of-thought for text reasoning.

Idea 5 · Competitors in any-to-any

  • AnyGPT (arXiv:2402.12226): 4 modalities (text, image, speech, music), similar design.
  • Unified-IO 2 (arXiv:2312.17172): adds vision action outputs, depth, normals. More task diversity, smaller scale.
  • NExT-GPT (arXiv:2309.05519): LLM + modality-specific diffusion decoders. Not a single-model approach.
  • CoDi (arXiv:2305.11846): composable diffusion; any-to-any via shared latent.

MIO is the closest to pure-token any-to-any. AnyGPT is its conceptual ancestor.

Idea 6 · Latency budget

For a conversational product, every component's latency matters:

  • Mic to audio tokens: ~50ms.
  • Prefill (audio tokens + history): ~100ms on an 8B model.
  • First output token: ~50ms.
  • Parallel residual-VQ + speech decoder: ~100-150ms.

Total time-to-first-audio-byte: roughly 300 ms at best for this design. GPT-4o reports as little as 232 ms (320 ms on average); Moshi 160 ms in theory, about 200 ms in practice. Neither MIO nor AnyGPT publishes a measured figure, so treat this budget as an estimate, not a benchmark.

Idea 7 · Why any-to-any stays hard

Even in 2026, open any-to-any models trail closed ones on two axes:

  • Speech quality. The residual-VQ tokenizer is lossy; conversational speech sounds robotic compared to ElevenLabs-class voices.
  • Cross-modality reasoning. Asking the model "sing about what you see" still fails more often than pure-vision tasks.

These are open research problems. Qwen3-Omni (Lesson 12.20) is the most advanced open attempt in 2025.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Open omni models moved to "thinker–talker" designs: Qwen2.5-Omni and Qwen3-Omni (Lesson 12.20) separate text reasoning from streaming speech generation.
  • Full-duplex speech models: Moshi listens and speaks at the same time with about 200 ms latency; GLM-4-Voice and Mini-Omni stream speech tokens from an LLM.
  • Audio codecs as tokenizers: EnCodec, DAC, Mimi (Moshi) and SpeechTokenizer turn audio into RVQ tokens; lower frame rates (12.5 Hz for Mimi) cut token counts.
  • Laptop reality: MIO is 7B (Yi-6B base) and needs 4-bit quantization on an 8 GB GPU; codecs like EnCodec run easily on a laptop CPU.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Any-to-any by tokens: every modality gets a tokenizer and its own id range in one vocabulary.
  • MIO: Yi-6B base, 32 SEED codes per image, 4 SpeechTokenizer levels at 50 Hz (200 tokens/s).
  • Residual VQ: each level quantises the leftover error; more levels, more detail, more tokens.
  • Curriculum: alignment, interleaved, speech-enhanced pretraining, then SFT.
  • Voice products live or die by time-to-first-audio; speech tokens are many and must be decoded fast.

Given a conversational product (speech in / speech out, optional vision, optional music), a model size, and a target latency, audit the any-to-any design and produce a viable configuration.

Produce:

1. Modality mix. Which modalities in, which out. Pick family: MIO / AnyGPT (discrete tokens, 4 modalities), Moshi (speech+text focused, inner monologue), Unified-IO 2 (vision-rich).
2. Shared vocabulary plan. ID ranges for text + image + speech + music + separators. Total size typically 40-50k.
3. Tokenizer stack. BPE + SEED + SpeechTokenizer-RVQ + Encodec. Highlight which are still bottlenecks (speech quality typically).
4. Training curriculum. Four-stage MIO recipe, or two-stage for speech-focused Moshi.
5. TTFAB latency budget. Mic encoder + prefill + first token + residual decode + speech decoder. Compare to ~500ms conversational bar.
6. Quality-vs-latency pareto. Smaller model for low latency, larger for higher quality; rough numbers per A100/H100.

Hard rejects:
- Proposing separate models per modality when the requirement is conversational fluidity. The pipeline latency stacks and feels worse.
- Using a speech tokenizer with only 1 codebook layer. Quality will be robotic for any production voice.
- Claiming MIO's TTFAB matches GPT-4o. It does not yet; Moshi 160ms is the closest open number.

Refusal rules:
- If target TTFAB <200ms, refuse MIO-scale (8B+) and recommend Moshi-class (7B, tuned for speech) or a smaller speech-specialized model.
- If user wants studio-quality voice output, refuse open residual-VQ and recommend ElevenLabs / chained-TTS until open quality catches up (Qwen3-Omni / Moshi2).
- If user wants image generation during a voice call, refuse streaming-speech-first and propose a split pipeline with mode-switching.

Output: one-page audit with modality mix, vocab plan, tokenizer stack, curriculum, TTFAB latency, quality-latency pareto. End with arXiv 2409.17692 (MIO), 2410.00037 (Moshi), 2402.12226 (AnyGPT).

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Tokens double, quality rises by diminishing steps

Tokens double with each doubling of levels: 450, 900, 1,800, 3,600 and 7,200 for 3 seconds. Quality rises, but by smaller steps: 7.0, 10.0, 14.3, 18.7 and 21.8 dB of signal-to-noise. The first levels capture most of the signal, and each later level refines a smaller leftover error.

SolutionB1 · The voice falls behind real time

The token rate doubles from 200 to 400 tokens per second of speech. If tokens are generated one at a time at about 12 ms each, the 8 levels of one 20 ms frame take about 96 ms, five times slower than real time. The voice starts late, stutters, and falls behind more with every sentence. The context also fills twice as fast: a 5-minute conversation becomes 120,000 tokens. The quality gain is small, since the later levels add the finest detail (the EnCodec run shows shrinking gains per level). Keep fewer levels in the main model and predict the rest with a small parallel decoder (as Moshi does), or use a codec with a lower frame rate. Measure the real-time factor, generation time divided by audio duration, which must stay well below 1.
