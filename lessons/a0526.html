The idea in one line

NER found the entities. Entity linking anchored them. Relation extraction finds the edges between them. A knowledge graph is the sum of nodes, edges, and their provenance.

The problem

In plain words first

Named entity recognition (Lesson 5.6) finds the things a text talks about. Entity linking (Lesson 5.25) says exactly which thing each one is. Relation extraction finds what the text says between them: "Tim Cook became CEO of Apple" connects a person to a company through the relation "chief executive". Each fact becomes a triple, (subject, relation, object), and a pile of triples is a knowledge graph you can query: who runs Apple, where was Microsoft's CEO born, who owns GitHub. This lesson builds the three kinds of extractor (hand-written patterns, trained models, prompted LLMs) and measures where each one fails. It then shows the part that makes a graph trustworthy: tying every triple to the words that support it, mapping relations onto a fixed vocabulary with the right direction, checking types, and recording when a fact was true.

An analyst reads: "Tim Cook became CEO of Apple in 2011." Four facts:

  • (Tim Cook, role, CEO)
  • (Tim Cook, employer, Apple)
  • (Tim Cook, start_date, 2011)
  • (Apple, type, Organization)

Relation Extraction (RE) turns free text into structured triples (subject, relation, object). Aggregate across a corpus and you have a knowledge graph. Aggregate and query and you have a reasoning substrate for RAG, analytics, or compliance audits.

The 2026 problem: LLMs extract relations enthusiastically. Too enthusiastically. They hallucinate triples that the source text does not support. Without provenance, you cannot tell real triples from plausible fiction. The current answer is to make every triple point back to the exact words that support it, and to reject triples that cannot. AEVS (Yang et al., 2026), described below, is one published version of this anchor-and-verify design.

The idea

Triple form. (subject_entity, relation_type, object_entity). Relations come from a closed ontology (Wikidata properties, FIBO, UMLS) or an open set (OpenIE-style, anything goes).

Three extraction approaches.

  1. Rule / pattern-based. Hearst patterns: "X such as Y" → (Y, isA, X). Plus hand-crafted regex. Brittle, precise, explainable.
  2. Supervised classifier. Given two entity mentions in a sentence, predict the relation from a fixed set, including "no relation". Trained on TACRED (106,264 sentences, 41 relations plus no_relation), ACE or the TAC KBP data. The standard approach from about 2015 to 2022.
  3. Generative. A sequence-to-sequence model trained to output triples (REBEL, 2021), or an LLM prompted to emit them. Works out of the box on new text. Needs provenance, or produces plausible-looking junk.

AEVS (Anchor-Extraction-Verification-Supplement; Yang et al., 2026). One recent hallucination-mitigation framework for LLM extraction:

  • Anchor. Identify every entity span and relation-phrase span with exact positions.
  • Extract. Generate triples linked to anchor spans.
  • Verify. Match each triple element back to the anchors: exact match first, then fuzzy, schema-based and full-text matching. Fully matched triples are accepted, partly matched ones get a second LLM check, the rest are rejected.
  • Supplement. A coverage pass ensures no anchored span is dropped.

In the paper's ablations the anchor constraint is the main reason hallucinations fall. The cost is 2.8 to 4.3 LLM calls per input instead of one, and in return every accepted triple can be traced to its source span. It is one recent paper, so treat its exact recipe as one option; the anchor-and-verify principle is the part to keep.

The open-vs-closed tradeoff.

  • Closed ontology. Fixed property list (e.g., Wikidata's more than 11,000 properties, most of them identifiers that link to other databases). Predictable. Queryable. Hard to invent.
  • Open IE. Any verbal phrase becomes a relation. High recall. Low precision. Messy to query.

Production KGs usually mix: open IE for discovery, then canonicalize relations onto a closed ontology before merging into the main graph.

The same idea as a formula

Relation classification. A sentence x with two marked entities e1​,e2​ (for example [E1] Tim Cook [/E1] became CEO of [E2] Apple [/E2], the entity-marker input of Baldini Soares et al., 2019) is encoded, the vectors at the two markers are joined, and a softmax picks a relation:

P(r∣x,e1​,e2​)=softmax(W[he1​​;he2​​]+b)r​,r∈R∪{no_relation}

Most pairs have no relation. A sentence with n entities has n(n−1) ordered pairs, and only a few are related. About 80% of TACRED's examples are labelled no_relation, so its standard score is micro-F1 over the other classes: a model that always says "no relation" gets 80% accuracy and an F1 of 0.

Scoring an extractor. Compare the set of predicted triples T^ with the gold set T:

P=∣T^∣∣T^∩T∣​,R=∣T∣∣T^∩T∣​,F1​=P+R2PR​,hallucination rate=∣T^∣∣{t∈T^:t not supported by the source}∣​

A triple with the right subject and relation but a wrong object ("White" for "White Plains") counts twice: once in the false triples, once in the missed ones.

Distant supervision (Mintz et al., 2009) makes training data without annotators: if the knowledge base holds (e1​,r,e2​), label every sentence containing both e1​ and e2​ with r. That is often wrong for one sentence, so later work moved to the weaker at-least-one assumption (Riedel et al., 2010): the bag B of sentences mentioning the pair expresses r at least once, and the model scores the bag, for example P(r∣B)=maxx∈B​P(r∣x).

Constraints a graph can check. Each property has a domain and a range: for (i,p,v), require type(i)∈dom(p) and type(v)∈range(p). Some properties are asymmetric: (a,p,b)∈G⇒(b,p,a)∈/G. Time-bounded statements carry qualifiers, and a query at time τ keeps only statements with start≤τ<end.

Filling gaps (link prediction) is a related task: knowledge-graph embeddings such as TransE (Bordes et al., 2013) give each entity and relation a vector and score a candidate edge by f(h,r,t)=−∥h+r−t∥. They predict edges that are likely; extraction finds edges a text states.

Worked example
Pairs, labels and noise by hand

Take "Born in Hyderabad, Satya Nadella joined Microsoft in 1992 and became its CEO in 2014." NER finds five entities: Hyderabad, Satya Nadella, Microsoft, 1992, 2014. That gives 5×4=20 ordered pairs. The sentence states three relations: (Satya Nadella, place of birth, Hyderabad), (Satya Nadella, employer, Microsoft) and (Microsoft, chief executive officer, Satya Nadella), with the two years as qualifiers. So 17 of the 20 pairs, 85%, are "no relation". A classifier sees mostly negatives.

Now distant supervision. The knowledge base holds (Satya Nadella, P19, Hyderabad). Three sentences mention both names (the second is invented for this example):

  1. "Born in Hyderabad, Satya Nadella joined Microsoft…": expresses P19. Label correct.
  2. "Satya Nadella gave a talk in Hyderabad.": does not. Label wrong.
  3. "Hyderabad-born Satya Nadella…": expresses P19. Label correct.

Sentence-level labelling is right 2 times in 3. At the bag level, the at-least-one assumption is satisfied (sentences 1 and 3), so the bag's label is right, and a max over the bag lets the model rely on its most convincing sentence.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "A triple an LLM returns is a fact from the text."
    ✓ It is a claim. Check each element against the source before it enters the graph.
  • ✗ "If the subject and object both occur in the text, the triple is supported."
    ✓ Both directions of "GitHub was acquired by Microsoft" pass that test. The relation, its direction and the entity types need checking too.
  • ✗ "A knowledge graph stores what is true."
    ✓ It stores statements, with a source and, for many relations, a time span. "Tim Cook is CEO of Apple" was true from 2011 to 2026.

Step 1 · pattern-based extraction

import re

SIMPLE_PATTERNS = [
    (r"(?P<s>[A-Z]\w+) (?:is|was) (?:a|an|the) (?P<o>[A-Z]?\w+)", "isA"),
    (r"(?P<s>[A-Z]\w+) (?:is|was) born in (?P<o>\w+)", "bornIn"),
    (r"(?P<s>[A-Z]\w+) works? (?:at|for) (?P<o>[A-Z]\w+)", "worksAt"),
    (r"(?P<s>[A-Z]\w+) founded (?P<o>[A-Z]\w+)", "founded"),
]

sentence = "Obama was born in Honolulu. Jobs founded Apple. Darwin was a naturalist. Tim Cook works at Apple."
for pattern, rel in SIMPLE_PATTERNS:
    for m in re.finditer(pattern, sentence):
        print((m["s"], rel, m["o"]))

Reading this output

Precision 0.78, recall 0.47 (F1​=2×0.78×0.47/1.25=0.58). When a pattern fires it is usually right; most facts are simply missed. The misses are the ways English moves words around: passive voice ("was founded by", "was acquired by"), apposition ("Apple's chief executive, Tim Cook"), a fronted phrase ("Born in Hyderabad, Satya Nadella…"), a hyphen ("co-founded") and a second subject joined by "with". The two false triples show the other weakness: "Nobody" looks like a name to a regex that only checks for a capital letter, and "White Plains" is cut to "White" because the object group allows one word. "No longer works at Meta" produced nothing, but only by luck: the pattern needs the name right before "works", and "LeCun no" is not a name.

A dependency parse instead of a regex

Open IE in about twenty lines: spaCy's parser gives every word a head and a grammatical role, and the code reads (subject, verb, object) and (subject, verb + preposition, object) off the tree, plus passives with a "by" agent. Same fifteen sentences. Relations are now the verb's dictionary form, not property IDs.

import spacy
nlp = spacy.load("en_core_web_sm")

def phrase(tok, doc):          # widen a token to the named entity it sits in
    for e in doc.ents:
        if e.start <= tok.i < e.end:
            return e.text
    return tok.text

def open_triples(doc):
    out = []
    for v in doc:
        if v.pos_ not in ("VERB", "AUX"):
            continue
        kids = list(v.children)
        subj = [c for c in kids if c.dep_ == "nsubj"]
        passive = [c for c in kids if c.dep_ == "nsubjpass"]
        agents = [g for c in kids if c.dep_ == "agent" for g in c.children if g.dep_ == "pobj"]
        objs = [c for c in kids if c.dep_ in ("dobj", "attr")]
        preps = [(c, g) for c in kids if c.dep_ == "prep" for g in c.children if g.dep_ == "pobj"]
        if passive and agents:   # "Google was founded by Larry Page" -> (Larry Page, found, Google)
            for a in [agents[0]] + list(agents[0].conjuncts):
                out.append((phrase(a, doc), v.lemma_, phrase(passive[0], doc)))
        for s in subj:
            out += [(phrase(s, doc), v.lemma_, phrase(o, doc)) for o in objs]
            out += [(phrase(s, doc), f"{v.lemma_} {p.text}", phrase(o, doc)) for p, o in preps]
    return out

for sent, gold in tests:
    print(f"{sent:58s} -> {open_triples(nlp(sent)) or '-'}")

Reading this output

The parse fixes the passive cases: "Google was founded by Larry Page and Sergey Brin" now gives both founders, in the right direction, and "GitHub was acquired by Microsoft" gives (Microsoft, acquire, GitHub). It also shows the costs of open IE. Relations are raw verbs, so ("found", "found with", "become in", "acquire in") must still be mapped onto properties, and dates come out as objects ("become in 2011"). "Became CEO of Apple" loses Apple, because "of Apple" hangs off the noun "CEO", not the verb. "Was born in" without a "by" agent is missed, and so is "co-founded". Worst, "Yann LeCun no longer works at Meta" comes out as a plain (Yann LeCun, work at, Meta): "no longer" is not tagged as a negation, so the extractor states the opposite of the sentence. Rules over a parse generalise better than regexes but still need a rule for every construction.

Step 2 · supervised seq2seq relation extraction

from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForSeq2SeqLM

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("Babelscape/rebel-large")
model = AutoModelForSeq2SeqLM.from_pretrained("Babelscape/rebel-large")

text = "Tim Cook was born in Alabama. He later became CEO of Apple."
encoded = tok(text, return_tensors="pt", truncation=True)
output = model.generate(**encoded, max_length=200)
decoded = tok.batch_decode(output, skip_special_tokens=False)

REBEL is a BART-based seq2seq relation extractor: it generates triples instead of classifying entity pairs. Text in, triples out, with relation names taken from Wikidata properties. The decoded strings still carry REBEL's <triplet>, <subj> and <obj> markers, so split on them (as the model card's parser does) to get (subject, relation, object) records. Fine-tuned on distant-supervision data. Standard open-weights baseline.

This block is not run here: rebel-large has about 400 million parameters, and the course does not download or train large models. What it produces is a string. The parser below reads that format; the string is written by hand to show the shape of REBEL's output.

Reading REBEL's output format

REBEL writes <triplet> before each subject, <subj> before each of that subject's objects, and <obj> before the relation's name, which is a Wikidata property label. One subject can have several objects.

def parse_rebel(generated):
    """Split REBEL's output string into (subject, relation, object) triples (as in the model card's parser)."""
    text = generated.replace("<s>", "").replace("</s>", "").replace("<pad>", "")
    triples, subj, obj, rel, cur = [], "", "", "", None
    for tok in text.split():
        if tok == "<triplet>":                 # a new subject starts
            if rel:
                triples.append((subj.strip(), rel.strip(), obj.strip()))
            subj, obj, rel, cur = "", "", "", "subj"
        elif tok == "<subj>":                  # what follows is an object of the current subject
            if rel:
                triples.append((subj.strip(), rel.strip(), obj.strip()))
            obj, rel, cur = "", "", "obj"
        elif tok == "<obj>":                   # what follows is the relation's name
            rel, cur = "", "rel"
        elif cur == "subj":
            subj += " " + tok
        elif cur == "obj":
            obj += " " + tok
        elif cur == "rel":
            rel += " " + tok
    if subj and obj and rel:
        triples.append((subj.strip(), rel.strip(), obj.strip()))
    return triples

# Written by hand in REBEL's output format (the model itself is not run on this page).
generated = "<s><triplet> Tim Cook <subj> Alabama <obj> place of birth <subj> Apple <obj> employer</s>"
for t in parse_rebel(generated):
    print(t)
LABEL_TO_PID = {"place of birth": "P19", "employer": "P108", "founded by": "P112", "chief executive officer": "P169"}
print([(s, LABEL_TO_PID.get(r, "?"), o) for s, r, o in parse_rebel(generated)])

The relation names are Wikidata labels in words, so a small lookup turns them into property IDs. Two things are not solved by the format: "He later became CEO of Apple" only becomes a Tim Cook triple if the model (or a coreference step, Lesson 5.24) resolves "He", and "employer" is the model's choice of property; Wikidata would also accept P169 on Apple's side. Generative extractors still need the same checks as LLMs.

A small LLM extracts triples, and a span check catches what it made up

Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct (inference only, greedy decoding) gets four sentences with a simple JSON prompt. It is not asked for character offsets: models see tokens, not characters (Lesson 5.18), so they count offsets badly. Instead the code finds each subject and object in the text itself, the minimal form of anchoring.

import json, re, torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM

name = "Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct"
ltok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(name)
lm = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(name, dtype=torch.float32).eval()

docs = ["Tim Cook became CEO of Apple in 2011. Steve Jobs founded Apple in 1976.",
        "Google was founded by Larry Page and Sergey Brin while they were students at Stanford.",
        "Born in Hyderabad, Satya Nadella joined Microsoft in 1992 and became its CEO in 2014.",
        "GitHub was acquired by Microsoft in 2018 for 7.5 billion dollars."]

def llm_extract(text):
    msgs = [{"role": "system", "content": "You extract facts as JSON."},
            {"role": "user", "content": 'Extract (subject, relation, object) triples from the text. Answer with a JSON list of '
             'objects with keys "subject", "relation", "object". Only include facts stated in the text.\n\nText: ' + text}]
    ids = ltok.apply_chat_template(msgs, add_generation_prompt=True, return_tensors="pt", return_dict=True)["input_ids"]
    with torch.no_grad():
        out = lm.generate(ids, max_new_tokens=200, do_sample=False)
    answer = ltok.decode(out[0, ids.shape[1]:], skip_special_tokens=True)
    m = re.search(r"\[.*\]", answer, re.S)
    return json.loads(m.group(0)) if m else []

def ground(text, phrase):      # where does this phrase occur in the source? None = nowhere
    i = text.lower().find(phrase.lower())
    return (i, i + len(phrase)) if i >= 0 else None

llm_triples = []
for d, text in enumerate(docs):
    print(f"\n{text}")
    for t in llm_extract(text):
        s, r, o = t["subject"], t["relation"], t["object"]
        spans = ground(text, s), ground(text, o)
        ok = None not in spans
        llm_triples.append((d, s, r, o, ok))
        print(f"   ({s} | {r} | {o})   subject at {spans[0]}, object at {spans[1]}  {'grounded' if ok else 'REJECT: not in the text'}")
n_ok = sum(t[-1] for t in llm_triples)
print(f"\n{len(llm_triples)} triples, {n_ok} grounded, {len(llm_triples) - n_ok} rejected by the span check")

Step 4 · canonicalize onto a closed ontology

# Wikidata statements point from the item that "has" the property to its value.
# P169 "chief executive officer" and P112 "founded by" belong to the organisation,
# so text that names the person first must be flipped.
RELATION_MAP = {
    "is the ceo of": ("P169", True),    # (Tim Cook, is the CEO of, Apple) -> (Apple, P169, Tim Cook)
    "became ceo of": ("P169", True),
    "was born in":   ("P19",  False),   # place of birth: person -> place
    "founded":       ("P112", True),    # (Steve Jobs, founded, Apple) -> (Apple, P112, Steve Jobs)
    "works at":      ("P108", False),   # employer: person -> organisation
}


def canonicalize(relation):
    rel_low = relation.lower().strip()
    if rel_low in RELATION_MAP:
        return RELATION_MAP[rel_low]
    return None   # drop unmapped open relations or route to manual review


def to_wikidata(s, relation, o):
    hit = canonicalize(relation)
    if hit is None:
        return None
    pid, flip = hit
    return (o, pid, s) if flip else (s, pid, o)

Canonicalize, then check types, entailment and direction

The grounded LLM triples from Step 3 go through three more checks. A phrase map gives the property and whether to flip; spaCy's entity labels (the anchors) are checked against each property's allowed types; the NLI model of Lesson 5.21 is asked whether the source text entails a fixed sentence for the triple; and an asymmetry rule catches a fact stated both ways.

import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForSequenceClassification
nli_name = "MoritzLaurer/mDeBERTa-v3-base-xnli-multilingual-nli-2mil7"
ntok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(nli_name)
nli = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(nli_name).eval()

# phrase -> (Wikidata property, flip?) for the phrases this LLM produced
LLM_MAP = {"became ceo of": ("P169", True), "founded": ("P112", True),
           "joined": ("P108", False), "acquired by": ("P1830", True)}
# domain and range: the entity types each property allows (item, value)
TYPES = {"P169": ("ORG", "PERSON"), "P112": ("ORG", "PERSON"), "P108": ("PERSON", "ORG"), "P1830": ("ORG", "ORG")}
# one fixed sentence per property, used to ask the NLI model "does the text say this?"
TEMPLATE = {"P169": "{v} is the CEO of {i}.", "P112": "{i} was founded by {v}.",
            "P108": "{i} works for {v}.", "P1830": "{i} owns {v}."}

def entails(premise, hypothesis):
    enc = ntok(premise, hypothesis, return_tensors="pt")
    with torch.no_grad():
        return float(nli(**enc).logits.softmax(-1)[0, 0])     # label 0 = entailment

    verdict = "ACCEPT" if got == want and p_ent > 0.5 else ("REJECT: types" if got != want else "REJECT: not entailed")
    print(f"({item}, {pid}, {value})  types {got} want {want}  NLI('{TEMPLATE[pid].format(i=item, v=value)}') = {p_ent:.2f}  {verdict}")
    if verdict == "ACCEPT":
        accepted.append((d, item, pid, value))

# Owning, founding and leading are one-way: if both directions survived, one of them is wrong.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Graphs built by LLMs for RAG. Microsoft's GraphRAG has an LLM extract entities and relations from every chunk, clusters the resulting graph into communities and summarises each, so that "global" questions about a whole corpus can be answered. Extraction quality and entity merging decide how good those summaries are.
  • Graph builders in common stacks. Neo4j's LLM Knowledge Graph Builder, LangChain's LLMGraphTransformer, LlamaIndex's property-graph index and Amazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases (GraphRAG with Neptune Analytics) all wrap the extract-then-merge pipeline of this lesson behind one call; the validation steps above are what they leave to you.
  • Small, fast extractors. GLiNER-style models with relation names given at inference time, and fine-tuned encoders, handle high-volume extraction where an LLM call per chunk is too costly.

Sources: GraphRAG, Neo4j LLM Graph Builder, LangChain LLMGraphTransformer, Bedrock GraphRAG, Apple's CEO announcement.

Pitfalls

  • Coreference before RE. "He founded Apple" — RE needs to know who "he" is. Run coref first (lesson 24).
  • Entity canonicalization. "Apple Inc" and "Apple" must resolve to the same node. Entity linking first (lesson 25).
  • Hallucinated triples. LLMs emit triples the text does not support. Enforce span verification.
  • Relation canonicalization drift. Open IE relations are inconsistent ("was born in," "came from," "is a native of"). Collapse to canonical ids or the graph is unqueryable.
  • Temporal errors. "Tim Cook is CEO of Apple" was true from August 2011 to September 2026: false in 2005, and false again since John Ternus took over on 1 September 2026. The lesson's own main.py says "Yann LeCun works at Meta", which stopped being true when he left in late 2025 to start a company. Many relations are time-bounded. Use qualifiers (P580 start time, P582 end time in Wikidata).
  • Reversed roles pass most checks. In the Step 4 run, "GitHub owns Microsoft" passed the span check, the type check and an NLI model (0.94). Use asymmetry and other ontology constraints, and send conflicts to review.
  • Negation and hedging. "No longer works at", "is rumoured to acquire", "denied founding": a triple keeps the relation and drops the qualifier unless you check for it.
  • Domain mismatch. REBEL trained on Wikipedia. Legal, medical, and scientific text often need domain-fine-tuned RE models.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • A relation is a typed, directed edge: (item, property, value), plus a source span and, for many properties, a time span.
  • Patterns are precise but miss most phrasings; parses generalise further; generative models and LLMs cover the most and invent the most.
  • Ground every element in the source text, then check property direction, entity types and constraints such as asymmetry. NLI checks help but can be fooled by swapped roles.
  • Canonicalize relation phrases onto a fixed vocabulary before merging; unmapped phrases go to review, not into the graph.
  • Score with precision, recall and hallucination rate on hand-labelled triples.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Two invented, three reversed

Two of ten triples name something not in the text ("location", "software company"), and three of the remaining eight point the wrong way: Google "founded" Larry Page and Sergey Brin, and Microsoft "acquired by" GitHub, which the model output alongside the correct reading. The instruction "only include facts stated in the text" does not stop either error. Span checks remove the first kind; only direction, type and constraint checks catch the second.

SolutionB1 · Evidence for one fact accepted for another

verify checks that the evidence string sits at its recorded span, and that the subject and object appear somewhere in the text. A triple {"subject": "Tim Cook", "relation": "P112", "object": "Apple"} carrying the span and evidence of "Steve Jobs founded Apple" passes: the evidence is real, and "Tim Cook" occurs earlier in the document. The fix is to require subject and object inside the evidence span, and the relation's trigger words too, so that each triple is supported by its own evidence and not by the document as a whole.
