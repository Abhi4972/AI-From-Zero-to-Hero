Why this came about

As AI moved into decisions that affect people, regulators and companies set rules: the NIST AI Risk Management Framework (2023), the EU AI Act (2024) and management standards such as ISO 42001. Engineering teams now produce much of the evidence those rules require.

In plain words first
Rules of the road, written down

Responsible AI turns good intentions into practices: knowing which systems use AI and how risky each is, documenting what each does and its limits, testing for unfair or harmful outcomes, keeping humans able to oversee and override, recording decisions, and meeting regulations such as the EU AI Act. Governance is the process that makes those practices happen before and after launch, not a document written once.

The regulatory map (as of October 2026)

Laws change; check the current text before relying on any date here.

India

  • Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and the DPDP Rules, 2025 (notified 13 November 2025). Phased: the Data Protection Board and definitions started immediately; consent managers from about November 2026; most obligations (notice and consent, data principal rights, security safeguards, breach notification, Significant Data Fiduciary duties, cross-border rules, penalties) from about May 2027. Breach reports to the Board are due within 72 hours. Agents that process customer data must have a lawful basis, honor erasure and access requests (including in vector indexes, memories and logs), and protect data with reasonable security safeguards.
  • India AI Governance Guidelines (MeitY, November 2025): a principles-based approach with no new AI-specific law; existing laws (IT Act, DPDP, consumer protection, sectoral rules) apply, with voluntary frameworks and techno-legal measures for new risks.
  • RBI FREE-AI framework (August 2025) for regulated financial entities: seven principles ("sutras") and 26 recommendations across infrastructure, policy, capacity, governance, protection and assurance, including board-approved AI policies, lifecycle model governance, independent audits and customer grievance channels. Expect sector regulators (RBI, SEBI, IRDAI) to set the binding detail.

European Union

The EU AI Act classifies AI systems by risk: prohibited practices; high-risk systems (for example in hiring, credit scoring, education, essential services, law enforcement) with obligations for risk management, data governance, documentation, logging, human oversight, accuracy and robustness; transparency obligations (tell people they are talking to AI; label deepfakes and certain AI-generated content); and obligations for general-purpose AI model providers.

Obligation Applies from
Prohibited practices, AI literacy 2 February 2025
General-purpose AI model obligations 2 August 2025
Transparency obligations (Article 50) 2 August 2026
High-risk systems in Annex III (stand-alone uses) 2 December 2027 (moved from 2 August 2026 by the Digital Omnibus, in force since 27 July 2026)
High-risk AI in regulated products (Annex I) 2 August 2028 (moved from 2 August 2027)

It applies to providers and deployers outside the EU when the system is used in the EU, so Indian firms serving European clients are in scope.

United States

No comprehensive federal AI law. Federal policy since December 2025 favors minimal regulation and pressure on states to harmonize; sector regulators and existing laws (consumer protection, anti-discrimination, privacy) still apply. States act on their own: Colorado repealed and replaced its 2024 AI Act in May 2026 with narrower rules for automated decision-making, expected from January 2027; California has laws on frontier-model transparency and other AI uses. Track the states where you have users.

What a governance program actually contains

  1. An AI system register: every model, agent and AI feature, with owner, purpose, data used, models and versions, risk tier and status. You cannot govern what you cannot list.
  2. Risk tiering: a short questionnaire that sorts use cases into tiers with different review requirements.
  3. Review gates: lightweight review for low risk; security, privacy, legal and fairness review plus red teaming for high risk.
  4. Documentation: model cards (what a model is and is not good for), system cards for the whole agent (tools, data, guardrails, evaluations, known limitations), data-protection impact assessments where required.
  5. Monitoring and incident response: quality and safety metrics in production, an AI incident process, and a kill switch.
  6. Literacy and accountability: trained staff and named owners.
In production
Why governance programs stall, and the fix

Programs stall when the review takes longer than the build: teams stop registering projects, and the register goes stale. What works in practice: a fast lane for low-risk uses (register, tier, ship with platform defaults), review effort concentrated on the few high-risk systems, controls built into the platform (gateway logging, approved model list, guardrail defaults, eval pipelines) so that compliance is the default, and a named owner for every system. If the register is out of date, generate it from gateway and cloud inventories instead of asking teams.

Step 1 · Risk tiering as code

Risk tiering can start as code everyone can read:

from dataclasses import dataclass, asdict

@dataclass
class UseCase:
    name: str
    affects_legal_or_financial_outcomes: bool     # credit, hiring, insurance, benefits...
    acts_without_human_approval: bool              # sends, pays, changes records on its own
    processes_personal_data: bool
    customer_facing: bool
    eu_users: bool

def risk_tier(u: UseCase) -> tuple[str, list[str]]:
    reviews = ["owner sign-off", "eval suite in CI"]
    if u.affects_legal_or_financial_outcomes:
        tier = "high"
        reviews += ["legal", "fairness testing", "human oversight design", "red team"]
        if u.eu_users:
            reviews.append("EU AI Act high-risk conformity assessment")
    elif u.acts_without_human_approval or (u.customer_facing and u.processes_personal_data):
        tier = "medium"
        reviews += ["security review", "privacy review", "red team"]
    else:
        tier = "low"
    if u.processes_personal_data:
        reviews.append("DPDP: notice, consent or legitimate use, erasure path")
    if u.customer_facing:
        reviews.append("AI disclosure to users")
    return tier, reviews

support = UseCase("ShopKart support agent", affects_legal_or_financial_outcomes=False,
                  acts_without_human_approval=False, processes_personal_data=True, customer_facing=True, eu_users=False)
loans = UseCase("SME loan pre-screening agent", affects_legal_or_financial_outcomes=True,
                acts_without_human_approval=False, processes_personal_data=True, customer_facing=False, eu_users=True)
for u in (support, loans):
    tier, reviews = risk_tier(u)
    print(f"{u.name}: {tier}\n  " + "\n  ".join(reviews))

Step 2 · The register, as data you can audit

A register in a spreadsheet goes stale within a quarter. Keep it as data in version control (YAML here), and run an audit in CI that flags missing owners, overdue reviews and models your gateway has retired:

from datetime import date
import yaml

REGISTER_YAML = """
- id: AI-007
  name: ShopKart support agent
  owner: priya.n@shopkart.example
  purpose: Answer order questions and cancel unshipped orders with approval
  models: [claude-sonnet-5-5, text-embedding-3-small]
  tools: [get_order_status, cancel_order]
  data: [orders, customer profile, support tickets]
  tier: medium
  status: production
  last_review: 2026-03-02
  eval_suite: evals/support_v4.yaml
- id: AI-012
  name: SME loan pre-screening agent
  owner: ""
  purpose: Rank SME loan applications for analyst review
  models: [gpt-4o]
  tools: [fetch_bureau_report]
  data: [applications, credit bureau data]
  tier: high
  status: pilot
  last_review: 2025-09-15
  eval_suite: ""
"""
REQUIRED = ["owner", "purpose", "models", "data", "tier", "eval_suite"]
REVIEW_EVERY = {"low": 365, "medium": 180, "high": 90}          # days between reviews, by tier
RETIRED_MODELS = {"gpt-4o"}                                     # from your gateway's model catalog

def audit(entry: dict, today: date) -> list[str]:
    issues = [f"missing {f}" for f in REQUIRED if not entry.get(f)]
    overdue = (today - entry["last_review"]).days - REVIEW_EVERY[entry["tier"]]
    if overdue > 0:
        issues.append(f"review overdue by {overdue} days")
    issues += [f"uses retired model {m}" for m in entry["models"] if m in RETIRED_MODELS]
    return issues

for e in yaml.safe_load(REGISTER_YAML):
    problems = audit(e, today=date(2026, 10, 27))
    print(f"{e['id']} {e['name']} [{e['tier']}]:", "; ".join(problems) or "ok")
What you should see
AI-007 ShopKart support agent [medium]: review overdue by 59 days
AI-012 SME loan pre-screening agent [high]: missing owner; missing eval_suite; review overdue by 317 days; uses retired model gpt-4o

The high-risk system has no owner and no evaluation suite, which is exactly the kind of project that otherwise surfaces only after an incident. Install with uv add pyyaml. Many teams generate the register automatically from the gateway's logs and the cloud accounts' inventories, then ask owners to fill in purpose and tier, so nothing can run without appearing in it.

Step 3 · Generate the system card from the evidence

A system card should be generated from the same sources the system runs on (register, eval results, guardrail configuration), so it cannot drift from reality:

EVALS = {"AI-007": {"task success": 0.91, "groundedness": 0.97, "pii leak rate": 0.0, "injection attack success": 0.02}}
GUARDRAILS = {"AI-007": ["input: prompt-injection classifier", "tool: cancel_order needs human approval",
                         "output: PII and canary scan"]}
LIMITS = {"AI-007": ["Cannot issue refunds; routes refund requests to a person",
                     "English and Hindi only; other languages fall back to a human"]}

def system_card(entry: dict) -> str:
    sid = entry["id"]
    lines = [f"# System card: {entry['name']} ({sid})", "",
             f"**Purpose**: {entry['purpose']}", f"**Owner**: {entry['owner']}  |  **Risk tier**: {entry['tier']}",
             f"**Models**: {', '.join(entry['models'])}  |  **Tools**: {', '.join(entry['tools'])}", "",
             "## Measured performance (latest eval run)"]
    lines += [f"- {k}: {v:.0%}" for k, v in EVALS[sid].items()]
    lines += ["", "## Guardrails"] + [f"- {g}" for g in GUARDRAILS[sid]]
    lines += ["", "## Known limitations"] + [f"- {l}" for l in LIMITS[sid]]
    return "\n".join(lines)

print(system_card(yaml.safe_load(REGISTER_YAML)[0]))

**Purpose**: Answer order questions and cancel unshipped orders with approval
**Owner**: priya.n@shopkart.example  |  **Risk tier**: medium
**Models**: claude-sonnet-5-5, text-embedding-3-small  |  **Tools**: get_order_status, cancel_order

In a real pipeline, EVALS is read from the latest evaluation run (Lesson 14.115) and GUARDRAILS from the deployed configuration. "Known limitations" is the section reviewers read first and teams most often leave empty.

Step 4 · Fairness testing for a high-risk use

For anything that affects people's access to money, jobs or services, measure outcomes by group before launch. Two common checks: the selection rate ratio (the "four-fifths rule" from US employment practice: the lower group's rate should be at least 80% of the higher group's), and equal opportunity (qualified people should be recommended at similar rates in every group):

# Analyst-review recommendations from the loan agent on a test set, by applicant group.
# Each row: (group, model_recommended, actually_repaid)   -- outcomes from historical loans
rows = ([("metro", 1, 1)] * 52 + [("metro", 1, 0)] * 8 + [("metro", 0, 1)] * 10 + [("metro", 0, 0)] * 30
        + [("tier-3", 1, 1)] * 27 + [("tier-3", 1, 0)] * 5 + [("tier-3", 0, 1)] * 23 + [("tier-3", 0, 0)] * 45)

def rates(group: str) -> tuple[float, float]:
    g = [r for r in rows if r[0] == group]
    selection = sum(r[1] for r in g) / len(g)                                  # share recommended
    good = [r for r in g if r[2] == 1]
    tpr = sum(r[1] for r in good) / len(good)                                  # good borrowers recommended
    return selection, tpr

(sel_m, tpr_m), (sel_t, tpr_t) = rates("metro"), rates("tier-3")
print(f"selection rate  metro {sel_m:.0%}  tier-3 {sel_t:.0%}  ratio {sel_t / sel_m:.2f}")
print(f"good borrowers recommended  metro {tpr_m:.0%}  tier-3 {tpr_t:.0%}  gap {tpr_m - tpr_t:.0%}")
print("four-fifths rule:", "PASS" if sel_t / sel_m >= 0.8 else "FAIL", "| equal opportunity gap > 10 points:",
      "FAIL" if tpr_m - tpr_t > 0.10 else "PASS")
What you should see
selection rate  metro 60%  tier-3 32%  ratio 0.53
good borrowers recommended  metro 84%  tier-3 54%  gap 30%
four-fifths rule: FAIL | equal opportunity gap > 10 points: FAIL

The second number is the damning one: among applicants who went on to repay, tier-3 city applicants were recommended far less often. Possible causes to investigate include features that act as proxies for location, thinner bureau histories, or prompts that reward signals metro applicants have more often. Which metric and threshold apply is a legal and policy decision, not an engineering one; your job is to measure them, report them in the system card, and re-measure after every change. Libraries such as Fairlearn and AIF360 compute these and many more.

Step 5 · Make erasure requests actually erase

Under the DPDP Act and the GDPR, a person can ask for their data to be erased. An agent spreads personal data far beyond the main database. Keep a data map of every store, and test erasure end to end:

import re

class Store:
    def __init__(self, name: str, rows: list[dict]):
        self.name, self.rows = name, rows
    def erase(self, customer_id: str) -> int:
        before = len(self.rows)
        self.rows = [r for r in self.rows if r.get("customer_id") != customer_id]
        return before - len(self.rows)
    def mentions(self, customer_id: str) -> int:
        return sum(customer_id in str(r) for r in self.rows)

DATA_MAP = [   # every place an agent can leave personal data behind
    Store("orders db", [{"customer_id": "C-42", "order": "A-1001"}, {"customer_id": "C-77", "order": "A-1003"}]),
    Store("conversation logs", [{"customer_id": "C-42", "text": "Where is A-1001?"}]),
    Store("traces", [{"customer_id": "C-42", "span": "get_order_status"}, {"span": "chat", "note": "user C-42 asked"}]),
    Store("long-term memory", [{"customer_id": "C-42", "text": "Prefers delivery after 6 pm"}]),
    Store("vector index", [{"customer_id": "C-42", "chunk": "ticket T-9 text"}]),
    Store("semantic cache", [{"prompt": "where is A-1001 for C-42", "answer": "shipped"}]),
]

def erase_everywhere(customer_id: str) -> None:
    for store in DATA_MAP:
        n = store.erase(customer_id)
        left = store.mentions(customer_id)
        print(f"{store.name:18} erased {n}  still mentions {left}" + ("  <-- not keyed by customer_id" if left else ""))

erase_everywhere("C-42")
What you should see
orders db          erased 1  still mentions 0
conversation logs  erased 1  still mentions 0
traces             erased 1  still mentions 1  <-- not keyed by customer_id
long-term memory   erased 1  still mentions 0
vector index       erased 1  still mentions 0
semantic cache     erased 0  still mentions 1  <-- not keyed by customer_id

The leftovers are the typical ones: free text in traces, and cache keys built from prompts. The fixes are to tag every record with the subject's ID at write time, redact identifiers before they reach traces and caches, and keep retention short where you cannot. Note that erasure has legal exceptions: invoices and orders may have to be kept for tax law, so the real process maps each store to a rule (erase, anonymize or retain with a reason).

Step 6 · Incidents and the kill switch

Every production agent needs a way to be contained in seconds, and a defined process when it misbehaves. Build degraded modes, not just on and off, and make the switch fail closed:

import json
from datetime import datetime, timezone

FLAGS = {"AI-007": "full"}            # in production: a feature-flag service, changeable in seconds
MODES = {"full": {"get_order_status", "cancel_order"}, "read_only": {"get_order_status"}, "off": set()}

def handle(system_id: str, tool: str) -> str:
    mode = FLAGS.get(system_id, "off")                       # fail closed if the flag is missing
    if mode == "off":
        return "Our assistant is unavailable; connecting you to a person."
    return f"run {tool}" if tool in MODES[mode] else f"{tool} disabled ({mode}); offered a person instead"

INCIDENTS = []
def open_incident(system_id: str, what: str, harm: str, users_affected: int) -> dict:
    severity = "SEV1" if harm in {"data leak", "financial loss"} or users_affected > 1000 else "SEV2"
    if severity == "SEV1":
        FLAGS[system_id] = "read_only"                       # contain first, investigate second
    inc = {"id": f"INC-{len(INCIDENTS) + 1:03d}", "system": system_id, "severity": severity, "what": what,
           "opened": datetime(2026, 10, 27, 9, 30, tzinfo=timezone.utc).isoformat(), "mode_now": FLAGS[system_id]}
    INCIDENTS.append(inc)
    return inc

print(handle("AI-007", "cancel_order"))
print(json.dumps(open_incident("AI-007", "cancelled orders that had shipped", "financial loss", 40)))
print(handle("AI-007", "cancel_order"))
print(handle("AI-007", "get_order_status"))
print(handle("AI-099", "get_order_status"))
What you should see
run cancel_order
{"id": "INC-001", "system": "AI-007", "severity": "SEV1", "what": "cancelled orders that had shipped", "opened": "2026-10-27T09:30:00+00:00", "mode_now": "read_only"}
cancel_order disabled (read_only); offered a person instead
run get_order_status
Our assistant is unavailable; connecting you to a person.

A SEV1 automatically dropped the agent to read-only, so customers could still check orders while the team investigated. An unknown system ID gets "off", because a flag service that cannot answer should never mean "full power". After the incident: a blameless review, a regression test for the failure, and an entry in the register. Report serious incidents where law requires it (the DPDP breach window is 72 hours; the EU AI Act has serious-incident reporting for high-risk systems), and consider sharing anonymized lessons through the AI Incident Database.

Transparency obligations, in practice

The EU AI Act's Article 50 obligations (from 2 August 2026) and similar rules elsewhere turn into a few concrete features: tell users at the start of a conversation that they are talking to an AI system; label AI-generated or manipulated images, audio and video, with machine-readable marking (for example C2PA content credentials or watermarks such as Google's SynthID) as well as visible labels where content could mislead; and disclose AI-generated text published to inform the public on matters of public interest unless a person reviewed it and takes editorial responsibility. Add them to your platform once, as defaults, rather than per project.

Industry practice
Governance that teams don't route around

Governance programs fail when review takes months, so teams stop declaring projects. Mature programs keep the low-risk path nearly automatic (register, tier, ship with standard controls), spend review effort on the few high-risk systems, and build controls into the platform (gateways with logging, approved model lists, guardrail defaults, evaluation pipelines) so that compliance is the easy path rather than paperwork.

From the field
Air Canada: the company owns what its chatbot says

In February 2024 a Canadian tribunal ordered Air Canada to compensate a passenger after its website chatbot wrongly told him he could claim a bereavement fare discount retroactively, within 90 days. Air Canada argued that it could not be held liable for information provided by the chatbot; the tribunal member called that "a remarkable submission", held that the chatbot was part of Air Canada's website, and that the airline was responsible for all information on it. The award was small (CAD 812.02), but the principle is the one your legal team will apply: answers from an agent are statements by the company. That is the governance case for grounded answers with citations, refusal when the policy is not found, and human escalation for anything involving money or rights. Exercises

Predict before you run

risk_tier classifies a use case. Where would an agent that decides refunds without human review fall, compared with one that drafts replies for agents to send? (Solution P1.)

Break it on purpose

Keep the AI register in a slide deck. What breaks when an auditor asks which systems use customer data? (Solution B1.)

Debugging exercise

The system card says the model was evaluated on Hindi, but the eval logs show only English. How do you prevent this? (Solution D1.)

Make it your own

Add a field "review due" to the register and an audit rule that flags systems whose last review is older than six months. (Solution M1.)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Autonomy raises the tier

The autonomous refund agent is higher risk: it makes consequential decisions about people without review. The drafting assistant has a human in the loop. Tiers decide which controls apply.

SolutionB1 · Registers as data

Nobody can query it, check it automatically, or tell whether it is current. A register as data (Step 2) can be audited and validated, for example by audit finding missing reviews.

SolutionD1 · Generate cards from evidence

Generate system cards from eval results and the register (Step 3), so claims come from data. Hand-written cards drift from reality.

SolutionM1 · Governance that runs

Date checks turn policy into alerts. Run the audit on a schedule and assign owners to fix findings.

Explain the mechanismWhat does an AI governance program actually contain?

Worked answer. An inventory (register) of AI systems with owners, purposes, data and models; risk tiering that decides required controls; evaluations, testing and documentation such as system cards; human oversight and incident processes; vendor and model management; and alignment with regulations and standards (the EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001, the NIST AI RMF). It works when it is automated into delivery, not a separate paperwork track.

A plausible wrong answer. "An ethics committee." Committees help; controls in the delivery process do the work.

Follow-ups. How do you keep the register current?

Debug a failureTeams bypass the governance review because it takes weeks. What do you change?

Worked answer. Tier the process: low-risk systems get an automated checklist, high-risk ones a full review. Automate evidence collection from pipelines, and publish turnaround targets.

A plausible wrong answer. "Make it mandatory." It already is; it is ignored because it is slow.

Follow-ups. What would the low-risk checklist contain?

Defend a designWho should own AI governance: legal, security or engineering?

Worked answer. Shared, with clear roles: legal and compliance define obligations, security owns threat controls, engineering owns evaluations and implementation, and product owners are accountable per system. A small central function coordinates and maintains the register.

A plausible wrong answer. "Legal, because it is about regulation." Without engineering, controls are not implemented.

Follow-ups. How would you report governance status to leadership?
