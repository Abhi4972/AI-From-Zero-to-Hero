Why RNNs ran out of road

In plain words first
The idea in one line

RNNs process tokens one at a time. Transformers process all tokens at once. That single architectural bet changed every scaling curve in deep learning after 2017.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
Recurrence "RNNs are sequential" Computation where step t depends on step t-1, forcing serial execution along the time axis.
Serial depth "How deep the graph is" Longest chain of dependent ops; bounds wall-clock even on infinite hardware.
Attention "Let tokens look at each other" Weighted sum $\sum_j a_{ij} v_j$ where $a_{ij}$ comes from a similarity score between positions $i$ and $j$.
Context window "How much the model sees" Number of positions an attention layer can take as input; quadratic memory cost scales here.
Inductive bias "Assumptions baked into the architecture" Prior about what the data looks like; CNNs assume translation invariance, RNNs assume recency.
State-space model "RNN with algebra behind it" Recurrence parameterized for parallel training via structured state-space matrices.
Quadratic bottleneck "Why context costs so much" Attention memory = $O(N^2)$ in sequence length; Flash Attention hides the constants, not the scaling.

The problem

In plain words first

A recurrent network reads a sentence the way you read with a finger under the words: one word at a time, carrying a running summary forward. That has two costs. The work cannot be split up, because word 10 waits for word 9, so a chip built to do thousands of things at once sits mostly idle. And anything the network learned about word 1 must survive being rewritten at every later step, so its influence fades. A transformer instead lets every word look at every other word directly, all at once; the price is that the number of word pairs grows with the square of the length. This lesson measures all three effects on this page's CPU with real PyTorch layers: the same arithmetic done as a chain versus in one batch, how much the last output still depends on early words, and how running time grows with length for a GRU and a self-attention layer. Nothing is trained.

Before 2017, every state-of-the-art sequence model on the planet — language, translation, speech — was a recurrent neural network. LSTMs and GRUs won ImageNet-equivalent translation benchmarks for half a decade. They were the only tool anyone had.

They had three fatal weaknesses. Sequential computation meant you could not parallelize along the time axis: token t+1 needs the hidden state from token t. A 1,024-token sequence meant 1,024 serial steps on a GPU that can do hundreds of thousands of floating-point operations per clock cycle. Training wall-clock time scaled linearly with sequence length on hardware designed for parallelism.

Vanishing gradients meant information 50 tokens back was already compressed through 50 non-linearities. Gated recurrent units (LSTM, GRU) softened the crush but never eliminated it. Long-range dependencies — "the book I read last summer on a plane to Kyoto was…" — routinely failed.

Fixed-width hidden states meant the encoder squeezed the entire source sequence into a single vector before the decoder saw anything. Doesn't matter if the source is 5 tokens or 500; the bottleneck is the same shape.

The 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need" proposed something radical: drop recurrence entirely. Let every position attend to every other position in parallel. Train in one big matrix multiplication instead of 1,024 sequential ones.

The result dominates every modality by 2026. Language (the GPT, Claude, Gemini, Llama and Qwen families), vision (ViT, DINOv3, SAM 3), audio (Whisper), biology (AlphaFold 3), robotics (vision-language-action models). Same block, different inputs.

The idea

Recurrence as a bottleneck. An RNN computes ht​=f(ht−1​,xt​). Each step depends on the previous. You cannot compute h5​ before h4​. On modern GPUs with 10,000+ parallel cores, this wastes 99% of the silicon on a long sequence.

Attention as a broadcast. Self-attention computes output_i = sum_j(a_ij * v_j) for every pair (i,j) simultaneously. The whole N×N attention matrix fills in one batched matmul. No step depends on another. GPUs love it.

The speedup is not a constant. It is the difference between O(N) serial depth and O(1) serial depth. In the original paper, the big Transformer beat the best recurrent translation models while training for 3.5 days on 8 GPUs, a fraction of their training cost; the advantage grows with sequence length until you hit the O(N2) cost of attention (which FlashAttention makes manageable in memory, though not in compute — see Lesson 7.12).

What transformers cost. Attention memory scales as O(N2). For 2K context, fine. For 128K context, you need sliding windows, RoPE extrapolation, Flash Attention tiling, or linear attention variants. Recurrence was O(N) in both time and memory; transformers trade time for memory and then win the time back through parallelism.

The inductive bias shift. RNNs assume locality and recency. Transformers assume nothing — every pair is a candidate for attention. That is why transformers need more data to train well but scale further once they have it. Kaplan et al. (2020) measured it: as models grow, transformers keep improving and make use of far more of their context than LSTMs of the same size. (Chinchilla, 2022, is about something else: how to split a compute budget between parameters and training tokens; see Lesson 7.13.)

The same idea as a formula

Recurrence. ht​=f(ht−1​,xt​). Computing hN​ needs N steps in order: serial depth N, total work O(Nd2) for width d, memory O(d) per sequence at inference.

Self-attention. For all positions at once, with Q=XWQ​, K=XWK​, V=XWV​:

Attn(X)=softmax(d​QK⊤​)V

Serial depth O(1) per layer (a few matrix products), work O(N2d+Nd2), and an N×N score matrix per head.

Gradient paths. In an RNN, the effect of xt​ on hN​ passes through every later step:

∂xt​∂hN​​=(k=t+1∏N​∂hk−1​∂hk​​)∂xt​∂ht​​

a product of N−t Jacobians. If their norms are below 1 it shrinks geometrically (vanishing), above 1 it grows (exploding). In one attention layer, output N reads xt​ through a single weighted sum: the path length is 1 for every t.

Where the crossover sits. Attention's N2d term overtakes the shared Nd2 projection term when N>d: for width 1,024, beyond about 1,000 tokens the score matrix dominates the cost.

Worked example
Counting depth and memory by hand

Depth. A 12-layer LSTM over 4,096 tokens has a dependency chain of about 12+4096 steps (each layer can start once the one below has produced its first output); a 12-layer transformer has 12 layers of a few batched products each.

Attention memory. One head at N=4,096 stores 40962=16.8 million scores: 64 MiB in fp32, 32 MiB in bf16. With 32 heads and a batch of 8, a naive implementation needs 64×32×8=16 GiB for one layer's scores, which is why FlashAttention never stores the full matrix.

RNN memory at inference. An LSTM with width 1,024 keeps 2×1024 numbers per sequence (hidden and cell state), whatever the length; a transformer's KV cache grows with every token (Lesson 7.12).

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Transformers do less arithmetic than RNNs."
    ✓ They do more, O(N2d) for the scores alone; they win because the arithmetic has no chain of dependencies, so parallel hardware can do it at once.
  • ✗ "Attention removes the vanishing-gradient problem everywhere."
    ✓ It gives every position a one-step path to every other within a layer; deep stacks still need residual connections and normalisation to train.
  • ✗ "RNNs are obsolete."
    ✓ Their constant memory per step is why state-space layers appear in production hybrid models.

How far back can the last output "see"?

An untrained single-layer LSTM and an untrained causal self-attention layer read the same 256 random input vectors. One backward pass measures how strongly the last output depends on each earlier input (the gradient norm ∥∂yN​/∂xt​∥).

import torch
# How strongly does the output at the LAST position depend on the input at each earlier position?
# Untrained networks, one backward pass each: the gradient norm |d out_T / d x_t| as a function of distance T - t.
torch.manual_seed(0)
N, d = 256, 64
x = torch.randn(1, N, d, requires_grad=True)
r = torch.randn(d)                          # a random read-out of the last output (a plain sum would cancel under LayerNorm)
lstm = torch.nn.LSTM(d, d, batch_first=True)
out, _ = lstm(x); (out[0, -1] @ r).backward()
g_rnn = x.grad[0].norm(dim=-1).detach().numpy(); x.grad = None
layer = torch.nn.TransformerEncoderLayer(d, nhead=4, dim_feedforward=4 * d, batch_first=True, dropout=0.0)
mask = torch.nn.Transformer.generate_square_subsequent_mask(N)
y = layer(x, src_mask=mask, is_causal=True); (y[0, -1] @ r).backward()
g_att = x.grad[0].norm(dim=-1).detach().numpy()
print(f"{'tokens back':>12} {'LSTM gradient':>14} {'attention gradient':>19}")
for back in [0, 1, 5, 20, 50, 100, 200, 255]:
    t = N - 1 - back
    print(f"{back:>12} {g_rnn[t]:14.2e} {g_att[t]:19.2e}")
print(f"LSTM: falls by {g_rnn[-1] / g_rnn[N - 51]:.0e}x over 50 tokens; attention: max/min over all earlier tokens "
      f"{g_att[:-1].max() / g_att[:-1].min():.1f}x")

Reading this output

In the LSTM, the dependence on an input falls by a factor of about 1011 over 50 tokens and underflows to exactly zero beyond about 200: each step multiplies the signal by a Jacobian smaller than 1, the product in the formula box. In the attention layer, every earlier position sits one weighted sum away from the last output, so its dependence stays at roughly the same level (around 10−2) whether the input is 1 or 255 tokens back; only the token itself, through the residual connection, stands out. Trained LSTMs learn forget gates near 1 and do much better than this untrained one, but the signal must still pass through every intermediate step; attention gives a direct path by construction.

Step 1 · measure serial depth

No neural network here — we simulate the core bottleneck numerically so you feel the gap on your laptop.

See code/main.py. We build two functions. One encodes a sequence as a chain of additions (serial, like an RNN). One encodes it as a parallel reduction (broadcast, like attention). Same math, different dependency graph.

def rnn_style(xs):
    h = 0.0
    for x in xs:
        h = 0.9 * h + x   # can't parallelize: h depends on previous h
    return h

def attention_style(xs):
    return sum(xs) / len(xs)  # every x is independent

We time both on sequences up to 100,000 elements. The RNN version is O(N) and a single CPU pipeline. Even in pure Python, the attention-style reduction beats it at length ≥ 1,000 because Python's sum() is implemented in C and iterates without interpreter overhead per step.

Step 2 · count theoretical operations

Both algorithms do N adds. The difference is dependency depth: how many operations must happen sequentially before the next can start. RNN depth = N. Attention depth = log(N) with a tree reduction, or 1 with a parallel scan. Depth, not op count, decides GPU time.

Step 3 · empirical scaling on long sequences

We print a timing table that makes the O(N) gap visible. On a 2026 Mac laptop, sequences under 1,000 elements are too fast to measure. Sequences of 100,000 show a clean linear scan. Scale that to a 16,384-token transformer with a 12-layer LSTM equivalent and you see why training wall-clock was a blocker in 2016.

Same arithmetic, two dependency structures

N steps of h=tanh(Wh+xt​) with width 512, computed as a chain (each step needs the previous h), against one N×512 matrix product where every row is independent. Both do 2Nd2 multiply-adds. Best of five runs.

import time, torch
torch.set_num_threads(2)
# The same arithmetic, two dependency structures: N small matrix-vector products one after another (each needs
# the previous result, as in an RNN) versus one N-row matrix product (rows independent, as in attention).
d = 512
W = torch.randn(d, d) / d ** 0.5
def timeit(f):
    t0 = time.perf_counter(); f(); return time.perf_counter() - t0
print(f"{'N':>6} {'serial chain (ms)':>18} {'one batched matmul (ms)':>24} {'speed-up':>9}  (same {2}·N·d² FLOPs)")
for N in [64, 256, 1024, 4096]:
    X = torch.randn(N, d)
    def chain():
        h = torch.zeros(d)
        for t in range(N):
            h = torch.tanh(W @ h + X[t])      # step t needs step t-1
    def batch():
        torch.tanh(X @ W.T)                   # every row at once
    serial, batched = [min(timeit(f) for _ in range(5)) for f in (chain, batch)]
    print(f"{N:>6} {1000 * serial:18.2f} {1000 * batched:24.2f} {serial / batched:8.0f}x")

Reading this output

With identical arithmetic, the batched version is 6 to 9 times faster on two CPU cores, and the gap is about the same at every length: the chain pays a fixed cost per step (a small matrix-vector product cannot keep the cores and vector units busy, and each step waits for the previous one), while one large product gives the matrix library big blocks it can split across cores. A GPU, with thousands of cores, widens the gap to tens or hundreds of times; that is the hardware argument for transformers.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

When to still pick an RNN in 2026:

Situation Pick
Streaming inference, one token at a time, constant memory RNN or state-space model (Mamba, RWKV)
Very long sequences (>1M tokens) where attention memory explodes Linear attention, Mamba 2, Hyena
Tiny always-on device (wake word, sensor stream) Small RNN or state-space model: constant memory per step
Anything else (training, batched inference, context up to 128K) Transformer

State-space models (SSMs) like Mamba are essentially RNNs with structured parameterization that gives them the best of both: O(N) scan memory, parallel training via selective scan. Pure SSMs still trail transformers on tasks that need exact recall from far back in the context, which is why the production versions are hybrids that keep a few attention layers: AI21's Jamba, NVIDIA's Nemotron-H, IBM's Granite 4.0 and Qwen3-Next. Recurrence is not dead; it is a component.

A GRU and a self-attention layer on this CPU

torch.nn.GRU against torch.nn.MultiheadAttention (4 heads), both width 256, batch 8, forward pass only, from 128 to 4,096 tokens; the last column is the size of one head's score matrix.

import time, torch
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
torch.manual_seed(0); torch.set_num_threads(2)

# A real recurrent layer against a real self-attention layer, same width, on this page's CPU.
d, B = 256, 8
gru = torch.nn.GRU(d, d, batch_first=True)
attn = torch.nn.MultiheadAttention(d, num_heads=4, batch_first=True)
def best_ms(f, reps=3):
    ts = []
    for _ in range(reps):
        t0 = time.perf_counter(); f(); ts.append(time.perf_counter() - t0)
    return 1000 * min(ts)
rows = []
print(f"{'N':>6} {'GRU (ms)':>9} {'attention (ms)':>15} {'ratio':>6}   attention matrix per head")
with torch.no_grad():
    for N in [128, 256, 512, 1024, 2048, 4096]:
        x = torch.randn(B, N, d)
        g = best_ms(lambda: gru(x))
        a = best_ms(lambda: attn(x, x, x, need_weights=False))
        rows.append((N, g, a))
        print(f"{N:>6} {g:9.1f} {a:15.1f} {g / a:6.1f}x   {N * N * 4 / 2**20:8.1f} MiB (fp32)")
Ns, G, A = zip(*rows)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.5, 3.6))
ax.loglog(Ns, G, "o-", label="GRU (one step after another)")
ax.loglog(Ns, A, "s-", label="self-attention (all positions at once)")
ax.loglog(Ns, [A[-1] * (n / Ns[-1]) ** 2 for n in Ns], ":", c="grey", label="∝ N² (attention's growth)")
ax.loglog(Ns, [G[0] * (n / Ns[0]) for n in Ns], "--", c="grey", label="∝ N (recurrence's growth)")
ax.set_xlabel("sequence length N"); ax.set_ylabel("forward time (ms), batch 8, width 256"); ax.legend(fontsize=8)
ax.set_xticks(Ns); ax.set_xticklabels([str(n) for n in Ns]); ax.minorticks_off()
ax.set_title("On a CPU: the GRU grows linearly, attention quadratically"); plt.tight_layout()

The GRU's time grows linearly, about 0.06–0.07 ms per token at every length (around 9 ms for 128 tokens, 250–300 ms for 4,096). Attention starts about twice as fast at 128 tokens but grows roughly with N2, and somewhere between 1,000 and 2,000 tokens the GRU overtakes it on this CPU (attention takes about 3× longer at 4,096 tokens, where one head's score matrix alone is 64 MiB). Timings vary a little between runs. That is the trade: attention buys parallel training with O(N2) work, and long contexts on any hardware need the efficient-attention methods of Lessons 7.12 and 7.15.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Transformers are the production default for language, vision, speech and multimodal models; the remaining engineering is about attention's cost: FlashAttention kernels, grouped-query attention and KV-cache compression (Lessons 7.12 and 7.15) in every serving engine (vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM).
  • Hybrids bring recurrence back. NVIDIA's Nemotron-H, IBM's Granite 4.0 (October 2025), AI21's Jamba and Qwen3-Next replace most attention layers with Mamba or linear-attention layers to cut memory and speed up long contexts, keeping some attention layers for exact recall.
  • Small RNNs ship on devices for wake words, keyboards and sensor streams, where constant memory per step matters more than parallel training.

Sources: FlashAttention-3, Nemotron-H, IBM Granite 4.0, Jamba, Qwen3-Next, Kaplan et al. 2020.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Recurrence forces N dependent steps; attention computes every position at once.
  • Same arithmetic is much faster when it has no chain of dependencies (the run under Step 3).
  • Attention gives every position a one-step path to every other; recurrence passes signals through every step in between.
  • Attention's price is the N×N score matrix: quadratic time and, without FlashAttention, quadratic memory.
  • Production models are transformers, with recurrent layers returning inside hybrids.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Several times faster here, far more on a GPU

Several times faster even here: 6 to 9 times at every length, with exactly the same number of operations. The chain of small matrix-vector products cannot use the cores and vector units fully, and each step waits for the previous one; one large matrix product can. On a GPU the gap grows to tens or hundreds of times.

SolutionB1 · LayerNorm makes the sum constant

The encoder layer ends with LayerNorm, whose output has mean zero across the feature dimension before its learned scale and shift. Summing the features therefore gives (almost) a constant, the sum of the shift parameters, and a constant has zero gradient with respect to the input. The LSTM has no final normalisation, so its sum still depends on the input. Reading the output through a random direction measures the dependence fairly for both.
