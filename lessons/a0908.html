In plain words first
The idea in one line

A2C throws away each rollout after one update. PPO wraps the policy gradient in a clipped importance ratio so you can do 10+ epochs on the same data without the policy exploding. Schulman et al. (2017). Still the default policy-gradient algorithm in 2026.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
Importance ratio "r_t(θ)" $\pi_\theta(a \mid s) / \pi_{\text{old}}(a \mid s)$; deviation from the policy that collected the data.
Clipped surrogate "PPO's main trick" $\min(r \cdot A, \mathop{\mathrm{clip}}(r, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon) \cdot A)$; flat gradient past the clip on beneficial side.
Trust region "TRPO / PPO intent" Limit each update's KL to guarantee monotone improvement.
KL penalty "Soft trust region" Alternative PPO: $L - \beta \cdot \mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \Vert \pi_{\text{old}})$. Adaptive $\beta$.
Clip fraction "How often clipping triggers" Diagnostic — should be 0.1-0.3; outside means mistuned.
Multi-epoch training "Data reuse" K epochs on each rollout; variance cost traded for sample efficiency.
On-policy-ish "Mostly on-policy" PPO is nominally on-policy but K>1 epochs uses slightly-off-policy data safely.
PPO-KL "The other PPO" KL-penalty variant; used in RLHF where KL-to-reference is already a constraint.

The problem

In plain words first

Policy-gradient methods learn from experience collected by the current policy, and after one update that experience is already slightly out of date. Reusing it for several more updates saves a lot of data, but if the policy changes too much, the old experience no longer describes what the new policy would do, and the updates can wreck a good policy. PPO (proximal policy optimisation) keeps each round of updates small in a simple way: it measures how much more or less likely each action has become, and once that ratio moves more than about 20% in the helpful direction, the action stops contributing to the update. It is the default policy-gradient method in robotics and was the original algorithm for training chat models from human feedback. This lesson runs the lesson's PPO on the grid world, computes exactly how far PPO's objective can be trusted, and reads PPO's standard diagnostics with Stable-Baselines3. No neural network is trained.

A2C (Lesson 9.7) is on-policy: the gradient Eπθ​​[A⋅∇logπθ​] requires data sampled from the current πθ​. Take one update, and πθ​ changes; the data you used is now off-policy. Re-use it and your gradient is biased.

Rollouts are expensive. On Atari, one rollout across 8 envs × 128 steps = 1024 transitions and a dozen seconds of environment time. Throwing that away after one gradient step is wasteful.

Trust Region Policy Optimization (TRPO, Schulman 2015) was the first fix: constrain each update so the KL divergence between old and new policy stays below δ. Theoretically clean, but requires a conjugate-gradient solve per update. TRPO is rarely used today.

PPO (Schulman et al. 2017) replaces the hard trust-region constraint with a simple clipped objective. One extra line of code. Ten epochs per rollout. No conjugate gradients. Good-enough theoretical guarantees. Nine years later it is still the default policy-gradient algorithm for everything from MuJoCo to RLHF.

The idea

The importance ratio.

rt​(θ)=πθold​​(at​∣st​)πθ​(at​∣st​)​

This is the likelihood ratio of the new policy vs the policy that collected the data. rt​=1 means no change. rt​=2 means the new policy is twice as likely to take at​ as the old.

The clipped surrogate.

LCLIP(θ)=Et​[min(rt​(θ)At​,clip(rt​(θ),1−ϵ,1+ϵ)At​)]

Two terms:

  • If the advantage At​>0 and the ratio tries to grow past 1+ϵ, the clip flattens the gradient — don't push a good action further than +ϵ above old probability.
  • If the advantage At​<0 and the ratio tries to fall below 1−ϵ (meaning we would make a bad action much less likely than the old policy did), the clip flattens the gradient: don't push a bad action further than −ϵ below old probability.

The min handles the other direction: if the ratio has moved in the beneficial direction, you still get the gradient (no clipping on the side that would hurt you).

Typical ϵ=0.2. Plot the objective as a function of rt​: a piecewise-linear function with a flat roof on the "good side" and a flat floor on the "bad side."

The full PPO loss.

L(θ,ϕ)=LCLIP(θ)−cv​⋅(Vϕ​(st​)−Vttarget​)2+ce​⋅H(πθ​(⋅∣st​))

Same actor-critic structure as A2C. Three coefficients, usually cv​=0.5, ce​=0.01, ϵ=0.2.

The training loop.

  1. Collect N×T transitions across N parallel envs for T steps each.
  2. Compute advantages (GAE), freeze them as constants.
  3. Freeze πθold​​ as a snapshot of current πθ​.
  4. For K epochs, for each minibatch of (s, a, A, V_target, log π_old(a|s)): - Compute rt​(θ)=exp(logπθ​(a∣s)−logπold​(a∣s)). - Apply LCLIP + value loss + entropy. - Gradient step.
  5. Discard the rollout. Return to step 1.

K=10 and minibatches of 64 is a standard hyperparameter set. PPO is robust: the exact numbers rarely matter within ±50%.

KL-penalty variant. The original paper proposed an alternative using an adaptive KL penalty: L=LPG−β⋅KL(πθ​∥πold​) with β adjusted based on observed KL. The clipping version became dominant; the KL variant survives in RLHF (where KL to the reference policy is a separate constraint you always want anyway).

The same idea as a formula

Why a surrogate. For a new policy π and the data-collecting policy πold​, the performance difference lemma gives exactly

J(π)−J(πold​)=s∑​dπ(s)a∑​π(a∣s)Aπold​(s,a)

with dπ the discounted state visitation of the new policy. We only have samples from dπold​, so PPO and TRPO optimise the surrogate that swaps dπ for dπold​:

Lπold​​(π)=Es∼dπold​,a∼πold​​[r(s,a)Aπold​(s,a)],r=πold​(a∣s)π(a∣s)​

It is accurate only while π stays close to πold​; TRPO bounds the error by the KL divergence between them.

Clipping. LCLIP=E[min(rA, clip(r,1−ϵ,1+ϵ)A)]: once r leaves [1−ϵ,1+ϵ] in the direction that helps, the sample's gradient is zero; in the direction that hurts, it is never clipped. The result is a pessimistic version of the surrogate.

Diagnostics. Approximate KL E[(r−1)−logr] (non-negative, low-variance), clip fraction Pr(∣r−1∣>ϵ), and explained variance of the critic.

RLHF form. For language models the reward is shaped with a KL term to a frozen reference model: rt​=R1[t=T]−βlogπref​(at​∣st​)π(at​∣st​)​.

Worked example
Clipping by hand

ϵ=0.2, advantage A=+2 for an action whose probability rose from 0.30 to 0.42, so r=1.4:

  • Unclipped term: 1.4×2=2.8; clipped: 1.2×2=2.4; PPO takes min=2.4, a constant in θ: zero gradient. The action has already gained enough.

Same action with A=−2:

  • Unclipped −2.8, clipped −2.4; min=−2.8, the unclipped term, so the gradient still pushes the probability back down. Mistakes are always corrected.

A=+2 but the probability fell to 0.21 (r=0.7): unclipped 1.4, clipped 0.8×2=1.6, min=1.4, unclipped, so the gradient pushes it back up.

Approximate KL by hand for one sample with r=1.4: (1.4−1)−ln1.4=0.4−0.336=0.064.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Clipping limits how far the policy can move."
    ✓ It removes the incentive for samples beyond the clip range; other samples, momentum and many epochs can still push the policy far, which is why KL is monitored.
  • ✗ "The surrogate objective measures how good the new policy is."
    ✓ Only near the old policy; far away the unclipped surrogate can promise five times the improvement that is actually possible (the run after Step 5).
  • ✗ "PPO is off-policy because it reuses data."
    ✓ It reuses one batch for a few epochs while staying close to the data-collecting policy; it is still essentially on-policy.

Step 1 · capture log π_old(a | s) at rollout time

for step in range(T):
    probs = softmax(logits(theta, state_features(s)))
    a = sample(probs, rng)
    s_next, r, done = env.step(s, a)
    buffer.append({
        "s": s, "a": a, "r": r, "done": done,
        "v_old": value(w, state_features(s)),
        "log_pi_old": log(probs[a] + 1e-12),
    })
    s = s_next

The snapshot is taken once, at rollout time. It does not change during the update epochs.

Step 2 · compute GAE advantages (Lesson 07)

Same as A2C. Normalize across the batch.

Step 3 · clipped surrogate update

for _ in range(K_EPOCHS):
    for mb in minibatches(buffer, size=64):
        for rec in mb:
            x = state_features(rec["s"])
            probs = softmax(logits(theta, x))
            logp = log(probs[rec["a"]] + 1e-12)
            ratio = exp(logp - rec["log_pi_old"])
            adv = rec["advantage"]
            surrogate = min(
                ratio * adv,
                clamp(ratio, 1 - EPS, 1 + EPS) * adv,
            )
            # backprop -surrogate, add value loss, subtract entropy
            grad_logpi = onehot(rec["a"]) - probs
            if (adv > 0 and ratio >= 1 + EPS) or (adv < 0 and ratio <= 1 - EPS):
                pg_grad = 0.0  # clipped
            else:
                pg_grad = ratio * adv
            for i in range(N_ACTIONS):
                for j in range(N_FEAT):
                    theta[i][j] += LR * pg_grad * grad_logpi[i] * x[j]

The "clipped → zero gradient" pattern is the heart of PPO. If the new policy has already drifted too far in the beneficial direction, the update stops.

Step 4 · value and entropy

Add standard MSE to the critic target and an entropy bonus on the actor, same as A2C.

Step 5 · diagnostics

Three things to watch every update:

  • Mean KL E[logπold​−logπθ​]. Should stay in [0, 0.02]. If it blows past 0.1, reduce K_EPOCHS or LR.
  • Clip fraction — the fraction of samples whose ratio lies outside [1−ϵ,1+ϵ]. Should be ~0.1-0.3. If ~0, the clip never triggers → raise LR or K_EPOCHS. If ~0.5+, you are over-fitting the rollout → lower them.
  • Explained variance 1−Var(Vtarget​−Vpred​)/Var(Vtarget​). Critic quality metric. Should climb toward 1 as the critic learns.

How far the surrogate can be trusted

Everything exact, by linear algebra: from the uniform-random policy, the logits are moved by increasing amounts along the exact policy gradient; for each step, the true improvement, the unclipped surrogate, PPO's clipped surrogate, the KL divergence, and the share of state-action pairs whose probability ratio left 1±0.2.

import numpy as np, runpy
m = runpy.run_path("main.py")                            # the lesson's grid world (step, TERMINAL)
gamma, nA = 0.99, 4
cells = [(r, c) for r in range(4) for c in range(4) if (r, c) != m["TERMINAL"]]; ix = {s: i for i, s in enumerate(cells)}; nS = 15
T = np.zeros((nS, nA, nS)); R = np.zeros((nS, nA))
for s in cells:
    for a in range(nA):
        s2, r, d = m["step"](s, a); R[ix[s], a] = r
        if not d: T[ix[s], a, ix[s2]] = 1.0
softmax = lambda z: np.exp(z - z.max(1, keepdims=True)) / np.exp(z - z.max(1, keepdims=True)).sum(1, keepdims=True)
def exact(pi):
    """V, Q, advantages and discounted state visitation from the start, all by linear algebra."""
    P = np.einsum("sa,sat->st", pi, T); r = (pi * R).sum(1)
    V = np.linalg.solve(np.eye(nS) - gamma * P, r)
    Q = R + gamma * T @ V
    d = np.linalg.solve(np.eye(nS) - gamma * P.T, np.eye(nS)[ix[(0, 0)]])   # sum_t gamma^t P(s_t = s)
    return V, Q, Q - V[:, None], d
z_old = np.zeros((nS, nA)); pi_old = softmax(z_old)       # the uniform-random policy
V_old, _, A_old, d_old = exact(pi_old)
g = d_old[:, None] * pi_old * A_old; g /= np.abs(g).max()  # policy-gradient direction in the logits
print(f"old policy: uniform, J = V(start) = {V_old[0]:.2f}")
print(" step | true J(new) - J(old) | surrogate (no clip) | clipped surrogate | KL(old || new) | share of (s,a) outside 1 +/- 0.2")
for step in (0.25, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 8):
    pi_new = softmax(z_old + step * g)
    V_new = exact(pi_new)[0]
    rho = pi_new / pi_old
    surr = (d_old[:, None] * pi_new * A_old).sum()
    clip = (d_old[:, None] * pi_old * np.minimum(rho * A_old, np.clip(rho, 0.8, 1.2) * A_old)).sum()
    kl = (d_old / d_old.sum() * (pi_old * np.log(pi_old / pi_new)).sum(1)).sum()
    print(f"{step:5.2f} | {V_new[0] - V_old[0]:20.2f} | {surr:19.2f} | {clip:17.2f} | {kl:14.3f} | {np.mean(np.abs(rho - 1) > 0.2):10.0%}")

Reading this output

For small steps all three numbers agree roughly: at a step of 0.25 the true improvement is 10.2 and the surrogates say 12.5 and 11.7, with almost no ratios outside 1±0.2. As the step grows, the unclipped surrogate runs away: it promises 52 at step 1 and 160 at step 8, while the true improvement can never exceed 33.6 (from −39.4 to the optimal −5.85) and is actually 24.5 and 33.4. The surrogate still assumes the old policy's state visits; the new policy visits different states, so it overcounts. PPO's clipped surrogate stays pessimistic, never claiming more than about 21 and falling again for large steps as more ratios leave the clip range, so maximising it does not reward giant steps. The KL column shows why practitioners watch it: 0.05 at step 1, 2 at step 8.

Pitfalls

  • Clip coefficient mistuned. ϵ=0.2 is the de-facto standard. Going to 0.1 makes updates too timid; 0.3+ invites instability.
  • Too many epochs. K>20 routinely destabilizes because the policy drifts far from πold​. Cap epochs, especially for large networks.
  • No reward normalization. Large reward scales eat into the clip range. Normalize rewards (running std) before computing advantages.
  • Forgetting advantage normalization. Per-batch zero-mean/unit-std normalization is standard. Skipping it wrecks PPO on most benchmarks.
  • Learning rate not decayed. PPO benefits from linear LR decay to zero. Constant LR is often worse.
  • Importance ratio math errors. Always exp(log_new - log_old) for numerical stability, not new / old.
  • Wrong gradient sign. Maximize the surrogate = minimize −LCLIP. A flipped sign is the most common PPO bug.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

PPO is 2026's default RL algorithm across a surprising number of domains:

Use case PPO variant
MuJoCo / robotics control PPO with Gaussian policy, GAE(0.95)
Atari / discrete games PPO with categorical policy, rolling 128-step rollouts
RLHF for LLMs PPO with KL penalty to reference model, reward from RM at end of response
Large-scale game agents PPO at scale (OpenAI Five); IMPALA-style V-trace and UPGO (AlphaStar)
Reasoning LLMs GRPO (Lesson 9.9) — PPO's clipped objective without a critic
Preference-only data DPO — the KL-regularised objective solved in closed form, no online sampling

The PPO loss shape — clipped surrogate + value + entropy — is the scaffolding for DPO, GRPO, and nearly every RLHF pipeline.

PPO's diagnostics with Stable-Baselines3

A freshly initialised Stable-Baselines3 PPO policy collects 2,048 CartPole steps; GAE uses its critic. The weights are then moved by increasing amounts along the gradient of PPO's clipped objective (a temporary change, undone afterwards; no training), and the clipped surrogate, the approximate KL SB3 logs, and the clip fraction are read off.

import numpy as np, torch, gymnasium as gym
from stable_baselines3 import PPO

torch.manual_seed(0)
env = gym.make("CartPole-v1")
model = PPO("MlpPolicy", env, gamma=0.99, gae_lambda=0.95, seed=0, device="cpu")   # Stable-Baselines3's PPO, freshly initialised
pol, gamma, lam = model.policy, model.gamma, model.gae_lambda
print(f"SB3 PPO policy (initialised, not trained): {sum(p.numel() for p in pol.parameters()):,} parameters; gamma {gamma}, GAE lambda {lam}")

# 1. Collect one rollout with the trained policy (sampling, as during training) and compute GAE with its own critic.
obs_l, act_l, rew_l, done_l, val_l, logp_l = [], [], [], [], [], []
obs, _ = env.reset(seed=0)
for t in range(2048):
    o = torch.as_tensor(obs, dtype=torch.float32)[None]
    with torch.no_grad():
        dist = pol.get_distribution(o); a = dist.get_actions(); v = pol.predict_values(o).item()
    obs2, r, term, trunc, _ = env.step(int(a))
    obs_l.append(obs); act_l.append(int(a)); rew_l.append(r); done_l.append(term); val_l.append(v)
    logp_l.append(dist.log_prob(a).item()); obs = obs2
    if term or trunc: obs, _ = env.reset(seed=t + 1)
O = torch.as_tensor(np.array(obs_l), dtype=torch.float32); Acts = torch.as_tensor(act_l)
adv = np.zeros(2048); g = 0.0
for t in reversed(range(2048)):
    nv = 0.0 if done_l[t] or t == 2047 else val_l[t + 1]
    delta = rew_l[t] + gamma * nv - val_l[t]; g = delta + gamma * lam * (0 if done_l[t] else g); adv[t] = g
ret = adv + np.array(val_l)
print(f"rollout of 2,048 steps: {sum(done_l)} episodes ended (pole fell), {2048 / max(sum(done_l), 1):.0f} steps each on average")
A = torch.as_tensor((adv - adv.mean()) / (adv.std() + 1e-8), dtype=torch.float32); logp_old = torch.as_tensor(logp_l)

# 2. Move the policy by different amounts along the clipped-surrogate gradient (copies only; nothing is trained)
#    and read PPO's diagnostics: approximate KL and clip fraction.
def surrogate(p):
    _, logp, _ = p.evaluate_actions(O, Acts); ratio = torch.exp(logp - logp_old)
    return torch.min(ratio * A, torch.clamp(ratio, 0.8, 1.2) * A).mean(), ratio, logp
pol.zero_grad(); s0, _, _ = surrogate(pol); s0.backward()
grads = [p.grad.clone() if p.grad is not None else torch.zeros_like(p) for p in pol.parameters()]   # the critic gets none
direction = [g_ / torch.cat([x.flatten() for x in grads]).norm() for g_ in grads]
pol.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
print(" step size | surrogate | approx KL | clip fraction")
original = [p.detach().clone() for p in pol.parameters()]
for step in (0.0, 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0):
    with torch.no_grad():
        for p, p0, d in zip(pol.parameters(), original, direction): p.copy_(p0 + step * d)   # moved copy of the weights
        s, ratio, logp = surrogate(pol)
        kl = ((ratio - 1) - torch.log(ratio)).mean()            # the k3 estimator SB3 reports as approx_kl
    print(f"{step:10.2f} | {s:9.4f} | {kl:9.4f} | {(torch.abs(ratio - 1) > 0.2).float().mean():13.2f}")
with torch.no_grad():
    for p, p0 in zip(pol.parameters(), original): p.copy_(p0)                            # put the weights back

Break it on purpose

Replace min(ratio * adv, clamp(ratio, 1 - EPS, 1 + EPS) * adv) with just the clipped term, clamp(ratio, 1 - EPS, 1 + EPS) * adv. It looks equivalent and is one line shorter. Which samples now behave differently, and what can go wrong? (Solution B1.)

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • PPO in control and games. PPO remains the default on-policy algorithm in Stable-Baselines3, CleanRL, RLlib and GPU-simulation stacks (Isaac Lab, MuJoCo Playground, Brax), where thousands of parallel environments feed it.
  • PPO and its descendants in LLM training. InstructGPT-style RLHF used PPO with a learned critic; current reasoning-model training mostly uses critic-free variants with the same clipped ratio (GRPO, DAPO's decoupled clipping, Dr. GRPO, GSPO's sequence-level ratios) in TRL, verl and OpenRLHF.
  • Diagnostics are standard telemetry. Approximate KL, clip fraction, entropy and explained variance are logged by every major implementation, and KL-based early stopping per epoch is common.

Sources: PPO paper, The 37 implementation details of PPO, Stable-Baselines3 PPO, DAPO, GSPO, verl.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Reuse a rollout for a few epochs, but keep the new policy near the old one.
  • Surrogate E[rA] is accurate only near πold​; clipping makes it pessimistic beyond 1±ϵ.
  • The min keeps gradients that correct mistakes; only "already improved enough" samples are clipped.
  • Watch approximate KL, clip fraction and explained variance every update.
  • Critic-free PPO variants (GRPO and successors) train today's reasoning models.

medium25 minimplement

PPO trains a policy for several epochs on one batch of samples. The importance ratio compares the new policy with the policy that collected the samples. PPO clips this ratio, so that one update cannot move the policy too far. The same clipped loss is the core of many RLHF pipelines.

Task

Write three functions. Each takes the new log probabilities, the old log probabilities and the advantages of N samples, and the clip size eps.

  1. ppo_clip_loss(logp_new, logp_old, advantages, eps=0.2) returns the loss to minimize.
  2. clip_fraction(logp_new, logp_old, advantages, eps=0.2) returns the fraction of clipped samples.
  3. ppo_clip_grad(logp_new, logp_old, advantages, eps=0.2) returns the gradient of the loss with respect to logp_new.

The ratio of sample t is rt​=exp(logπnew,t​−logπold,t​). The loss is:

L=−N1​t∑​min(rt​At​, clip(rt​,1−ϵ,1+ϵ)At​)

Sample t is clipped when At​>0 and rt​>1+ϵ, or when At​<0 and rt​<1−ϵ. Both comparisons are strict. A sample with At​=0 is not clipped.

The gradient of a clipped sample is 0. The gradient of any other sample is −rt​At​/N.

  • Log probabilities are from -1000 to 0.
  • Do not normalize the advantages.
  • Do not change the input arrays.
  • Use numpy only.

Your code
Common mistakes the checks catch
  • Counts every ratio outside [1 - eps, 1 + eps] as clipped, also when the min keeps the unclipped term.
  • Returns minus r A / N for every sample, also for the clipped samples.
  • Takes the larger of the two terms with np.maximum, so the objective is not a lower bound.
  • Computes the ratio as exp(logp_new) / exp(logp_old), which gives 0 / 0 for very small probabilities.
  • Returns the surrogate and its gradient without the minus sign, so gradient descent makes the policy worse.
  • Sums over the samples and does not divide the loss and the gradient by N.
Hint 1

Compute the ratios from the difference of the log probabilities. Do not compute np.exp(logp_new) / np.exp(logp_old), because both exponentials can be 0.

Hint 2

np.clip(r, 1 - eps, 1 + eps) gives the clipped ratio. np.minimum takes the smaller term for each sample. PPO maximizes the mean of the min, so the loss is minus that mean.

Hint 3

Make one boolean mask for the clipped samples. Use it for the clip fraction and for the gradient. The fraction is the mean of the mask.

Hint 4

On an unclipped sample the term is rA, and dr/dlogπnew​=r. On a clipped sample the term is a constant. So the gradient is −rA/N or 0. np.where selects between the two.

Reference solution and notes
import numpy as np


def ratios(logp_new, logp_old):
    return np.exp(np.asarray(logp_new, dtype=float) - np.asarray(logp_old, dtype=float))


def clipped_mask(ratio, advantages, eps):
    return ((advantages > 0) & (ratio > 1 + eps)) | ((advantages < 0) & (ratio < 1 - eps))


def ppo_clip_loss(logp_new, logp_old, advantages, eps=0.2):
    advantages = np.asarray(advantages, dtype=float)
    ratio = ratios(logp_new, logp_old)
    surrogate = np.minimum(ratio * advantages, np.clip(ratio, 1 - eps, 1 + eps) * advantages)
    return float(-np.mean(surrogate))


def clip_fraction(logp_new, logp_old, advantages, eps=0.2):
    advantages = np.asarray(advantages, dtype=float)
    ratio = ratios(logp_new, logp_old)
    return float(np.mean(clipped_mask(ratio, advantages, eps)))


def ppo_clip_grad(logp_new, logp_old, advantages, eps=0.2):
    advantages = np.asarray(advantages, dtype=float)
    ratio = ratios(logp_new, logp_old)
    grad = np.where(clipped_mask(ratio, advantages, eps), 0.0, -ratio * advantages)
    return grad / advantages.size

Why the min

The clip removes the reward for a move past 1±ϵ in the good direction. The min keeps the full penalty for a move in the bad direction. So the clipped objective is a lower bound on the plain objective. PPO improves a pessimistic estimate, and a large step cannot look better than it is.

Why the gradient is 0 on a clipped sample

A clipped term is (1±ϵ)A, and this value does not depend on the new policy. So the sample stops to push. The other samples still update the policy. This is how PPO can run many epochs on one batch.

The traps

The most common bug is a flipped sign. PPO maximizes the surrogate, so the loss for gradient descent is minus the surrogate. The second trap is the ratio. Always take the exponential of the difference of the log probabilities. The third trap is the clip fraction. Count only the samples where the clip changes the gradient, not every ratio outside the interval.

Where this goes next

A full PPO loss adds a value loss and an entropy bonus. The lesson normalizes the advantages in each minibatch before this step. GRPO and other RLHF methods use the same clipped ratio with other advantages.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Over 100

Over 100: the unclipped surrogate claims an improvement of 160 at the largest step, nearly five times the maximum possible 33.6 and five times the true 33.4. The surrogate weights the new policy's action probabilities by the old policy's advantages and the old policy's state visits; once the policy changes a lot it goes to different states, and those weights mean nothing. This is the reason for trust regions: TRPO limits KL directly, PPO clips the ratio so that the objective it maximises stays pessimistic (about 20 here) and stops rewarding large moves.

SolutionB1 · Mistakes stop being corrected

With the plain clipped term, every sample whose ratio has left [0.8,1.2] gets zero gradient, including those that moved in the harmful direction: an action with negative advantage whose probability has risen to r=1.4 contributes 1.2A, a constant, so nothing pushes it back down; likewise a good action whose probability has fallen below 0.8. The min in PPO is asymmetric on purpose: it removes the incentive to keep improving a sample past the limit, but keeps the full gradient whenever the policy has moved the wrong way. Without it, an unlucky epoch can push some probabilities in the wrong direction and leave them there, and the policy can degrade while the loss looks stable.
