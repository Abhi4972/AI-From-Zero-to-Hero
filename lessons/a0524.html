The problem

In plain words first

Coreference resolution works out which words in a text refer to the same thing. In "Apple announced new phones. The company said they will ship soon", "Apple" and "the company" are one entity and "new phones" and "they" are another. The output is a set of groups (clusters) of mentions, one group per real-world entity. Search, summarization, question answering and knowledge graphs all need this: without it, "she" is a dead end and one company becomes three. This lesson describes the mention-ranking model behind modern systems, runs the lesson's rule-based resolver on easy and famously hard sentences, and builds the three standard scores (MUC, B³ and CEAF) from scratch. Scoring a clustering turns out to be subtle: the scores disagree sharply on simple degenerate systems, which is exactly why the field reports their average.

Extract every mention of Apple Inc. from a 300-word article. Easy when the article says "Apple." Hard when it says "the company," "they," "Cupertino's technology giant," or "Jobs's firm." Without resolving these mentions to the same entity, your NER pipeline misses 60-80% of the mentions.

Coreference resolution links every expression that refers to the same real-world entity into one cluster. It is the glue between surface-level NLP (NER, parsing) and downstream semantics (IE, QA, summarization, KG).

Why it matters in 2026:

  • Summarization: "The CEO announced..." vs "Tim Cook announced..." — the summary should name the CEO.
  • Question answering: "Who did she call?" requires resolving "she."
  • Information extraction: a knowledge graph with "PER1 founded Apple" and "Jobs founded Apple" as separate entries is wrong.
  • Multi-document IE: merging mentions across articles about the same event is cross-document coreference.

The idea

The task. Input: a document. Output: a clustering of mentions (spans) where each cluster refers to one entity.

Mention types.

  • Named entity. "Tim Cook"
  • Nominal. "the CEO", "the company"
  • Pronominal. "he", "she", "they", "it"
  • Appositive. "Tim Cook, Apple's CEO,"

Architectures.

  1. Rule-based (Hobbs, 1978). Syntactic-tree-based pronoun resolution using grammar rules. Good baseline, and for decades surprisingly hard to beat on pronouns (Lappin and Leass, 1994, and later learned systems took many years to pull clearly ahead).
  2. Mention-pair classifier. For every pair of mentions (mi​,mj​), predict whether they corefer. Cluster by transitive closure. Standard pre-2016.
  3. Mention-ranking. For each mention, rank candidate antecedents (including "no antecedent"). Pick the top.
  4. Span-based end-to-end (Lee et al., 2017). Transformer encoder. Enumerate all candidate spans up to a length cap. Predict mention scores. Predict antecedent-probability for each span. Cluster greedily. The modern default.
  5. Generative (2024+). Prompt an LLM: "List every pronoun in this text and its antecedent." Works well on easy cases, struggles on long documents and rare referents.

The evaluation metrics. Five standard metrics (MUC, B³, CEAF, BLANC, LEA) because no single metric captures clustering quality. Report the average of the first three as CoNLL F1. State of the art on the English CoNLL-2012 test set is around 83 to 84 CoNLL F1 (for example Maverick, ACL 2024, with a much smaller model than earlier leaders).

Known hard cases.

  • Definite descriptions referring to entities introduced pages earlier.
  • Bridging anaphora ("the wheels" → a previously mentioned car).
  • Zero anaphora in languages like Chinese and Japanese.
  • Cataphora (pronoun before referent): "When she walked in, Mary smiled."

The same idea as a formula

Mention ranking (the end-to-end model of Lee et al., 2017). Every span i up to a maximum width gets a representation gi​ from a transformer (its boundary tokens plus an attention-weighted sum of its tokens). Each span gets a mention score sm​(i)=wm⊤​FFNN(gi​) and each pair an antecedent score sa​(i,j)=wa⊤​FFNN([gi​;gj​;gi​∘gj​;ϕ(i,j)]), where ϕ encodes distance and speaker. The coreference score is

s(i,j)=sm​(i)+sm​(j)+sa​(i,j),s(i,ε)=0

and each span picks an antecedent among earlier spans or the dummy ε ("starts a new entity, or is not a mention"):

P(yi​=j)=∑j′∈{ε,1,…,i−1}​exps(i,j′)exps(i,j)​

Training maximises the probability of any correct antecedent, log∑j∈GOLD(i)​P(yi​=j). Clusters are the connected components of the chosen links. There are O(T2) spans and O(T4) span pairs, so low-scoring spans are pruned early.

Evaluation. Let K be the gold clusters (key) and R the system clusters (response). - MUC counts links: recall =∑k∈K​(∣k∣−1)∑k∈K​(∣k∣−∣p(k)∣)​, where p(k) is the set of pieces k is split into by R; precision swaps the roles. It ignores singletons. - B³ averages over mentions: for mention m in gold cluster km​ and system cluster rm​, precision =∣rm​∣∣km​∩rm​∣​, recall =∣km​∣∣km​∩rm​∣​. - CEAF-ϕ4​ aligns gold and system clusters one-to-one (Hungarian algorithm) maximising ∑ϕ4​(k,r) with ϕ4​(k,r)=∣k∣+∣r∣2∣k∩r∣​, then divides by the number of gold (recall) or system (precision) clusters. - CoNLL F1 is the mean of the three F1 scores.

Worked example
MUC by hand

Gold: {Apple, the company, it} and {phones, them}. A system outputs {Apple, the company}, {it, phones, them}.

  • Recall. Gold cluster 1 (size 3) is split by the system into 2 pieces ({Apple, the company} and {it}): 3−2=1 correct link out of 3−1=2. Gold cluster 2 (size 2) stays in 1 piece: 2−1=1 out of 1. Recall =(1+1)/(2+1)=0.667.
  • Precision (swap roles). System cluster 1 (size 2) lies in one gold cluster: 1/1. System cluster 2 (size 3) is split by gold into {it} and {phones, them}: 3−2=1 out of 2. Precision =(1+1)/(1+2)=0.667.
  • MUC F1 =0.667. The one error, attaching "it" to the phones, costs a third of the links on each side.

  • ✗ "Coreference is pronoun resolution."
    ✓ Pronouns are one case; names, descriptions ("Cupertino's technology giant") and appositives also have to be linked, and they are often harder.
  • ✗ "One clustering score is enough."
    ✓ The scores reward different things; under "Use it", putting every mention in its own cluster scores 0 on MUC and well on B³.
  • ✗ "Gender and number agreement settle most pronouns."
    ✓ Many pronouns are ambiguous on agreement alone; "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big" needs world knowledge to resolve "it".

Step 2 · rule-based pronoun resolver (teaching)

See code/main.py for a stdlib-only implementation:

  1. Extract mentions: named entities (capitalized spans), pronouns (dict lookup), definite descriptions ("the X").
  2. For each pronoun, look at the previous K mentions and score them by: - gender/number agreement (heuristic) - recency (closer wins) - syntactic role (subjects preferred)
  3. Link the highest-scoring antecedent.

Not competitive with neural models. But it shows the search space and the decisions an end-to-end model must make.

Break it on purpose

In the lesson's agreement_score, remove the gender check entirely (so only number agreement and recency count), and resolve "Mary Chen joined Apple last year. She leads the AI team." What does "She" link to now, and why? (Solution B1.)

Step 3 · using LLMs for coreference

prompt = f"""Text: {text}

List every pronoun and noun phrase that refers to a person or company.
Cluster them by what they refer to. Output JSON:
[{{"entity": "Apple", "mentions": ["Apple", "the company", "it"]}}, ...]
"""

Two failure modes to watch. First, LLMs over-merge ("him" and "her" referring to two distinct people). Second, LLMs silently drop mentions in long documents. Always verify with span-offset checks.

Step 4 · evaluation

The standard conll-2012 script computes MUC, B³, CEAF-φ4 and reports the average. For an in-house eval, start with span-level precision and recall on your annotated test set, then add mention-linking F1.

Pitfalls

  • Singleton explosion. Some systems report every mention as its own cluster. B³ is lenient. MUC punishes this. Always check all three metrics.
  • Pronouns in long context. Accuracy drops on long documents, where antecedents can be pages away and models process text in windows. Chunk carefully and merge clusters across windows.
  • Gender assumptions. Hard-coded gender rules break on non-binary referents, organizations, animals. Use learned models or neutral scoring.
  • LLM drift on long docs. A single API call cannot reliably cluster mentions across 50+ paragraphs. Use sliding-window + merge.

Reading this output

On the lesson's own document, "He" goes to Tim Cook and "She" to Mary Chen, as they should. Then the cracks show. "It will ship in June" means the laptop, but "It" links to "The company": both candidates sit in the previous sentence, the recency score cannot tell them apart, and the tie goes to the first one. "Her" links to "AI", because the capitalisation rule treated the acronym in "the AI team" as a name of unknown gender. The hard cases fail in instructive ways. Cataphora: "she" comes before "Mary", the resolver only looks backwards, and the only earlier capitalised word is "When", so it links "she" to a sentence-initial word. Winograd: "trophy" and "suitcase" are not in the list of known nouns, so "it" gets no antecedent at all, and even with them, agreement could not decide; a reader knows that the thing that is too big is the one that does not fit. Unknown names: "they" links to "Jordan Kim" because unknown gender agrees with everything. Each fix would be another rule; this is why learned models took over.

Scoring a clustering: MUC, B³ and CEAF from scratch

A gold clustering of 12 mentions into 4 entities, and four system outputs: a good one with one error, every mention alone, everything in one cluster, and one with two entities merged. Each metric is implemented from the formulas above.

import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

gold = [{"Apple", "the company", "it", "Apple Inc"}, {"Tim Cook", "he", "its CEO"}, {"new phones", "them", "the devices"}, {"Mary", "she"}]
mentions = set().union(*gold)
systems = {"one error": [{"Apple", "the company", "Apple Inc"}, {"Tim Cook", "he", "its CEO", "it"}, {"new phones", "them", "the devices"}, {"Mary", "she"}],
           "all singletons": [{m} for m in mentions],
           "one big cluster": [set(mentions)],
           "two entities merged": [gold[0] | gold[2], gold[1], gold[3]]}

def muc(key, resp):
    def score(K, R):
        num = den = 0
        for k in K:
            pieces = {next((i for i, r in enumerate(R) if m in r), ("alone", m)) for m in k}
            num += len(k) - len(pieces); den += len(k) - 1
        return num / den if den else 0.0
    r, p = score(key, resp), score(resp, key)
    return p, r, (2 * p * r / (p + r) if p + r else 0.0)
def b3(key, resp):
    kof = {m: k for k in key for m in k}; rof = {m: r for r in resp for m in r}
    p = np.mean([len(kof[m] & rof[m]) / len(rof[m]) for m in mentions])
    r = np.mean([len(kof[m] & rof[m]) / len(kof[m]) for m in mentions])
    return p, r, 2 * p * r / (p + r)
def ceaf(key, resp):
    S = np.array([[2 * len(k & r) / (len(k) + len(r)) for r in resp] for k in key])
    rows, cols = linear_sum_assignment(-S)
    total = S[rows, cols].sum()
    p, r = total / len(resp), total / len(key)
    return p, r, (2 * p * r / (p + r) if p + r else 0.0)

scores = {}
print(f"{'':22s}{'MUC F1':>8s}{'B3 F1':>8s}{'CEAF F1':>9s}{'CoNLL':>8s}")
for name, sys_ in systems.items():
    f = [muc(gold, sys_)[2], b3(gold, sys_)[2], ceaf(gold, sys_)[2]]
    scores[name] = f + [np.mean(f)]
    print(f"{name:22s}" + "".join(f"{v:8.3f}" for v in scores[name][:2]) + f"{scores[name][2]:9.3f}{scores[name][3]:8.3f}")
print(f"\nworked example check, MUC: {muc([{'Apple', 'the company', 'it'}, {'phones', 'them'}], [{'Apple', 'the company'}, {'it', 'phones', 'them'}])}")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 3.2))
x = np.arange(len(systems))
for k, (lab, colour) in enumerate(zip(["MUC", "B3", "CEAF-phi4", "CoNLL (mean)"], ("#b24a1d", "#474d9a", "#24753a", "#566070"))):
    ax.bar(x + (k - 1.5) * 0.2, [scores[n][k] for n in systems], 0.19, label=lab, color=colour)
ax.set_xticks(x, list(systems), fontsize=8); ax.set_ylabel("F1"); ax.set_ylim(0, 1.05); ax.legend(frameon=False, fontsize=8, ncol=4)
ax.grid(axis="y", alpha=0.3); ax.set_title("Degenerate systems fool one metric each", fontsize=9)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

Reading this output

The worked example checks out (MUC 0.667 each way). The degenerate systems expose each metric's blind spot. All singletons: MUC 0, because there are no links to get right, while B³ still gives 0.50, since every mention's cluster is "pure". One big cluster: MUC gives 0.84, because every gold link is present, while B³ (0.42) and CEAF (0.20) punish the impurity. The most striking row is the third: merging two whole entities scores higher on MUC (0.94) than the system with a single misplaced pronoun (0.88), because MUC only counts the minimum number of links needed and a merge costs few of them. CEAF, with its one-to-one alignment, is the metric that notices the merge. The CoNLL average ranks the good system first and both degenerate ones far below, which is why papers report it rather than any single number.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • LLMs as resolvers. Production pipelines increasingly prompt an LLM to resolve references within a document (or to rewrite pronouns before retrieval); they handle many easy and medium cases well but still over-merge distinct entities and drop mentions in long documents, as the lesson warns.
  • Small trained models for volume. Libraries such as fastcoref and spaCy's experimental coreference component, and open checkpoints such as Maverick, run on CPUs or one GPU when every document must be processed.
  • Coreference inside RAG and knowledge graphs. Resolving "she" and "the company" before chunking or extraction keeps facts attached to the right entity (Lessons 5.23 and 5.26).

Sources: fastcoref, spacy-experimental coref, Maverick, End-to-end Neural Coreference Resolution.

The integration pattern that ships in 2026: run NER first, run coref, merge coref clusters into NER entities. Downstream tasks see one entity per cluster, not one entity per mention.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Coreference groups mentions (names, descriptions, pronouns) into one cluster per entity.
  • Modern systems score spans and antecedents and let each span pick an earlier antecedent or none; clusters are the linked components.
  • Rules (agreement, recency, syntax) fail on cataphora, ambiguous agreement and world-knowledge cases.
  • MUC counts links, B³ averages over mentions, CEAF aligns clusters; each can be gamed, so report all three and their mean.
  • Run coreference after NER and merge clusters into entities for downstream use.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · MUC gives 0; B³ is fooled

MUC counts the links a system gets right; all singletons have no links, so MUC precision, recall and F1 are all 0. B³ scores every mention: each one is "correctly" in a cluster with no wrong members, so B³ precision is perfect and its F1 stays fairly high. CEAF falls in between. The reverse trick, one big cluster, gets full MUC recall and a high MUC F1 while B³ precision collapses. Each metric has a blind spot, which is why CoNLL F1 averages them.

SolutionB1 · Recency takes over

Without the gender check, "Mary Chen" and "Apple" both agree in number with "She", and only recency is left to decide. Both are in the previous sentence, so the decision falls to the token-position term, and "She" can attach to the company. Agreement features are cheap, strong filters; removing one shows how much the resolver leans on them, and how fragile its tie-breaking is (the lesson's own document already shows "It" choosing "The company" over "a laptop" on a tie). The real fix is not more rules but a model that represents what kind of thing each mention is.
