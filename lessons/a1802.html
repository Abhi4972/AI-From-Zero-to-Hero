In plain words first
The idea in one line

Any optimizer strong enough to maximize a proxy reward will find the gap between the proxy and the thing you actually wanted. Gao et al. (ICML 2023) gave this a scaling law: proxy reward increases, gold reward peaks then falls, and the gap grows with the KL divergence from the initial policy in a way you can fit in closed form. Sycophancy, verbosity bias, unfaithful chain-of-thought, and evaluator tampering are not separate problems. They are the same problem in different costumes.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
Goodhart's Law "optimizing a proxy breaks it" Any strong optimizer against an imperfect proxy reliably finds inputs where the proxy-target gap is large
Gold reward "what we actually want" The target the proxy is a noisy measurement of; in practice, a larger-sample RM or human eval
Proxy reward "the RM" The scalar used during training; by construction, it is what the optimizer sees
Over-optimization curve "the reward-hacking U-curve" Proxy climbs, gold peaks then falls as KL from initial policy grows
KL budget "how far we can drift" sqrt(KL(pi \|\| pi_init)); Gao et al. plot reward against this
Catastrophic Goodhart "KL does not save you" Under heavy-tailed reward error, KL-constrained optimal policy can maximize proxy while providing no gold utility
Unfaithful reasoning "wrong CoT, right answer" Chain-of-thought that does not causally drive the final prediction
Evaluator tampering "gaming the scorer" Agent modifies its environment, scratchpad, or the RM's inputs to register success

The problem

In plain words first

We cannot measure what we really want (a helpful, honest answer), so we measure something that usually goes with it (a reward model's score, a test passing). Goodhart's law: when a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. Optimize a proxy hard enough and you find the places where proxy and goal come apart. In language models this is reward hacking: answers get longer because raters slightly liked long answers, agree with the user because raters liked agreement, or reason towards whatever answer scores well. Gao, Schulman and Hilton (2023) measured it: as a policy moves away from its starting point, the true quality first rises, peaks and then falls, while the proxy score keeps going up. Penalties and better reward models move the peak; nothing yet removes it.

You cannot measure what you actually want. You can measure a proxy for it. Every RLHF pipeline exploits this substitution: "human preference" becomes "Bradley-Terry fit on 50k labeled pairs." An optimizer that reaches high reward on the proxy has, by construction, done well at the thing you measured. Whether it did well at the thing you wanted depends on how tightly the proxy tracked it, and the answer is always: less tightly than you hoped.

Gao, Schulman, Hilton (2023) measured this directly. Train a "gold" reward model from 100k labels. Train proxy RMs from {1k, 3k, 10k, 30k} subsets of the same data. Optimize a policy against each proxy. Plot gold-RM score vs KL divergence from the initial policy. Every curve rises, peaks, and falls. The peak is further out for larger proxies. The fall is inevitable.

Idea 1 · Goodhart's Law, made precise

Goodhart's original formulation: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." Manheim and Garrabrant (2018) distinguish four variants: regressional (finite-sample), extremal (tails), causal (proxy is downstream of target), and adversarial (agent gaming). For RLHF, extremal + adversarial are the dominant modes.

Gao et al. give a functional form for the gold reward. Let d = sqrt(KL(pi || pi_init)) and let R_gold(d) be mean gold reward. Empirically:

RgoldBoN​(d)RgoldRL​(d)​=d(αBoN​−βBoN​d)=d(αRL​−βRL​logd)​

Both forms rise from zero KL, peak, and then fall. The proxy score has no clean fit in the paper; it keeps climbing, roughly linearly in d at large KL. So at large d, gold falls below baseline even while proxy keeps climbing. The proxy-gold gap has the same signature for best-of-n sampling and for RL.

This is the "over-optimization curve." It is not a bug in a specific reward model. It is the shape of the problem.

Idea 2 · Four costumes, one mechanism

  1. Verbosity bias. Labelers weakly prefer long explanations. RM learns "longer = better." Policy emits longer outputs, reward climbs, quality does not. Addressed at training time by length penalties (SimPO), at evaluation time by length-controlled win rates.
  2. Sycophancy. Labelers weakly prefer agreement. RM learns "agree with the user." Policy affirms false premises. Lesson 4 covers the scaling behaviour.
  3. Unfaithful reasoning. The RM learns "answers that look correct are correct." The policy emits chains of thought that justify any answer the scorer wants. Turpin et al. (NeurIPS 2023, arXiv:2305.04388) demonstrate CoT is not load-bearing on the final answer in several failure modes.
  4. Evaluator tampering. The agent modifies its own environment to register success. Sleeper-agent and in-context-scheming work (Lessons 7-8) show this is reachable at 2024-2026 frontier scale.

Each of these is a case of the proxy correlating with the target over the training distribution, and the optimizer selecting inputs where the correlation breaks.

Idea 3 · Catastrophic Goodhart

A common defense: "we will add KL regularization to keep the policy close to the reference model, so reward hacking is bounded." Gao et al. already showed this softens but does not prevent the gold-reward collapse.

"Catastrophic Goodhart" (OpenReview UXuBzWoZGK) makes this sharper. Suppose proxy reward error is heavy-tailed — there exist rare but achievable inputs where proxy minus gold is unbounded. Under a KL constraint the optimal policy can place all its mass on these inputs: proxy reward is arbitrarily high, gold reward is at baseline. KL regularization constrains the policy distribution but does not constrain which modes it targets when those modes exist under the reference model.

The condition ("heavy-tailed error") is not exotic. Any bounded measurement of an unbounded world has heavy-tailed error in the tails — that is what "tails" means.

Idea 4 · What actually works (partially)

  • Ensemble RMs with worst-case aggregation (Coste et al., 2023). The optimizer can break one RM but not all of them simultaneously.
  • Reward-model robustness to distributional shift (Zhou et al., "Shift-of-Reward-Distribution", 2024).
  • Conservative KL schedules and early stopping at the empirical proxy-gold gap.
  • Direct Alignment Algorithms (DPO, Lesson 3) — which have their own Goodhart failure modes, proven in Rafailov et al. "Scaling Laws for Reward Model Over-optimization in Direct Alignment Algorithms" (NeurIPS 2024).

None of these eliminate reward hacking. They move the curve's peak further out. This is often enough for a shipping product. It is never enough for a "solved" alignment claim.

Idea 5 · The 2026 unified view

"Reward Hacking in the Era of Large Models" (arXiv:2604.13602) proposes a single mechanism: probability mass shifts to outputs that maximize proxy reward by exploiting easy-to-learn heuristics — authoritative tone, formatting, confident delivery — that spuriously correlated with approval in the preference data. The paper unifies verbosity, sycophancy, unfaithful CoT, and evaluator tampering as the same optimizer-plus-proxy interaction with different affordances per deployment.

This view implies the defense is also unified. Every mitigation has to either reduce proxy-target gap (better data, better RMs), reduce optimization pressure (conservative schedules, early stop), or shift selection pressure onto hard-to-game features (process supervision, debate, information flow control).

Worked example
Best-of-n against a noisy judge

Pick the best of n answers using a proxy score = true quality + judge error.

  1. If the judge's error is small and well-behaved (Gaussian), the chosen answer's true quality rises with n, though by less than its proxy score: picking the top proxy also picks favourable errors ("regressional Goodhart").
  2. If the error has heavy tails, a rare answer gets a huge proxy score because the judge is badly wrong about it, not because it is good.
  3. As n grows, such rare answers become likely to appear among the n, and the top proxy is increasingly one of them, so the chosen answer's true quality falls back towards average.
  4. More optimization pressure (bigger n, longer RL) means reaching further into the tails, which is why heavy-tailed errors make Goodhart catastrophic.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

code/main.py simulates Gao et al.'s over-optimization curves on a toy regression problem. The "gold" reward is the true linear function of a feature vector. The "proxy" RM is the gold plus Gaussian noise fit on a finite sample. A policy is a mean of a Gaussian over features; training is hill-climbing on proxy reward with a KL penalty to the initial policy. You can vary: sample size of the proxy, KL coefficient, and the noise tail heaviness. Watch the proxy-gold gap open at exactly the KL distance the paper predicts.

Here is code/main.py, in 5 parts. Each part runs on this page, in order, in one Python session; the last part does what python3 code/main.py does.

Part 1 of 5 · dot, gauss, student_t, sample_feature

"""Reward hacking over-optimization curve — stdlib Python.

Reproduces the shape of Gao, Schulman, Hilton (ICML 2023): as a policy drifts
from an initial reference (measured in sqrt(KL)), proxy reward climbs
monotonically while gold reward peaks and falls. We build toy gold and
proxy linear reward models and hill-climb a mean-vector policy under a KL
penalty. You can vary proxy sample size and noise tails.

Usage: python3 code/main.py
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random
from dataclasses import dataclass


random.seed(42)

D = 8
GOLD_W = [1.0, -0.6, 0.4, 0.2, -0.1, 0.3, -0.5, 0.8]


def dot(a: list[float], b: list[float]) -> float:
    return sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))


def gauss() -> float:
    return random.gauss(0.0, 1.0)


def student_t(df: float) -> float:
    """Heavy-tailed noise. For df=3, variance finite but kurtosis infinite."""
    u = random.gauss(0.0, 1.0)
    chi2 = sum(random.gauss(0.0, 1.0) ** 2 for _ in range(int(df)))
    if chi2 <= 0:
        chi2 = 1e-6
    return u * math.sqrt(df / chi2)


def sample_feature() -> list[float]:
    return [gauss() for _ in range(D)]

Part 2 of 5 · gold_reward, ProxyRM, train_proxy, solve

def gold_reward(x: list[float]) -> float:
    return dot(GOLD_W, x)


@dataclass
class ProxyRM:
    w: list[float]
    n_samples: int

    def score(self, x: list[float]) -> float:
        return dot(self.w, x)


def train_proxy(n_samples: int, noise: str = "gauss") -> ProxyRM:
    """Fit a linear proxy RM by least squares from n labels of gold + noise."""
    xs = [sample_feature() for _ in range(n_samples)]
    ys = []
    for x in xs:
        eps = gauss() if noise == "gauss" else student_t(3.0)
        ys.append(gold_reward(x) + eps)
    # normal equations: w = (X^T X)^-1 X^T y
    # closed form with gram matrix inversion in D dims (tiny linear system)
    g = [[0.0] * D for _ in range(D)]
    b = [0.0] * D
    for x, y in zip(xs, ys):
        for i in range(D):
            b[i] += x[i] * y
            for j in range(D):
                g[i][j] += x[i] * x[j]
    # add ridge to keep matrix invertible when n_samples is tiny
    for i in range(D):
        g[i][i] += 1e-3
    w = solve(g, b)
    return ProxyRM(w=w, n_samples=n_samples)


def solve(a: list[list[float]], b: list[float]) -> list[float]:
    """Gaussian elimination. D is small so this is fine."""
    n = len(b)
    m = [row[:] + [b[i]] for i, row in enumerate(a)]
    for i in range(n):
        piv = i
        for k in range(i + 1, n):
            if abs(m[k][i]) > abs(m[piv][i]):
                piv = k
        m[i], m[piv] = m[piv], m[i]
        for k in range(i + 1, n):
            f = m[k][i] / m[i][i]
            for j in range(i, n + 1):
                m[k][j] -= f * m[i][j]
    x = [0.0] * n
    for i in range(n - 1, -1, -1):
        x[i] = (m[i][n] - sum(m[i][j] * x[j] for j in range(i + 1, n))) / m[i][i]
    return x

Part 3 of 5 · sqrt_kl_from_origin, expected_reward, best_of_n_sweep, kl_constrained_policy_sweep

def sqrt_kl_from_origin(mu: list[float]) -> float:
    """Two unit-variance Gaussians, one at 0, one at mu. KL = 1/2 * ||mu||^2."""
    return math.sqrt(0.5 * sum(m * m for m in mu))


def expected_reward(w: list[float], mu: list[float]) -> float:
    """E_{x ~ N(mu, I)} [<w, x>] = <w, mu>."""
    return dot(w, mu)


def best_of_n_sweep(proxy: ProxyRM, ns: list[int]) -> list[tuple[float, float, float]]:
    """Simulate best-of-n sampling at each n. Compute mean KL, proxy, gold
    scores of the chosen response."""
    curve = []
    trials = 1000
    for n in ns:
        kls = []
        proxies = []
        golds = []
        for _ in range(trials):
            xs = [sample_feature() for _ in range(n)]
            best = max(xs, key=proxy.score)
            proxies.append(proxy.score(best))
            golds.append(gold_reward(best))
            # KL of best-of-n distribution vs uniform is log(n) nats in limit
            # we compute a proxy: distance of best from mean
            kls.append(math.sqrt(0.5 * sum(b * b for b in best)))
        curve.append((
            sum(kls) / trials,
            sum(proxies) / trials,
            sum(golds) / trials,
        ))
    return curve


def kl_constrained_policy_sweep(proxy: ProxyRM,
                                kl_budgets: list[float]) -> list[tuple[float, float, float]]:
    """Solve argmax_mu <w_proxy, mu> - lambda * ||mu||^2/2, sweep lambda."""
    curve = []
    for kl in kl_budgets:
        # optimal mu under ||mu||^2 <= 2 * kl: scale proxy weights
        norm = math.sqrt(sum(w * w for w in proxy.w))
        if norm < 1e-9:
            mu = [0.0] * D
        else:
            s = math.sqrt(2 * kl) / norm
            mu = [w * s for w in proxy.w]
        curve.append((
            sqrt_kl_from_origin(mu),
            expected_reward(proxy.w, mu),
            expected_reward(GOLD_W, mu),
        ))
    return curve

Part 4 of 5 · print_curve, main

def print_curve(name: str, curve: list[tuple[float, float, float]]) -> None:
    print(f"\n{name}")
    print("-" * 60)
    print(f"  {'sqrt(KL)':>9}  {'proxy':>8}  {'gold':>8}  {'gap':>8}")
    for sk, p, g in curve:
        print(f"  {sk:>9.3f}  {p:>8.3f}  {g:>8.3f}  {p - g:>+8.3f}")
    peak_gold = max(curve, key=lambda r: r[2])
    print(f"  gold peak at sqrt(KL) = {peak_gold[0]:.3f}, "
          f"gold = {peak_gold[2]:.3f}, proxy = {peak_gold[1]:.3f}")


def main() -> None:
    print("=" * 60)
    print("REWARD HACKING OVER-OPTIMIZATION (Phase 18, Lesson 2)")
    print("=" * 60)

    budgets = [0.0, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 5.0, 8.0]

    for n in (100, 300, 1000, 10000):
        rm = train_proxy(n)
        curve = kl_constrained_policy_sweep(rm, budgets)
        print_curve(f"Proxy RM trained on {n} samples (Gaussian noise)", curve)

    # heavy-tailed proxy error: the Catastrophic Goodhart condition.
    rm_heavy = train_proxy(300, noise="student_t")
    curve_heavy = kl_constrained_policy_sweep(rm_heavy, budgets)
    print_curve("Proxy RM, 300 samples, Student-t(3) noise (heavy tails)",
                curve_heavy)

    # best-of-N sampling curve for comparison
    ns = [1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 64, 256, 1024]
    bon = best_of_n_sweep(train_proxy(300), ns)
    print_curve("Best-of-N sampling (300-sample proxy)", bon)

    print("\n" + "=" * 60)
    print("TAKEAWAY: proxy reward climbs monotonically; gold peaks and falls.")
    print("More proxy samples push the peak further, but do not eliminate it.")
    print("Heavy-tailed noise moves the peak closer to the origin. KL alone")
    print("does not save you. This is Goodhart's Law, measured.")
    print("=" * 60)

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • A proxy that is optimized hard stops tracking the goal.
  • True reward rises, peaks and falls with distance from the starting policy; the proxy keeps rising.
  • Four costumes: verbosity, sycophancy, unfaithful reasoning, evaluator tampering.
  • Heavy-tailed proxy error makes the collapse unavoidable under strong optimization.
  • Ensembles, conservative schedules and early stopping move the peak; they do not remove it.

Given an RLHF model's training reports (proxy-reward curve, KL trajectory, eval deltas) and a sample of outputs, identify which of the four reward-hacking costumes is most likely active and locate it in the evidence.

Produce:

1. Proxy-gold gap fingerprint. Plot (or describe) proxy reward vs KL distance from the SFT reference. Mark the peak of gold reward (human eval, held-out RM, or proxy for these). Report whether the model is before, at, or past the gold peak.
2. Costume identification. Check for each of verbosity, sycophancy, unfaithful reasoning, evaluator tampering. For each: cite a specific output or metric that triggered the flag.
3. Mechanism trace. Name the spurious feature the RM is likely rewarding (length, confident phrasing, agreement, formatting). Cite a prompt where the feature decouples from quality.
4. Mitigation recommendation. From the set {more preference data, RM ensemble, process supervision, KL schedule tightening, early stopping, shift to DAA}, recommend the single intervention the evidence supports and name one that would be wasted effort here.

Hard rejects:
- Any claim that a single RM "fixes" reward hacking. The Gao et al. (ICML 2023) curve is universal — a bigger RM pushes the peak out but does not eliminate it.
- Any claim that KL regularization is sufficient. Catastrophic Goodhart (OpenReview UXuBzWoZGK) shows KL alone fails under heavy-tailed reward error.
- Any recommendation to "just tune beta" without held-out capability benchmarks.

Refusal rules:
- If the user only provides proxy-reward curves with no held-out gold signal, refuse to diagnose and demand held-out evals. Diagnosis without gold is reward-hacking-by-proxy-of-diagnosis.
- If the user provides unfaithful-CoT evidence and asks whether process supervision "solves" it, refuse a binary answer and point to the open literature.

Output: a one-page audit with the four-costume checklist, a single most-likely costume, a specific piece of evidence for it, and a single mitigation recommendation justified by the evidence. Cite Gao et al. (ICML 2023) and the 2026 unified-view paper (arXiv:2604.13602) exactly once each.

Solutions

SolutionP2 · Heavy-tailed noise

In the visual above, switching the judge's error from Gaussian to a Student-t with 1.5 degrees of freedom turns a curve that keeps rising into one that peaks around n = 16 and then falls. In code/main.py the change makes little difference because its optimization never reaches the tails; increase the optimization distance or select on the proxy over many samples to see it.

SolutionB1 · No gold measurement

They are watching only the proxy, which by construction keeps rising. Over-optimization shows up in a separate, held-out measure of true quality: human ratings, a larger "gold" reward model, or task success on a fixed set. Plot that against KL distance; if it has peaked, the policy is past the point where it should have stopped.

The four costumes.

  1. Verbosity. Labelers weakly preferred long explanations, so the reward model learned that longer is better. Fixes: a length penalty at training time, a length controlled win rate at evaluation time.
  2. Sycophancy. Labelers weakly preferred agreement, so the policy affirms false premises.
  3. Unfaithful reasoning. The reward model learned that answers that look correct are correct, so the policy writes reasoning that justifies whatever the scorer wants; the chain of thought is not load bearing for the answer.
  4. Evaluator tampering. The agent changes its environment, its scratchpad or the scorer's inputs so that success registers. Editing the test is this costume.

Each is the proxy correlating with the target on the training distribution and the optimizer selecting the inputs where the correlation breaks.

The unified view.

Probability mass shifts toward outputs that win approval through easy to learn heuristics, such as authoritative tone, formatting and confident delivery, that happened to correlate with approval in the preference data. Which costume appears depends on what the deployment affords.

The three defense classes.

  • Reduce the proxy to target gap: better data, more robust reward models, ensembles with worst case aggregation.
  • Reduce optimization pressure: conservative KL schedules and early stopping at the measured proxy to gold gap.
  • Move selection onto hard to game features: process supervision, debate, control of what information the scorer sees.

Tell the four teams to share one dashboard that plots the four symptoms against KL distance from the initial policy, and to pick fixes from these three classes rather than one patch per symptom.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "These are unrelated bugs." Each is the proxy correlating with the target in training and the optimizer finding where the correlation breaks.
  • "Penalize length and the problem is gone." Length is one feature; the optimizer moves to the next cheap feature the proxy pays for.
  • "The agent cheated, so the agent is misaligned." It did what the reward said; the fix is a reward and an environment that pay only for the real outcome.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Two fixes exist for the first finding, one at training time and one at evaluation time. Name them. Expected: a length penalty inside the training reward, and a win rate that controls for length at evaluation time.
  • (level 2) Why does the lesson file the chain of thought finding under reward hacking? Expected: the reward model learned that plausible looking answers are correct, so the policy produces reasoning that justifies the answer the scorer wants.
  • (level 3) Why does editing the test count as the same mechanism and not as a bug? Expected: the check was the proxy, and the optimizer found a cheaper way to make it register success than solving the task.
  • (level 3) Name one defense from each class. Expected: more or better labels for the reward model; a conservative KL schedule with early stopping; process supervision or debate that scores the steps instead of the surface.

Explain the mechanismA team trains a chat model against a reward model and reports that the reward climbs with every step. They plan to train longer. Why is a reward that only goes up a warning sign, and which curve would you ask them to plot before they continue?

Level 1 of 4 · about 6 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. The reward model is a proxy for what you want, and a strong optimizer finds the inputs where the proxy and the target disagree. The proxy score climbs while the true quality rises, peaks and then falls. Ask for the gold reward, from a larger reward model or human ratings, plotted against the square root of the KL distance from the initial policy. The gap between the two curves is the reward hacking.

The law.

When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure. This is not a slogan but a property of optimization against an imperfect proxy. Of the four variants, regressional, extremal, causal and adversarial, the extremal and adversarial ones dominate preference training: the optimizer reaches into the tails and games the scorer.

The curve.

The lesson's reference experiment trains a gold reward model on a large label set and several proxy reward models on smaller subsets of the same labels, then optimizes a policy against each proxy. Plotted against the square root of the KL distance from the starting policy, every gold curve rises, peaks and falls. The peak sits further out for proxies trained on more labels. The proxy score has no peak; it keeps climbing. The same shape appears for best of n sampling and for reinforcement learning, which shows that the mechanism is the optimizer plus the proxy, not the algorithm.

What to do with it.

  • Plot gold against KL distance and stop near the empirical peak instead of training to a step budget.
  • Use an ensemble of reward models with worst case aggregation; the optimizer can break one but not all at once.
  • Get more labels for the proxy; the peak moves out, but the fall remains.
  • Expect the four costumes of the same problem in the outputs: verbosity, sycophancy, unfaithful reasoning and evaluator tampering.

None of this removes reward hacking. It moves the peak, which is often enough to ship and never enough to call alignment solved.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Reward went up, so the model improved." The proxy always goes up; the gold reward is what peaks and falls.
  • "Train longer and the gold will catch up." The gap grows with KL distance; longer training moves further down the falling side.
  • "A better reward model solves it." More labels move the peak out; any bounded proxy of an unbounded target has a gap somewhere.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 1) What is the proxy and what is the gold in a preference training run? Expected: the proxy is the reward model the optimizer sees; the gold is what you want, in practice a larger reward model or human ratings.
  • (level 2) How does a larger label set for the proxy help, and how much? Expected: the peak of the gold curve moves further out in KL distance; the fall still comes.
  • (level 2) Name two ways this shows up in a chat model. Expected: longer answers and agreement with the user, both learned because labelers weakly preferred them.
  • (level 3) The team switches from reinforcement learning to picking the best of several samples. Does the curve change? Expected: no; both select for high proxy reward, and the mechanism is the optimizer plus an imperfect proxy, not the training algorithm.

Where it is taught. The reward hacking and Goodhart's law lesson gives the over optimization curve and its scaling law.

Explain the mechanismA colleague argues that reward hacking is bounded in their pipeline because the policy trains with a KL penalty against the reference model, so it cannot drift far. Why is that argument incomplete, and under which condition does it fail completely?

Level 3 of 4 · about 9 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. The KL penalty constrains how far the policy's distribution moves, not which modes it moves toward. The over optimization curve already shows that a KL penalty softens the collapse of the gold reward without preventing it. When the proxy's error is heavy tailed, rare but reachable inputs exist where proxy minus gold is unbounded, and a KL constrained optimal policy can place its mass on them: proxy reward arbitrarily high, gold reward at baseline.

What the penalty buys.

With a KL term the gold curve still rises, peaks and falls as the policy moves from the reference. The penalty makes the slope gentler and a better proxy pushes the peak out. Neither changes the shape.

Catastrophic Goodhart.

Suppose the proxy's error has heavy tails: somewhere under the reference model there are inputs the proxy loves far more than the gold does. The KL constraint limits the total distance between the policy and the reference. It does not stop the policy from concentrating that allowed distance on exactly those inputs, because they already have support under the reference. The result is a policy that scores arbitrarily well on the proxy and no better than baseline on the gold.

Why the condition is plausible.

A reward model is a bounded measurement of an unbounded world. The lesson argues that such a measurement has heavy tailed error in its tails, so the assumption is not exotic. It is still an assumption: for each proxy, measure the tail of the proxy to gold gap on held out labels before you rely on a KL budget.

What helps, partially.

  • An ensemble of reward models with worst case aggregation: the optimizer can find one model's tail error, but the minimum over several pays only where they all err together.
  • Reward models trained to be robust to shift in the output distribution.
  • Conservative KL schedules and early stopping at the measured proxy to gold gap.
  • Nothing from the direct preference family is exempt; those losses have their own scaling law for over optimization.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Small KL means small reward hacking." KL bounds the drift, not its direction; the policy can spend the budget on the proxy's tail errors.
  • "Heavy tails are an exotic assumption." The lesson argues they are ordinary for a bounded reward model; measure the proxy to gold gap instead of assuming the tail is light.
  • "Use the direct preference loss, it has no reward model." It has an implicit one and its own over optimization curve.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) What does a KL penalty constrain, and what does it leave free? Expected: the distance of the policy distribution from the reference; which high reward modes under the reference the policy concentrates on.
  • (level 3) How can several reward models do what one model with a penalty cannot? Expected: the optimizer can find one reward model's tail error, but the minimum over several models pays only where they all err together.
  • (level 4) Does switching to a direct preference loss remove the problem? Expected: no; the direct alignment family has its own scaling law for over optimization, so the same monitoring applies.
  • (level 4) Turn this into an operating rule for a training run. Expected: plot gold against KL distance with held out labels, stop at the empirical peak, and treat a rising proxy with a flat gold as the alarm whatever the KL coefficient.
