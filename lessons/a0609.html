The problem

In plain words first

Music generators turn a text description (and sometimes a hummed melody or lyrics) into audio. Two designs dominate. One treats audio like text: a neural codec turns sound into a few streams of discrete tokens, and a transformer predicts those tokens one step at a time, as MusicGen does. The other is diffusion: start from noise in a compressed audio representation and refine it towards music that matches the prompt, as Stable Audio does. Commercial song generators (Suno, Udio) add vocals and song structure on top, with architectures they do not publish. Two practical questions sit around the models: how to measure output that has no single right answer (embedding distances such as FAD, text-audio agreement such as the CLAP score, and listeners), and who owns the rights, which changed sharply with the 2025 label settlements. This lesson computes the token budgets behind MusicGen with a real codec, and builds CLAP scoring and FAD on generated test signals. No music model is trained or run: the open generators are multi-gigabyte downloads.

Text → a 30-second to 4-minute music clip, with lyrics, vocals, and structure. Three sub-problems:

  1. Instrumental generation. Text like "lo-fi hip-hop drums with warm keys" → audio. MusicGen, Stable Audio, AudioLDM.
  2. Song generation (with vocals + lyrics). "Country song about rainy Texas nights" → full song. Suno, Udio, YuE, ACE-Step.
  3. Conditional / controllable. Extend an existing clip, regenerate a bridge, swap genre, stem-separate, or inpaint. Inpainting and stem export are now standard features of the commercial tools.

The idea

The same idea as a formula

Codec tokens. A neural codec (EnCodec, Lesson 6.13) encodes audio into frames at rate fr​ (50 Hz for MusicGen's 32 kHz EnCodec), each represented by K codebook indices from residual vector quantisation; K sets the bitrate, K×fr​×log2​(codebook size) bits per second.

Token language model. MusicGen models P(tokens∣text) autoregressively with a transformer that cross-attends to a T5 encoding of the prompt. Flattening K codebooks into one sequence would need Kfr​ steps per second; the delay pattern shifts codebook k by k steps so that each step emits one token from every codebook, fr​+K−1 steps per second-long window, with codebook k of frame t predicted after codebooks <k of that frame.

Latent diffusion (Stable Audio): an autoencoder compresses audio to latents z; a network ϵθ​(zt​,t,text,timing) learns to remove noise, L=E∥ϵ−ϵθ​(zt​,t,⋅)∥2; timing conditioning (start and total seconds) controls length.

CLAP score. With audio and text encoders trained contrastively, CLAP(a,p)=cos(fa​(a),ft​(p)): how well the audio matches the prompt.

Fréchet Audio Distance. Fit Gaussians N(μr​,Σr​) and N(μg​,Σg​) to embeddings of real and generated clips:

FAD=∥μr​−μg​∥2+tr(Σr​+Σg​−2(Σr​Σg​)1/2)

It is biased by sample size (small sets inflate it) and depends entirely on the embedding model (VGGish, PANNs, CLAP).

Tempo. A beat tracker finds the period τ that best explains the onset-strength envelope; BPM =60/τ.

Worked example
Token budgets by hand

MusicGen. 30 s × 50 frames/s × 4 codebooks = 6,000 tokens. Flattened, that is 6,000 decoding steps; with the delay pattern, 30×50+3=1,503 steps, each predicting 4 tokens. At 2,048 entries per codebook (11 bits), the token stream is 4×50×11=2,200 bits per second, 2.2 kbps.

Text tokens for comparison. A 30-second song's lyrics are perhaps 80 words, about 100 text tokens: the audio is 60 times longer as a sequence, which is why music LMs struggle with structure past 30 seconds.

FAD by hand in one dimension. Real embeddings N(0,1), generated N(0.5,0.25): FAD=0.52+(1+0.25−20.25​)=0.25+0.25=0.5. Shifting the mean and shrinking the spread (less variety) both cost.

    ✓ It means embeddings closer to the reference set. Change the embedding model or the reference set and the ranking can change.

    ✓ CLAP knows coarse categories (genre, instrument, mood) much better than details such as tempo, key or structure.

Idea 1 · Token LM over neural-codec tokens

Meta's MusicGen (2023; code MIT, model weights CC-BY-NC 4.0) and many derivatives: condition on text/melody embeddings, autoregressively predict EnCodec tokens (32 kHz, 4 codebooks), decode with EnCodec. 300M - 3.3B params. Strong baseline; struggles past 30 seconds.

ACE-Step (open source) targets full songs with lyrics; ACE-Step 1.5 (2026) pairs a language-model planner, which writes the song's structure and lyrics, with a diffusion transformer that generates the audio, and runs in under 4 GB of GPU memory. The open community's closest thing to Suno.

Idea 2 · Diffusion over mels or latents

Stable Audio (2023) and Stable Audio Open (2024): latent diffusion on compressed audio. Excels at loops, sound design, ambient textures. Not great at structured full songs.

AudioLDM / AudioLDM2: text-to-audio via T2I-style latent diffusion, generalized to music, sound effects, speech.

Idea 3 · Hybrid (production) — Suno, Udio, Lyria

Closed weights, unpublished architectures. Suno (v5 launched in 2025) is widely regarded as the commercial quality leader for full songs with vocals; Udio offers inpainting and stem downloads. Google's Lyria models power its music tools, including Lyria RealTime for interactive, continuously steerable generation.

Idea 4 · Evaluation

  • FAD (Fréchet Audio Distance). Embedding-level distance between generated vs real audio distribution using VGGish or PANNs features. Lower is better. FAD depends on the embedding model and the number of clips, so compare only numbers computed the same way.
  • Musicality (subjective). Human preference, from listening tests or pairwise arenas.
  • Text-audio alignment. CLAP score between prompt and output.
  • Musicality artifacts. Off-beat transitions, vocal-phrase drift, loss of structure past 30 s.

The legal landscape (2025-2026)

  • Universal vs Udio. Settled in October 2025; the two announced a licensed AI music platform for 2026.
  • Warner Music vs Suno. Settled in November 2025 with a partnership: Suno is to launch licensed models in 2026, with downloads limited to paid accounts.
  • Disclosure. The EU AI Act's marking duty for generated audio (Article 50, from August 2026; Lesson 6.8) covers music; California's AI Transparency Act (SB 942) requires large generative AI providers to offer detection tools and embed disclosures.
  • Open weights are not automatically commercial. MusicGen's weights are CC-BY-NC 4.0; Stable Audio Open uses Stability AI's community licence.

Safe-to-ship patterns:

  1. Generate instrumentals with models whose weight licence allows your use.
  2. Use commercial APIs (Suno, Udio, ElevenLabs Music) under their licence terms.
  3. Train on owned or licensed catalog (most enterprises end up here).
  4. Tag generations with watermarks + metadata.

Audio as tokens: a real codec, and MusicGen's delay pattern

Six seconds of a synthetic piano melody through Meta's 24 kHz EnCodec at three bitrates (more codebooks = more tokens), with the reconstruction error; then the delay pattern that lets MusicGen predict 4 codebooks per step. The test signals are generated in code (piano-like plucked notes, a 128 BPM drum loop, sustained synth chords), so nothing copyrighted is used.

import numpy as np, torch, librosa
from scipy import linalg

SR = 48000                                     # CLAP's sample rate
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def env(n, attack=0.005, decay=0.4):           # a pluck-like amplitude envelope
    t = np.arange(n) / SR
    return np.minimum(t / attack, 1) * np.exp(-t / decay)

def piano_melody(seed, bpm=100, seconds=6):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed); out = np.zeros(int(SR * seconds)); step = int(SR * 60 / bpm / 2)
    scale = [60, 62, 64, 65, 67, 69, 71, 72]
    for k, start in enumerate(range(0, len(out) - step, step)):
        f = 440 * 2 ** ((r.choice(scale) - 69) / 12); n = 2 * step; t = np.arange(n) / SR
        note = sum(np.sin(2 * np.pi * h * f * t) / h ** 1.5 for h in range(1, 6)) * env(n)
        out[start:start + n] += 0.3 * note[: len(out) - start]
    return out

def drum_loop(seed, bpm=128, seconds=6):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed); out = np.zeros(int(SR * seconds)); beat = int(SR * 60 / bpm)
    for k, start in enumerate(range(0, len(out) - beat, beat // 2)):
        if k % 2 == 0:                                                   # kick on the beat: a falling sine thump
            n = int(0.25 * SR); t = np.arange(n) / SR
            out[start:start + n] += 0.9 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * (50 + 100 * np.exp(-t * 30)) * t) * np.exp(-t * 12)
        n = int(0.05 * SR)                                               # hi-hat on every half beat: a burst of noise
        out[start:start + n] += 0.2 * r.standard_normal(n) * np.exp(-np.arange(n) / SR * 80)
    return out

def synth_pad(seed, seconds=6):
    r = np.random.default_rng(seed); t = np.arange(int(SR * seconds)) / SR
    root = r.choice([48, 50, 53, 55]); chord = [root, root + 4, root + 7, root + 11]
    return 0.15 * sum(np.sin(2 * np.pi * 440 * 2 ** ((m - 69) / 12) * t + r.uniform(0, 6)) * (1 + 0.3 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 0.2 * t))
                      for m in chord)

kinds = {"piano melody": piano_melody, "drum loop": drum_loop, "synth pad": synth_pad}
clips = {k: [f(s) for s in range(12)] for k, f in kinds.items()}
print({k: f"{len(v)} clips of {len(v[0]) / SR:.0f} s" for k, v in clips.items()})

# The tempo check from the pitfalls list, on the 128 BPM drum loop.
tempo, _ = librosa.beat.beat_track(y=librosa.resample(clips["drum loop"][0], orig_sr=SR, target_sr=22050), sr=22050)
print(f"librosa.beat_track on the 128 BPM drum loop: {float(np.atleast_1d(tempo)[0]):.1f} BPM")

from transformers import EncodecModel, AutoProcessor

codec = EncodecModel.from_pretrained("facebook/encodec_24khz").eval()          # Meta's 24 kHz codec (MusicGen uses a 32 kHz sibling)
eproc = AutoProcessor.from_pretrained("facebook/encodec_24khz")
x = librosa.resample(piano_melody(3), orig_sr=SR, target_sr=24000).astype(np.float32)
def logmel_db(w, ref_power):
    """Log-mel in dB relative to the original's peak, floored 60 dB down (silence should not dominate the error)."""
    m = librosa.feature.melspectrogram(y=w, sr=24000, n_fft=1024, hop_length=256, n_mels=64)
    return np.maximum(10 * np.log10(m / ref_power + 1e-12), -60)
peak = librosa.feature.melspectrogram(y=x, sr=24000, n_fft=1024, hop_length=256, n_mels=64).max()
ref = logmel_db(x, peak)
print(f"{len(x) / 24000:.0f} s of piano at 24 kHz = {len(x):,} samples")
for bw in [1.5, 6.0, 24.0]:
    inp = eproc(raw_audio=x, sampling_rate=24000, return_tensors="pt")
    with torch.no_grad():
        enc = codec.encode(inp["input_values"], inp["padding_mask"], bandwidth=bw)
        y = codec.decode(enc.audio_codes, enc.audio_scales, inp["padding_mask"])[0][0, 0].numpy()[: len(x)]
    codes = enc.audio_codes[0, 0]                                                 # (codebooks, frames)
    n_q, n_f = codes.shape
    err = np.mean(np.abs(logmel_db(y, peak) - ref))
    print(f"{bw:5.1f} kbps: {n_q:2d} codebooks x {n_f} frames ({n_f / (len(x) / 24000):.0f} per s) = "
          f"{n_q * n_f / (len(x) / 24000):5.0f} tokens/s, log-mel error {err:.1f} dB")

# MusicGen's "delay" pattern: 4 codebooks at 50 frames/s. Shift codebook k right by k steps, so one transformer
# step predicts one token from every codebook, each conditioned on the coarser codebooks of the same frame.
K, T = 4, 6
grid = np.array([[f"{'abcd'[k]}{t}" for t in range(T)] for k in range(K)])
delayed = np.full((K, T + K - 1), " .", dtype=object)
for k in range(K):
    delayed[k, k:k + T] = grid[k]
print("\ncodebook tokens (letter = codebook, number = frame):", "\n".join(" ".join(r) for r in grid), sep="\n")
print("after the delay pattern, each column is one decoding step:", "\n".join(" ".join(r) for r in delayed), sep="\n")
for secs in [10, 30]:
    print(f"{secs} s of MusicGen audio: flattened {secs * 50 * 4:,} steps, delay pattern {secs * 50 + 3:,} steps")

Reading this output

The beat tracker finds 129.2 BPM in the 128 BPM loop, close enough to use as a post-generation check that a model kept the requested tempo. EnCodec runs at 75 frames per second at every bitrate; the bitrate only changes the number of codebooks, from 2 (150 tokens/s) to 32 (2,400 tokens/s). The reconstruction error falls from 4.1 dB to 2.3 dB as codebooks are added: the first codebooks carry most of the sound, later ones refine it, which is why MusicGen gets away with 4. The grids show the delay pattern: after the shift, each column holds one token from each codebook, and 30 seconds take 1,503 steps instead of 6,000.

CLAP scores and FAD, from scratch

LAION's CLAP (the laion/clap-htsat-unfused checkpoint in transformers) embeds the 36 test clips and three prompts. The table is the CLAP score, averaged per kind; FAD is computed from the formula above between sets of 12 clips.

from transformers import ClapModel, ClapProcessor

clap = ClapModel.from_pretrained("laion/clap-htsat-unfused").eval()
cproc = ClapProcessor.from_pretrained("laion/clap-htsat-unfused")
def audio_emb(waves):
    with torch.no_grad():
        e = clap.get_audio_features(**cproc(audio=[w.astype(np.float32) for w in waves], sampling_rate=SR, return_tensors="pt"))
    e = e.pooler_output if hasattr(e, "pooler_output") else e
    return torch.nn.functional.normalize(e, dim=-1).numpy()
def text_emb(texts):
    with torch.no_grad():
        e = clap.get_text_features(**cproc(text=texts, return_tensors="pt", padding=True))
    e = e.pooler_output if hasattr(e, "pooler_output") else e
    return torch.nn.functional.normalize(e, dim=-1).numpy()

E = {k: audio_emb(v) for k, v in clips.items()}
prompts = ["a solo piano melody", "an electronic drum beat", "a soft ambient synthesizer pad"]
T = text_emb(prompts)
print("CLAP score (cosine of audio and text embeddings), averaged over 12 clips per kind:")
print(f"{'':14s}" + "".join(f"{p[:22]:>25s}" for p in prompts))
for k in kinds:
    print(f"{k:14s}" + "".join(f"{v:25.3f}" for v in (E[k] @ T.T).mean(0)))

def fad(A, B):
    """Frechet distance between Gaussians fitted to two sets of embeddings."""
    mu_a, mu_b = A.mean(0), B.mean(0)
    Sa, Sb = np.cov(A, rowvar=False), np.cov(B, rowvar=False)
    covmean = linalg.sqrtm(Sa @ Sb).real
    return float(np.sum((mu_a - mu_b) ** 2) + np.trace(Sa + Sb - 2 * covmean))

extra = {k: audio_emb([f(s) for s in range(100, 112)]) for k, f in kinds.items()}   # fresh clips, new seeds
print("\nFAD between sets of 12 clips (CLAP embeddings; lower = more alike):")
for k in kinds:
    print(f"  {k:14s} vs new {k:14s} {fad(E[k], extra[k]):.3f}   vs drum loops {fad(E[k], extra['drum loop']):.3f}")

Reading this output

Drum loops match their prompt clearly (0.59, against 0.16 and −0.01 for the others), and the piano melodies prefer the piano prompt (0.36). The synth pads are confused: 0.31 for the ambient-pad prompt and 0.31 for the drum prompt. CLAP's sense of "electronic" overlaps "synthesizer", and these test pads are simple sine chords. FAD behaves as intended at the coarse level: a new set of the same kind is close (0.001–0.011), drums against melodies or pads are far (about 1.3). With 12 clips and 512-dimensional embeddings, the covariance estimates are rough; real FAD evaluations use hundreds or thousands of clips and report the embedding model.

  • Licensing is the product decision. After the 2025 settlements, the commercial services moved to licensed training data, and enterprises increasingly train or fine-tune on catalogues they own. Disclosure duties for generated audio (EU AI Act Article 50, Lesson 6.8) apply to music too.

Pitfalls that still ship in 2026

  • Copyright-laundering prompts. "Song in the style of Taylor Swift" — commercial Suno/Udio filter these now, open models do not. Add your own filter list.
  • Repetition / drift past 30 s. AR models loop. Crossfade multiple generations, or use ACE-Step for structural coherence.
  • Tempo drift. Models wander off the BPM. Use BPM tags in the prompt and post-filter with librosa's beat_track.
  • Vocal intelligibility. Suno is excellent; open models are often mushy on words. If lyrics matter, use a commercial API or fine-tune.
  • Mono output. Open models generate mono or fake-stereo. Use a stereo model where one exists: MusicGen has stereo variants, and Stable Audio Open generates 44.1 kHz stereo.
Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Two families: autoregressive models over codec tokens (MusicGen) and diffusion over compressed audio latents (Stable Audio); commercial song models are closed.
  • Codec tokens: frames per second × codebooks; MusicGen's delay pattern predicts all codebooks of a frame in one step.
  • Evaluate with FAD (distribution distance, embedding- and size-dependent), CLAP score (prompt agreement) and listeners; check tempo with a beat tracker.
  • Read weight licences; open does not mean commercial.
  • Disclose and mark generated music.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Drums clearly, pads confused

Drum loops get the clearest match (0.59 for the drum prompt). Piano melodies are matched less strongly but correctly (0.36). Synth pads are confused between "ambient synthesizer pad" and "electronic drum beat" (0.31 each): CLAP associates "electronic" with synthetic timbres, and these pads are plain sine chords.

SolutionB1 · FAD shrinks as the sample grows

With 3 clips per set, each covariance is estimated from 3 points in 512 dimensions, and the FAD between two sets of the same kind roughly doubles: 0.0021 against 0.0010 for drum loops, 0.024 against 0.011 for piano melodies (6 clips fall in between). FAD is biased upwards for small samples, so it can only compare systems evaluated with the same number of clips (and the same embedding model); papers that report it use thousands of clips, and some extrapolate to an infinite sample size.
