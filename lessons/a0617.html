The problem

In plain words first

Every lesson in this phase ended with a number: a word error rate, a speaker similarity, an equal error rate, a distance between sets of music. This lesson puts them side by side. The core idea is that each metric measures one axis, such as intelligibility, naturalness, speaker identity or speed, and a system can be excellent on one and poor on another. The second idea is that a metric is a recipe, not just a formula: how text is normalised, how errors are averaged over utterances, and which test set is used can move a score more than a better model would. The runs score Whisper on LibriSpeech under three normalisations with a confidence interval, then score real, degraded and synthetic speech with UTMOS (a learned MOS predictor), round-trip WER and speaker similarity together. Nothing is trained.

Every audio task has multiple metrics, each measuring a different axis. Using the wrong metric is how you ship a model that looks great on your dashboard and terribly in production. The 2026 canonical list:

The idea

The same idea as a formula

Word error rate. Align the hypothesis to the reference of N words with the fewest edits: S substitutions, D deletions and I insertions.

WER=NS+D+I​

It can exceed 100%, because insertions are not bounded by N; the raw-string row in the run under "Scoring Whisper properly" is 100.3%.

Corpus or average. For utterances i=1,…,U with ei​ errors and ni​ reference words:

WERcorpus​=∑i​ni​∑i​ei​​WER=U1​i∑​ni​ei​​

Leaderboards report the corpus form; the average weights a four-word command as much as a forty-word sentence.

Equal error rate. For a threshold θ on a similarity score, FAR(θ) is the share of different-speaker pairs accepted and FRR(θ) the share of same-speaker pairs rejected. The EER is their common value at the threshold θ∗ where FAR(θ∗)=FRR(θ∗).

Worked example
Normalisation and averaging by hand
  • One contraction. Reference "what's the weather today" (4 words), hypothesis "what is the weather today". The cheapest alignment is one substitution (what's → what) and one insertion (is): (1+0+1)/4=50%. After Whisper's normaliser turns "what's" into "what is", both sides match: 0/5=0%.
  • Two ways to average. A 4-word utterance with 4 errors (100%) and a 46-word utterance with 1 error (2.2%). Corpus WER: (4+1)/(4+46)=10.0%. Mean of per-utterance WERs: (100%+2.2%)/2=51.1%. One short utterance dominates the average, which is why the run's 30 utterances give 7.8% as a corpus but 12.5% averaged.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "A lower WER always means a better model."
    ✓ Only with the same normaliser, averaging and test set. Changing the normaliser alone moved Whisper from 100.3% to 7.8% in the run under "Scoring Whisper properly".
  • ✗ "Real recordings will get the highest UTMOS."
    ✓ Both TTS systems scored higher than the audiobook recordings (4.29 and 4.21 against 3.73). UTMOS predicts what its training listeners liked, so it is a proxy, not ground truth.
  • ✗ "A speaker similarity of 0.89 means the same speaker."
    ✓ WavLM-SV cosines run high for any speech: MMS-TTS's different, synthetic voice scored 0.89. Thresholds come from real same-speaker and different-speaker pairs (Lesson 6.6).
  • ✗ "Thirty utterances are enough to rank two ASR systems."
    ✓ The bootstrap interval was 4.7% to 11.8%; two systems a point or two apart cannot be told apart on a set this small.

Idea 1 · ASR metrics

WER (Word Error Rate). (S + D + I) / N. Lowercase, strip punctuation, normalize numbers before scoring. Use jiwer or OpenAI's whisper_normalizer. For scale, professional transcribers made 5.9% errors on the Switchboard conversational test set in Microsoft's 2016 "human parity" study (11.3% on the harder CallHome set).

CER (Character Error Rate). Same formula, character-level. Used for tone languages (Mandarin, Cantonese) where word segmentation is ambiguous.

RTFx (inverse real-time factor). Audio seconds processed per wall-clock second. Higher is better. On the Open ASR Leaderboard's GPU measurements, NVIDIA's Parakeet-TDT-0.6B v2 reaches an RTFx of about 3,386 and Whisper large-v3 about 146 (Lesson 6.4).

First-token latency. Wall-clock from audio input to first transcript token. Critical for streaming. Vendors publish their own figures; measure from your own region and network.

Idea 2 · TTS metrics

MOS (Mean Opinion Score). 1-5 human rating. Gold standard but slow. Collect 20+ listeners per sample, 100+ samples per model.

UTMOS (2022-2026). Learned MOS predictor. UTMOS22 (University of Tokyo) scored highest on several metrics of the VoiceMOS Challenge 2022; it predicts system averages well and single clips less well, and it was trained on English TTS and voice-conversion samples. Many TTS papers and model cards report it next to round-trip WER.

SECS (Speaker Encoder Cosine Similarity). For voice cloning. Cosine between speaker embeddings (ECAPA, WavLM-SV) of the reference and the cloned output. The threshold for "same speaker" depends on the embedding model; calibrate it on real same-speaker and different-speaker pairs (Lesson 6.6).

WER-on-ASR-round-trip. Run Whisper over TTS output, compute WER against the input text. Catches intelligibility regressions. 2026 SOTA: < 2% WER on English test sets (Mandarin sets report CER instead).

TTFA (time-to-first-audio). Wall-clock latency from request to the first audio sample; it depends on hardware and streaming support more than on the model card, so measure it (Lesson 6.12).

Idea 3 · Voice-cloning-specific

SECS + MOS + CER as a triple. Cloning that scores high SECS but low MOS means timbre-right-but-unnatural; the opposite means natural voice but wrong speaker.

Idea 4 · Speaker verification

EER (Equal Error Rate). The threshold where False Accept Rate equals False Reject Rate. SpeechBrain's ECAPA-TDNN checkpoint reports 0.80% on VoxCeleb1-O (cleaned trial list; Lesson 6.6).

minDCF (min Detection Cost). Weighted cost at a chosen operating point (often FAR=0.01). More production-relevant than EER.

Idea 5 · Diarization

DER (Diarization Error Rate). (FA + Miss + Confusion) / total_speaker_time. Missed speech + false-alarm speech + speaker-confusion, each as a fraction. On AMI meetings, pyannote's open community-1 pipeline reports 17.0% DER and its hosted precision-2 12.9% (Lesson 6.6).

JER (Jaccard Error Rate). Alternative to DER, robust to short-segment bias.

Idea 6 · Audio classification

Multi-label: mAP (mean Average Precision) over all classes. AudioSet-2M: 0.486 mAP for BEATs (iter3), 0.506 for an ensemble (BEATs paper; Lesson 6.3).

Multi-class exclusive: top-1, top-5 accuracy. Speech Commands v2: 98.3% top-1 for BEATs (iter3) and Audio-MAE (BEATs paper).

Imbalanced: macro F1 + per-class recall. Report per-class — aggregate accuracy hides which classes fail.

Idea 7 · Music generation

FAD (Fréchet Audio Distance). Distance between VGGish-embedding distributions of real vs generated audio. Lower is better. Values are comparable only with the same embedding model (VGGish, CLAP, ...) and the same reference set; Lesson 6.9 measured FAD with CLAP embeddings.

CLAP Score. Text-audio alignment score using CLAP embeddings. > 0.3 = reasonable alignment.

Listening panel MOS. Still the final word for consumer-grade music. At its launch (September 2025) Suno reported an ELO of 1,293 for v5 on its own paired-preference benchmark (v4.5+: 1,208); the method was not published, so it is a vendor number. Independent arenas with blind votes, such as Artificial Analysis's music leaderboards, are the comparable alternative; TTS Arena ranks speech, not music.

Idea 8 · Audio-language benchmarks

MMAU (Massive Multi-Audio Understanding). 10k audio-QA pairs.

MMAU-Pro. 5,305 instances testing 49 skills across speech, sound, music and their mixtures, including multi-audio and open-ended questions. In the paper the best model, Gemini 2.5 Flash, scored 59.2% and the best open model, Audio Flamingo 3, 51.7% (Lesson 6.10).

LongAudioBench. Multi-minute clips with semantic queries. Introduced with Audio Flamingo 2; results come mostly from model authors, so check the evaluation setup.

AudioCaps / Clotho. Captioning benchmarks. SPICE, CIDEr, FENSE metrics.

Idea 9 · Streaming speech-to-speech

Latency P50 / P95 / P99. Wall-clock from end-of-user-speech to first audible response. Moshi: about 200 ms on an L4 GPU (Kyutai's figure); hosted speech-to-speech APIs depend on region and network (Lesson 6.15).

WER / MOS on the output.

Barge-in responsiveness. Time from user interrupt to assistant mute. Target < 150 ms.

Step 1 · WER with normalization

import jiwer

# jiwer 3+ renamed the arguments (reference / hypothesis), and a transform must end in a list of word lists.
transform = jiwer.Compose([jiwer.ToLowerCase(), jiwer.RemovePunctuation(), jiwer.RemoveMultipleSpaces(),
                           jiwer.Strip(), jiwer.ReduceToListOfListOfWords()])
score = jiwer.wer(
    reference="Please turn on the lights.",
    hypothesis="please turn on the light",
    reference_transform=transform,
    hypothesis_transform=transform,
)
print(f"WER {score:.3f}  (1 substitution in 5 words)")
print(f"without normalisation: {jiwer.wer('Please turn on the lights.', 'please turn on the light'):.3f}")

Scoring Whisper properly

Whisper base.en on 40 LibriSpeech utterances: corpus WER under three normalisations, corpus WER against the mean of per-utterance WERs, a bootstrap interval, a slice by length, and speed as RTFx and latency percentiles.

import io, time
import numpy as np, torch, soundfile as sf
from datasets import load_dataset, Audio
from transformers import WhisperProcessor, WhisperForConditionalGeneration

libri = load_dataset("hf-internal-testing/librispeech_asr_dummy", "clean", split="validation").cast_column("audio", Audio(decode=False))
def clip(i):
    return sf.read(io.BytesIO(libri[i]["audio"]["bytes"]), dtype="float32")[0]
wproc = WhisperProcessor.from_pretrained("openai/whisper-base.en")
asr = WhisperForConditionalGeneration.from_pretrained("openai/whisper-base.en").eval()
def transcribe(x):
    with torch.no_grad():
        ids = asr.generate(wproc(x, sampling_rate=16000, return_tensors="pt").input_features, max_new_tokens=128)
    return wproc.batch_decode(ids, skip_special_tokens=True)[0].strip()

ids = [i for i in range(len(libri)) if len(libri[i]["text"].split()) <= 30][:30]   # the shorter utterances, for time
refs, hyps, secs, lat = [], [], [], []
for i in ids:
    x = clip(i); t0 = time.perf_counter()
    hyps.append(transcribe(x)); lat.append(time.perf_counter() - t0)
    refs.append(libri[i]["text"]); secs.append(len(x) / 16000)
print(f"{len(ids)} utterances, {sum(secs)/60:.1f} min; Whisper base.en on 2 CPU cores")
print(f"example  ref: {refs[3][:70]!r}\n         hyp: {hyps[3][:70]!r}")

basic = jiwer.Compose([jiwer.ToLowerCase(), jiwer.RemovePunctuation(), jiwer.RemoveMultipleSpaces(), jiwer.Strip()])
norms = {"none (raw strings)": lambda s: s, "lower-case + strip punctuation": basic, "Whisper English normaliser": wproc.tokenizer.normalize}
print("corpus WER by normalisation:")
for name, f in norms.items():
    print(f"  {name:32s} {jiwer.wer([f(r) for r in refs], [f(h) for h in hyps]):6.1%}")

N = wproc.tokenizer.normalize
R, H = [N(r) for r in refs], [N(h) for h in hyps]
per = np.array([jiwer.wer(r, h) for r, h in zip(R, H)]); nw = np.array([len(r.split()) for r in R])
errs = per * nw
print(f"corpus WER (total errors / total words) {errs.sum() / nw.sum():.1%}; mean of per-utterance WERs {per.mean():.1%}; "
      f"{np.mean(per == 0):.0%} of utterances perfect, worst {per.max():.0%}")
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
boot = [errs[s].sum() / nw[s].sum() for s in (rng.integers(0, len(ids), len(ids)) for _ in range(2000))]
w = int(per.argmax()); print(f"worst utterance: ref {R[w][:60]!r}")
print(f"                 hyp {H[w][:60]!r}")
print(f"95% bootstrap interval over utterances: {np.percentile(boot, 2.5):.1%} to {np.percentile(boot, 97.5):.1%}")
short = np.array(secs) < np.median(secs)
for name, m in [("shorter half", short), ("longer half", ~short)]:
    print(f"  {name}: WER {errs[m].sum() / nw[m].sum():.1%}")
lat = np.array(lat)
print(f"RTFx {sum(secs) / lat.sum():.1f} (audio seconds per compute second); latency per utterance "
      f"p50 {np.percentile(lat, 50):.2f} s, p95 {np.percentile(lat, 95):.2f} s, max {lat.max():.2f} s")

Reading this output

Without normalisation the WER is about 100%: LibriSpeech references are upper-case and unpunctuated while Whisper writes cased, punctuated text, so nearly every word "differs". Lower-casing and stripping punctuation gives 8.8%; Whisper's English normaliser, which also standardises spellings, contractions and number formats, 7.8%. Averaging per-utterance WERs gives 12.5% instead of the corpus 7.8%, because short utterances, where one error is a large fraction, count as much as long ones: the shorter half of the utterances has 13.0% WER against 5.5% for the longer half. The worst utterance is four words long: "not exactly returned kaliko" became "not exactly we have turned calico", four errors in four words, 100% (the reference's spelling of a name counts as an error too). Half the utterances are perfect. The bootstrap interval, 4.7% to 11.8%, is wide: with 30 utterances, two systems a point or two apart cannot be told apart. Step 2 · TTS round-trip WER

def ttr_wer(tts_model, asr_model, texts):
    errors = []
    for txt in texts:
        audio = tts_model.synthesize(txt)
        recog = asr_model.transcribe(audio)
        errors.append(wer(truth=txt, hypothesis=recog))
    return sum(errors) / len(errors)

Step 3 · SECS for voice cloning

import io, numpy as np, torch, soundfile as sf, librosa
from datasets import load_dataset, Audio
from transformers import AutoFeatureExtractor, WavLMForXVector

# SpeechBrain's ECAPA needs torchaudio, which this page cannot install; WavLM-SV (Lesson 6.6) gives the same kind of score.
libri = load_dataset("hf-internal-testing/librispeech_asr_dummy", "clean", split="validation").cast_column("audio", Audio(decode=False))
def clip(i):
    return sf.read(io.BytesIO(libri[i]["audio"]["bytes"]), dtype="float32")[0]
sv_fe = AutoFeatureExtractor.from_pretrained("microsoft/wavlm-base-plus-sv")
sv = WavLMForXVector.from_pretrained("microsoft/wavlm-base-plus-sv").eval()
def embed(x):
    with torch.no_grad():
        return torch.nn.functional.normalize(sv(**sv_fe(x, sampling_rate=16000, return_tensors="pt")).embeddings, dim=-1)

emb_ref = embed(clip(0))                                       # "reference.wav": the speaker's enrolment clip
for name, x in [("same reader, another sentence", clip(1)),
                ("same clip pitched up 4 semitones", librosa.effects.pitch_shift(clip(0), sr=16000, n_steps=4))]:
    secs = torch.nn.functional.cosine_similarity(emb_ref, embed(x), dim=-1).item()
    print(f"SECS {secs:.2f}  {name}")

Three metrics, five kinds of speech

Eight LibriSpeech sentences as real recordings, with noise added, through EnCodec at 1.5 kbps, and synthesised by two TTS systems. Each scored by UTMOS22, by Whisper's round-trip WER and by speaker similarity (WavLM-SV) to the LibriSpeech reader.

import sys, types, librosa
from transformers import (AutoModel, AutoFeatureExtractor, WavLMForXVector, SpeechT5Processor, SpeechT5ForTextToSpeech,
                          SpeechT5HifiGan, VitsModel, AutoTokenizer, EncodecModel)

# UTMOS22 (strong learner), the learned MOS predictor most TTS papers report, as a transformers port.
# Its code imports torchaudio for one resampling call; torchaudio is not installed here, so give it a stand-in.
ta = types.ModuleType("torchaudio"); ta.functional = types.ModuleType("torchaudio.functional")
ta.functional.resample = lambda w, a, b: torch.tensor(librosa.resample(w.numpy(), orig_sr=a, target_sr=b))
sys.modules.setdefault("torchaudio", ta); sys.modules.setdefault("torchaudio.functional", ta.functional)
utmos = AutoModel.from_pretrained("prj-beatrice/utmos22-torch-native", trust_remote_code=True,   # runs code from the repo:
                                  revision="eecd3e6e7ad9dd794308a4338c3ac7cb34691af9").eval()  # pin the version you reviewed
def mos(x):
    with torch.no_grad():
        return float(utmos(torch.tensor(x, dtype=torch.float32), sampling_rate=16000).scores[0])
sv_fe = AutoFeatureExtractor.from_pretrained("microsoft/wavlm-base-plus-sv")
sv = WavLMForXVector.from_pretrained("microsoft/wavlm-base-plus-sv").eval()
def spk(x):
    with torch.no_grad():
        e = sv(**sv_fe(x, sampling_rate=16000, return_tensors="pt")).embeddings[0]
    return torch.nn.functional.normalize(e, dim=-1)

sel = [i for i in range(40, len(libri)) if 8 <= len(libri[i]["text"].split()) <= 20][:8]
texts = [libri[i]["text"].lower() for i in sel]
real = [clip(i) for i in sel]
ref_voice = spk(np.concatenate([clip(0), clip(1)]))              # the same reader, other sentences
tproc = SpeechT5Processor.from_pretrained("microsoft/speecht5_tts")
st5 = SpeechT5ForTextToSpeech.from_pretrained("microsoft/speecht5_tts").eval()
hifigan = SpeechT5HifiGan.from_pretrained("microsoft/speecht5_hifigan").eval()
xv = load_dataset("Matthijs/cmu-arctic-xvectors", revision="refs/convert/parquet", split="validation")
slt = [k for k, f in enumerate(xv["filename"]) if "cmu_us_slt" in f][0]          # the female ARCTIC voice of Lesson 6.7
vtok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("facebook/mms-tts-eng"); vits = VitsModel.from_pretrained("facebook/mms-tts-eng").eval()
enc = EncodecModel.from_pretrained("facebook/encodec_24khz").eval()
def encodec(x, bw):
    y = torch.tensor(librosa.resample(x, orig_sr=16000, target_sr=24000))[None, None]
    with torch.no_grad():
        e = enc.encode(y, bandwidth=bw); z = enc.decode(e.audio_codes, e.audio_scales)[0][0, 0].numpy()
    return librosa.resample(z, orig_sr=24000, target_sr=16000)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def noisy(x, snr):
    n = rng.standard_normal(len(x)).astype(np.float32)
    return x + n * np.sqrt((x ** 2).mean() / (n ** 2).mean() / 10 ** (snr / 10))
torch.manual_seed(0)                                            # SpeechT5 keeps dropout on at inference
with torch.no_grad():
    systems = {
        "real recordings": real,
        "real + white noise, 10 dB SNR": [noisy(x, 10) for x in real],
        "real via EnCodec 1.5 kbps": [encodec(x, 1.5) for x in real],
        "SpeechT5 + HiFi-GAN (ARCTIC voice)": [hifigan(st5.generate_speech(tproc(text=t, return_tensors="pt")["input_ids"],
                                               torch.tensor(xv[slt]["xvector"])[None])).numpy() for t in texts],

Reading this output

The three metrics disagree, which is the point. White noise at 10 dB SNR collapses UTMOS from 3.73 to 1.37, more than doubles round-trip WER (10.9% to 26.7%) and barely moves speaker similarity (0.92): the voice is still recognisable but unpleasant. EnCodec at 1.5 kbps (Lesson 6.13) does similar damage. Both TTS systems score higher UTMOS than the real recordings (4.29 and 4.21 against 3.73): clean, evenly read synthetic speech is what the predictor was trained to like, while the audiobook recordings carry room sound. SpeechT5 is also more intelligible to Whisper than the real reader (5.9% WER). Speaker similarity separates the systems differently: SpeechT5 speaks with an ARCTIC voice (0.45 to the reader), while MMS-TTS scores 0.89 despite being a different, synthetic voice. WavLM-SV cosines run high for any speech, so 0.89 does not mean "same speaker"; thresholds must be calibrated on real pairs, as in Lesson 6.6. With 8 sentences, treat differences of a few points of WER as noise.

Step 5 · EER for speaker verification (same code as Lesson 6)

def eer(same_scores, diff_scores):
    thresholds = sorted(set(same_scores + diff_scores))
    best = (1.0, 0.0)
    for t in thresholds:
        far = sum(1 for s in diff_scores if s >= t) / len(diff_scores)
        frr = sum(1 for s in same_scores if s < t) / len(same_scores)
        if abs(far - frr) < best[0]:
            best = (abs(far - frr), (far + frr) / 2)
    return best[1]

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

Pair every deploy with a fixed eval harness that runs on every model update. Three cardinal rules:

  1. Normalize before scoring. Lowercase, punctuation-strip, number-expand. Report the normalization rule.
  2. Report distributions, not averages. P50/P95/P99 for latency. Per-class recall for classification. Per-category for MMAU.
  3. Run one canonical public benchmark. Even if your production data differs, reporting on Open ASR / TTS Arena / MMAU lets reviewers compare apples-to-apples.
Break it on purpose

Score Whisper's transcripts against references that still contain LibriSpeech's upper case but with the hypotheses lower-cased by Whisper's normaliser (normalise only one side). What WER do you get, and what does it tell you about checking a scoring script? (Solution B1.)

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Leaderboards in use: the Hugging Face Open ASR Leaderboard (WER and RTFx across eight English sets, plus multilingual and long-form tracks); TTS Arena and Artificial Analysis's speech and music arenas (blind pairwise votes, ELO); MMAU-Pro for audio-language models.
  • Automatic quality metrics in TTS model cards: UTMOS (naturalness), round-trip WER or CER with Whisper or Paraformer (intelligibility), speaker similarity with a WavLM-based verifier (cloning); human preference tests remain the final word.
  • Speech-enhancement and call-quality teams use Microsoft's DNSMOS (ITU-T P.808 / P.835 style predicted MOS for signal, background and overall quality).
  • Normalisers: OpenAI's Whisper English normaliser (in transformers as tokenizer.normalize) is the de facto standard for English WER on leaderboards.

Sources: Open ASR Leaderboard, TTS Arena, Artificial Analysis, UTMOS paper, UTMOS22 port, DNSMOS, Human parity study, jiwer.

Pitfalls

  • UTMOS extrapolation. Trained on VCTK-style clean speech; scores noisy / cloned / emotional audio poorly.
  • MOS panel bias. 20 Amazon Mechanical Turk workers ≠ 20 target users. Pay for a domain panel if stakes are high.
  • FAD depends on reference set. Compare against the same reference distribution across models.
  • Aggregate WER. A 5% WER overall can hide 30% WER on accented speech. Report by demographic slice.
  • Public benchmark saturation. Most frontier models are near the ceiling on standard benchmarks. Build an in-house held-out set that reflects your traffic.
Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Each metric measures one axis: intelligibility (WER), naturalness (MOS, UTMOS), identity (SECS, EER), distribution (FAD), speed (RTFx, latency).
  • A metric is a recipe: normalisation, averaging and test set must be reported with the number.
  • Report intervals and slices, not just averages.
  • Learned predictors (UTMOS) are proxies; confirm important decisions with listeners.
  • Public leaderboards for comparison, your own held-out set for decisions.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Noise hurts naturalness most

UTMOS: from 3.73 to 1.37, a fall of about two-thirds. Round-trip WER rises from 10.9% to 26.7%, and speaker similarity barely changes (0.97 to 0.92). Noise mostly damages perceived quality; the words and the voice survive better.

SolutionB1 · Normalise both sides

About 100%, like the raw row: every reference word is upper-case and every normalised hypothesis word lower-case, so nearly every word counts as a substitution. A WER near 100% from a model known to work almost always means a scoring bug, not a bad model. Test a scoring script on a hypothesis identical to the reference (it must give 0%) and on one known error, and apply the same normaliser to both sides.
