In plain words first
The idea in one line

GPT predicts the next word. BERT predicts a missing word. One sentence of difference — and half a decade of everything embedding-shaped.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means

Bidirectional "Looks both ways" Encoder attention has no causal mask — every position sees every other position.

The problem

In plain words first

BERT is a transformer encoder trained by a fill-in-the-blank game. Take a sentence, hide some of its words, and ask the model to guess them. Because an encoder has no causal mask, it can use the words on both sides of the gap, something a left-to-right model like GPT cannot do. After training on billions of sentences, the model's internal vectors capture what words mean in context, and a small "head" added on top turns it into a classifier, a search embedder or a reranker. This lesson checks the masking rules on 100,000 tokens, then works with real trained encoders: ModernBERT filling blanks with clues that come after the gap (against GPT-2, which cannot see them), ModernBERT spotting a wrong word with no mask at all, and two fine-tuned BERTs ranking documents for a search query. Nothing is trained.

In 2018 every NLP task — sentiment, NER, QA, entailment — trained its own model from scratch on its own labeled data. There was no pre-trained "understand English" checkpoint you could fine-tune. ELMo (2018) showed you could pre-train contextual embeddings with a bidirectional LSTM; it helped but did not generalize.

BERT (Devlin et al. 2018) asked: what if we took a transformer encoder, trained it on every sentence on the internet, and forced it to predict missing words from context on both sides? Then you fine-tune one head on your downstream task. Parameter efficiency was a revelation.

The result: within 18 months BERT and its variants (RoBERTa, ALBERT, ELECTRA) dominated the NLP leaderboards of the time. By the end of 2019 Google Search was using BERT to understand queries, and BERT-style encoders became the default for classification and semantic search.

In 2026 encoder-only models are still the right tool for classification, retrieval, and structured extraction: they read the whole input in one forward pass with no generation loop, small ones run comfortably on a CPU, and their embeddings are the backbone of most retrieval stacks. ModernBERT (Dec 2024) pushed the architecture to 8K context with Flash Attention + RoPE + GeGLU.

The idea

Idea 1 · The training signal

Take a sentence: the quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog.

Mask 15% of tokens randomly:

input:  the [MASK] brown fox jumps [MASK] the lazy dog
target: the  quick brown fox jumps  over  the lazy dog

Train the model to predict the original tokens at masked positions. Because the encoder is bidirectional, predicting [MASK] at position 1 can use brown fox jumps at positions 2+. That is the thing GPT cannot do.

Idea 2 · The BERT mask rules

Of the 15% of tokens selected for prediction:

  • 80% are replaced with [MASK].
  • 10% are replaced with a random token.
  • 10% are left unchanged.

Why not always [MASK]? Because [MASK] never appears at inference time. Training the model to expect [MASK] at 100% of masked positions would create a distribution shift between pretraining and fine-tuning. The 10% random + 10% unchanged keeps the model honest.

Idea 3 · Next Sentence Prediction (NSP) — and why it was dropped

Original BERT also trained on NSP: given two sentences A and B, predict if B follows A. RoBERTa (2019) found that removing it matched or slightly improved downstream results. Modern encoders skip it.

Idea 4 · What changed in 2026: ModernBERT

The 2024 ModernBERT paper rebuilt the block with 2026 primitives:

Component Original BERT (2018) ModernBERT (2024)
Positional Learned absolute RoPE
Activation GELU GeGLU
Normalization Post-norm LayerNorm Pre-norm LayerNorm, no bias terms
Attention Full dense Alternating local (128) + global
Context length 512 8192
Tokenizer WordPiece BPE

And unlike the 2018 stack, it is Flash-Attention-native. The paper reports faster long-input inference than earlier encoders together with higher GLUE and retrieval scores.

Idea 5 · Use cases that still pick an encoder in 2026

Task Why encoder beats decoder
Retrieval / semantic search embeddings Bidirectional context = better embedding quality per token
Classification (sentiment, intent, toxicity) One forward pass; no generation overhead
NER / token labeling Per-position output, natively bidirectional
Zero-shot entailment (NLI) Classifier head on top of encoder
Reranker for RAG Cross-encoder scoring, 10x faster than LLM rerankers

The same idea as a formula

The MLM objective. Choose a random set M of positions (15% of them), corrupt the input at those positions to get x~, and minimise

LMLM​=−∣M∣1​i∈M∑​logpθ​(xi​∣x~)

Every prediction may use the whole corrupted sequence, left and right. Positions outside M get the label −100 and contribute nothing.

The corruption, per token. Selected with probability 0.15, then replaced by [MASK] with probability 0.8, by a random token with 0.1, left alone with 0.1. So each token is [MASK] with probability 0.12, random with 0.015 and an unchanged target with 0.015. Because any visible token might secretly be a target, the model must keep a correct prediction for every position, not only for [MASK] ones.

Signal per sequence. A causal language model gets a loss at all n−1 positions; MLM at about 0.15n. That is why ELECTRA trains a discriminator to judge every token as original or replaced, extracting more signal per sequence.

Using the encoder. The final vectors h1​,…,hn​ feed task heads:

  • classification: softmax(Wh[CLS]​+b), or a mean of the hi​;
  • token labelling (NER): a softmax at every position;
  • bi-encoder embedding: e(x)=normalise(n1​∑i​hi​), relevance =e(q)⋅e(d);
  • cross-encoder: one input [CLS] q [SEP] d [SEP], relevance =w⋅h[CLS]​.

Search cost. For N documents, a bi-encoder embeds them once offline and answers a query with one encoder pass plus N dot products; a cross-encoder needs N full passes per query, so it only re-ranks a short list.

Worked example
One sequence and one search by hand

A 512-token sequence. About 0.15×512≈77 positions are selected: about 61 become [MASK], 8 a random token, 8 stay as they are. The loss uses those 77 positions; a GPT-style model would get 511 next-token losses from the same sequence, about 6.6 times as many.

Re-ranking for retrieval. A query of 10 tokens, 100 candidate passages of 300 tokens each:

  • Bi-encoder at query time: one pass over 10 tokens, then 100 dot products (passages embedded in advance).
  • Cross-encoder: 100 passes over about 310 tokens each, about 31,000 tokens of encoder work per query.

Production systems therefore retrieve the top 100 with embeddings, then re-rank only those with a cross-encoder.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "BERT only learns from the [MASK] tokens it sees."
    ✓ It learns from the 15% selected positions, a fifth of which are not [MASK]; so it must also judge ordinary-looking tokens, which is what lets it notice a wrong word with no mask present (the run under "Use it").
  • ✗ "An encoder can generate text if you mask the next position."
    ✓ It predicts all masked positions independently in one pass, with no left-to-right factorisation of the probability; decoders are built for generation.
  • ✗ "An embedding model is a different architecture from BERT."
    ✓ The encoder is the same; only the fine-tuning loss (contrastive) and the pooling differ. A cross-encoder reranker is the same encoder again with a one-number head.

Step 1 · masking logic

See code/main.py. The function create_mlm_batch takes a list of token IDs, a vocab size, and a mask probability. Returns input IDs (with masks applied) and labels (only at masked positions, -100 elsewhere — PyTorch's ignore index convention).

def create_mlm_batch(tokens, vocab_size, mask_prob=0.15, rng=None):
    input_ids = list(tokens)
    labels = [-100] * len(tokens)
    for i, t in enumerate(tokens):
        if rng.random() < mask_prob:
            labels[i] = t
            r = rng.random()
            if r < 0.8:
                input_ids[i] = MASK_ID
            elif r < 0.9:
                input_ids[i] = rng.randrange(vocab_size)
            # else: keep original
    return input_ids, labels

Embedding models are fine-tuned BERT. sentence-transformers models like all-MiniLM-L6-v2 are BERTs trained with contrastive loss. The encoder is the same. The loss changed.

Cross-encoder rerankers are also fine-tuned BERT. Pair-classification on [CLS] query [SEP] doc [SEP]. The bidirectional attention between query and doc is exactly what gives cross-encoders their quality edge over biencoders.

When not to pick BERT in 2026. Anything generative. The encoder has no sensible way to autoregressively produce tokens. Also: tasks whose output is free-form text or whose instructions change per request, where a small instruction-tuned decoder (for example Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct) is more flexible.

Filling blanks with clues from both sides

ModernBERT-base fills masked words; then GPT-2, a left-to-right model, predicts the word after "Every morning the", the only context it has at the gap.

import torch
from transformers import AutoModelForMaskedLM, AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM

repo = "answerdotai/ModernBERT-base"
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(repo)
mlm = AutoModelForMaskedLM.from_pretrained(repo).eval()

def fill(text, k=5):
    enc = tok(text, return_tensors="pt")
    pos = (enc.input_ids[0] == tok.mask_token_id).nonzero()[0, 0]
    with torch.no_grad():
        p = mlm(**enc).logits[0, pos].softmax(-1)
    top = p.topk(k)
    return ", ".join(f"{tok.decode(int(i)).strip()} {v:.2f}" for v, i in zip(top.values, top.indices))

for s in ["The capital of France is [MASK].",
          "Every morning the [MASK] barked at the mailman.",
          "Every morning the [MASK] purred on the sofa.",
          "She deposited the cheque at the [MASK] before it closed."]:
    print(f"{s:58s} -> {fill(s.replace('[MASK]', tok.mask_token))}")

# A left-to-right model sees only the words before the gap: "Every morning the" in both sentences.
gtok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("openai-community/gpt2")
gpt = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("openai-community/gpt2").eval()
with torch.no_grad():
    p = gpt(**gtok("Every morning the", return_tensors="pt")).logits[0, -1].softmax(-1)
top = p.topk(5)
print("\nGPT-2 after 'Every morning the' (the same for both sentences):",
      ", ".join(f"{gtok.decode(int(i))!r} {v:.3f}" for v, i in zip(top.values, top.indices)),

No mask at all: noticing a wrong word

The 10% random replacements train the model to check every token. Here ModernBERT's MLM head predicts every position of a clean sentence and of the same sentence with one word swapped.

# No [MASK] at all: what does the MLM head predict at every position?
def unmasked(text):
    enc = tok(text, return_tensors="pt")
    with torch.no_grad():
        p = mlm(**enc).logits[0].softmax(-1)
    ids = enc.input_ids[0]
    rows = []
    for i in range(1, len(ids) - 1):                          # skip the special start and end tokens
        best = int(p[i].argmax())
        rows.append((tok.decode(int(ids[i])).strip(), p[i, ids[i]].item(), tok.decode(best).strip(), p[i, best].item()))
    return rows

clean = "I drank a cup of hot coffee before the meeting started."
noisy = "I drank a cup of hot carpet before the meeting started."
for label, s in [("clean", clean), ("one word swapped", noisy)]:
    rows = unmasked(s)
    kept = sum(w == b for w, _, b, _ in rows)
    print(f"{label}: '{s}'\n   the head's top guess equals the input token at {kept} of {len(rows)} positions")
    for w, pw, b, pb in rows:
        if w != b or w in ("coffee", "carpet"):
            print(f"   at '{w}': P(input token) = {pw:.3f}; top guess '{b}' ({pb:.2f})")

Two fine-tuned BERTs ranking search results

A bi-encoder (all-MiniLM-L6-v2, embeds query and documents separately, cosine similarity) and a cross-encoder reranker (ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2, reads query and document together) score the same five documents.

import torch, torch.nn.functional as F
from transformers import AutoModel, AutoModelForSequenceClassification, AutoTokenizer

query = "How do I reset a forgotten router password?"
docs = ["To restore factory settings, hold the reset button on the back of the router for 30 seconds, then log in with the default password on the label.",
        "Our router supports WPA3 and dual-band Wi-Fi with speeds up to 1.2 Gbps.",
        "If you forgot your email password, use the 'Forgot password' link on the sign-in page.",
        "Routers forward packets between networks using routing tables.",
        "The default admin password is printed on the sticker under the device; change it after first login."]

# Bi-encoder: a BERT fine-tuned with a contrastive loss; query and documents are embedded separately.
btok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
benc = AutoModel.from_pretrained("sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2").eval()
def embed(texts):
    enc = btok(texts, padding=True, return_tensors="pt")
    with torch.no_grad():
        h = benc(**enc).last_hidden_state
    m = enc.attention_mask.unsqueeze(-1).float()
    return F.normalize((h * m).sum(1) / m.sum(1), dim=-1)          # mean of the token vectors
bi = (embed(docs) @ embed([query]).T).squeeze(1)

# Cross-encoder: a BERT fine-tuned to score one (query, document) pair, read together in one input.
ctok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2")
cenc = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained("cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L6-v2").eval()
with torch.no_grad():
    cross = cenc(**ctok([query] * len(docs), docs, padding=True, truncation=True, return_tensors="pt")).logits.squeeze(-1)

print(f"query: {query}\n")
print(f"{'bi-encoder cosine':>18} {'cross-encoder score':>20}   document")
for i in sorted(range(len(docs)), key=lambda i: -cross[i]):
    print(f"{bi[i]:18.3f} {cross[i]:20.2f}   {docs[i][:70]}...")
print(f"\nbi-encoder ranking:    {[int(i) for i in bi.argsort(descending=True)]}")
print(f"cross-encoder ranking: {[int(i) for i in cross.argsort(descending=True)]}")
n_enc = sum(p.numel() for n, p in cenc.named_parameters() if not n.startswith("classifier"))
print(f"\nthe cross-encoder is a {cenc.config.num_hidden_layers}-layer BERT ({n_enc / 1e6:.1f}M parameters) plus a head of "
      f"{sum(p.numel() for n, p in cenc.named_parameters() if n.startswith('classifier'))} parameters "
      f"(one linear layer on the [CLS] vector)")

Break it on purpose

In create_mlm_batch, set labels[i] = t for every position, not only the selected ones, and train as before. The code runs and the loss drops very fast. What has the model actually learned to do, and why would it be a poor encoder? (Solution B1.)

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Encoders run retrieval. Embedding models built on BERT-style encoders (for example the BGE, E5, GTE and Nomic families and all-MiniLM-L6-v2) serve most vector-search and RAG pipelines, through sentence-transformers, Hugging Face's Text Embeddings Inference and the vector databases' built-in embedders.
  • Cross-encoder rerankers (BGE-reranker, the MS MARCO MiniLM cross-encoders, and hosted rerank APIs such as Cohere Rerank) re-score the top results of a first-stage search in production RAG systems.
  • ModernBERT (December 2024) updated the encoder with RoPE, GeGLU, alternating local and global attention and an 8,192-token context, and is supported directly by transformers; classifiers for moderation, intent and PII detection are still mostly fine-tuned encoders.

Sources: ModernBERT, Sentence Transformers, Text Embeddings Inference, MTEB leaderboard, Cohere Rerank.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • MLM: hide 15% of tokens (80% [MASK], 10% random, 10% unchanged) and predict them from both sides.
  • Bidirectional context is what an encoder adds; it cannot generate left to right.
  • The same pretrained encoder becomes a classifier, an embedder or a cross-encoder by changing the head and the fine-tuning loss.
  • Retrieval: bi-encoders for the first pass over millions of documents, cross-encoders to re-rank the top few.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Below 1%

Below 1%: 0.13% for " dog" and 0.05% for " cat". From "Every morning the", hundreds of continuations are possible (GPT-2's top guess, " sun", has only 1.5%), and the decisive word comes after the gap, where a left-to-right model cannot look. ModernBERT, reading both sides, gives dog 0.79 when the verb is "barked" and cat 0.66 when it is "purred". A decoder can only use the right-hand clue later, by revising its interpretation once the verb arrives; for filling a gap it is the wrong tool.

SolutionB1 · It learned to copy

With labels at every position, 85% of the loss comes from tokens that are visible in the input, and the easiest way to predict them is to copy them through: attention can pass each position's own embedding straight to the output. The loss falls quickly, but almost all of it measures copying; the 15% of positions that require understanding context are swamped. Fine-tuned on a downstream task, such an encoder has learned far less about how words relate to each other. Keeping the loss on selected positions only, the −100 labels, is what forces the model to reconstruct from context.
