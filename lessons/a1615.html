In plain words first
The idea in one line

The cheapest aggregation: sample N independent agents, majority-vote. Wang et al. 2022 self-consistency did this with one model sampled N times. Multi-agent extends it with heterogeneous agents to escape monoculture — different models, different prompts, different temperatures, different contexts. Beyond majority vote, debate topology matters: MultiAgentBench (arXiv:2503.01935, ACL 2025) evaluated star / chain / tree / graph coordination and found graph best for research, and that adding agents beyond three lowered its overall score. AgentVerse (ICLR 2024) documents volunteer, conformity and destructive behaviors; conformity is both a feature (finding consensus) and, in debate, a risk (groupthink, Lesson 16.24). This lesson maps the topology space, builds each variant, and measures the coordination tax.

Problem

In plain words first

The simplest way to combine agents is to ask several and take a vote. Self-consistency does this with one model sampled several times. Debate lets agents see each other's answers and revise. Topology is who sees whose answers: a star (everyone reports to a hub), a chain, a tree or a fully connected graph. Planning and reasoning patterns for a single agent are covered in Zero to Agents 3.8 (linked above). This lesson measures what the lesson's simulator does and does not show, then compares one prompt sampled three times with three phrasings and a round of debate on a small real model.

Debate can improve accuracy (Du et al., arXiv:2305.14325). It can also degrade it. Whether debate helps depends on four structural choices:

  1. Who talks to whom (topology).
  2. How many rounds (Du 2023: both rounds and agents matter independently).
  3. Whether agents are heterogeneous (different base models break monoculture).
  4. Whether an adversarial voice is present (steel-manning vs. straw-manning).

Teams that bolt "run 5 agents and vote" onto a task often regress vs. a single agent. The failures are not random. They track topology and heterogeneity. This lesson is the topology map.

Concept

Self-consistency, the single-model baseline

Wang et al. 2022 ("Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning") sampled the same model N times at temperature > 0 and majority-voted on reasoning-path answers. On GSM8K, 40 sampled paths raised PaLM 540B's accuracy by 17.9 points over a single greedy decode. Self-consistency is the single-agent precursor to multi-agent voting.

Limit: self-consistency uses one base model. Errors are correlated by construction. If the model has a systematic bias, all N samples share it.

Multi-agent vote, the heterogeneous extension

Replace N samples with N different agents. Different base models (Claude, GPT, Llama), different prompts, different tool access. The benefit: uncorrelated errors. The cost: different agents cost different amounts; coordinating them adds overhead.

This lesson calls heterogeneous debate with an adversarial voice A-HMAD (Adversarial Heterogeneous Multi-Agent Debate). There is no settled standard name; 2025–2026 papers describe similar designs as heterogeneous multi-agent debate, with different models debating to reduce errors they would share.

The four topologies

star                chain               tree                graph

    ┌─A─┐           A─B─C─D         ┌──A──┐              A───B
    │   │                           │     │              │ × │
    B   C                           B     C              D───C
    │   │                          / \   / \
    D   E                         D   E F   G           (fully connected)

Star: one hub, all others talk only to hub. Equivalent to supervisor-worker without back-channel. Chain: linear, each agent sees the prior one's output. Pipeline-like. Tree: hierarchical, used by hierarchical agent systems (Lesson 16.6). Graph: any-to-any. Includes fully-connected clique and arbitrary DAGs.

The coordination tax (MultiAgentBench)

MultiAgentBench (MARBLE, ACL 2025, arXiv:2503.01935) compared star, chain, tree and graph coordination protocols in its research scenario, alongside coding, gaming and other tasks. What it reported:

  • Graph did best on research tasks, with the best task score, planning efficiency and token use. Information flows any-to-any; agents can critique each other.
  • Star scored about the same as graph on task score.
  • Tree did worst: the highest token use and the lowest task and coordination scores. The paper does not single out chain.
  • More agents, lower overall score. With 1, 3, 5 and 7 agents on research tasks, coordination improved sharply from 1 to 3 agents, task score rose only gradually, and the overall score fell as agents were added. This lesson calls that the coordination tax.

The point where the tax bites is empirical, not fundamental. One reason is context capacity: each agent's context fills with peers' outputs, and the marginal value of agent N+1 drops once everyone can see everyone.

Multi-Agent Debate Strategies ("Should we be going MAD?")

arXiv:2311.17371 (Smit et al., ICML 2024) benchmarks MAD strategies. Its main finding: debate systems in their current form do not reliably outperform other prompting strategies such as self-consistency and ensembling, and they usually cost more calls. They are sensitive to hyperparameters: telling agents how often to agree with each other moved Multi-Persona from worst to best on MedQA. Disagreement helped on a counter-intuitive dataset (CIAR) and hurt on medical ones, so an adversarial voice is not a general win.

AgentVerse emergent patterns

AgentVerse (ICLR 2024, https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2024/file/578e65cdee35d00c708d4c64bce32971-Paper-Conference.pdf) documents three behaviors that emerge from multi-agent collaboration without explicit design (volunteer, conformity and destructive behaviors). Two matter most here:

  • Volunteer. An agent offers help ("I can take the next step") unprompted. Useful: it allocates work to the most-capable agent for a subtask.
  • Conformity. An agent that drifted from the shared goal corrects course after others criticise it. In AgentVerse this is useful. In debate, the same pull makes an agent adopt a peer's answer even when the peer is wrong, the debate form of sycophancy (Lesson 16.14).

Conformity is why debate-until-agreement rewards bullies. Bounded rounds with a separate judge mitigate.

Heterogeneity: the actual knob that moves accuracy

A common heuristic in practice: swapping one of your N agents for a different base model is worth more than adding one more copy of the same model. The intuition is monoculture: each new source of independent errors is worth more than another correlated sample. Measure it on your task; this lesson's small-model run below tests the prompt version of it.

In the limit, heterogeneity can beat numerosity: if five copies of one model share most of their errors, three models with different errors can do better on tasks with clean ground truth.

Jury methods

Sibyl (Wang et al., arXiv:2407.10718), inspired by Minsky's Society of Mind, adds a "multi-agent debate-based jury" that refines the final answer. An Actor proposes an answer and explains its reasoning, and a Critic looks for logical errors in it; the Actor weighs the feedback. Jury methods are a midpoint between plain vote (cheap, monoculture-prone) and full MAD (expensive, conformity-prone).

When vote-with-debate dominates

  • The question has ground truth (fact, math, code behavior). Vote convergence is meaningful.
  • Agents can access different sources or tools (heterogeneity is available).
  • Rounds are bounded (2-3 typical) and there is a separate judge or verifier.
  • Budget allows 3-5 agents. Beyond 5-7 on graph topology, coordination tax dominates.

When vote-with-debate hurts

  • The question is opinion-shaped. Agents converge to whichever answer looks most confident, not most correct.
  • All agents share a base model. Monoculture makes consensus meaningless.
  • Rounds are unbounded. Conformity wins every time.
  • The task is simple. A single agent with self-consistency at N=5 is cheaper and as accurate.

The same idea as a formula

What a vote can reach. With n independent voters each right with probability p, majority accuracy is ∑k>n/2​(kn​)pk(1−p)n−k. With an even n, ties need a rule; breaking them by "first answer" makes an even group no better than the odd group one smaller.

Correlation. If two voters' errors coincide with probability q when both are wrong, the vote can only outvote errors that do not coincide. Copies of one model have high q; different prompts or models lower it.

Cost per correct answer. A topology that spends T tokens per question and is right with probability a costs T/a tokens per correct answer. Doubling T for a gain from 0.97 to 0.98 roughly doubles that cost.

Worked example
Reading the lesson's graph advantage
  • Graph with 3 agents: every agent answers, and the vote is a majority of 3. Accuracy 0.81 in the homogeneous run.
  • Star with 3 agents: the hub only collects, so the vote is a majority of 2 workers, and a 1–1 tie goes to the first. Accuracy 0.73, the same as one agent.
  • Star with 4 agents has 3 workers. Accuracy 0.81, the same as graph with 3.
  • The graph's second round never changes the majority, because agents only move toward it. So in this simulator the graph's lead is one extra vote, bought at twice the tokens.

# The lesson's topologies (imported from main.py), measured on what its benchmark does not vary: errors that
# are correlated, ties, the chain's update rule, conformity in the graph, and tokens per correct answer.
# 2,000 trials per cell; runs in a few seconds.
import random
from main import SimAgent, make_agents, run_star, run_chain, run_tree, run_graph, majority

T = 2000
def acc(run, n, het, **kw):
    return sum(run(make_agents(n, het, t), "RIGHT", random.Random(t * 31 + 7), **kw).accuracy() for t in range(T)) / T

print("1. the lesson's 'monoculture' has independent errors; only the wrong label is shared")
class Shared(SimAgent):
    draw = None                                   # one draw per trial from the shared base model
    rho = 0.0
    def answer(self, correct, rng):
        if rng.random() < Shared.rho:
            return Shared.draw
        return super().answer(correct, rng)
for rho in (0.0, 0.5, 0.9):
    Shared.rho, s = rho, 0
    for t in range(T):
        rng = random.Random(t)
        Shared.draw = "RIGHT" if rng.random() < 0.72 else "WRONG-A"
        agents = [Shared(f"a{i}", 0.72, "WRONG-A") for i in range(7)]
        s += run_star(agents, "RIGHT", rng).accuracy()
    print(f"   star, 7 agents, chance of copying the shared draw {rho:.1f}: accuracy {s / T:.2f}")
print(f"   the lesson's 'heterogeneous' agents differ only in which wrong label they give: star N=5 "
      f"{acc(run_star, 5, False):.2f} -> {acc(run_star, 5, True):.2f}")

print("\n2. ties: an even number of voters, broken by whoever answered first")
ties = 0
for t in range(T):
    rng = random.Random(t * 31 + 7)
    leaves = make_agents(5, True, t)[1:]
    l, r = [a.answer("RIGHT", rng) for a in leaves[:2]], [a.answer("RIGHT", rng) for a in leaves[2:]]
    ties += majority(l) != majority(r)
print(f"   tree with 5 agents: the root's vote between two subtree answers was a tie in {ties / T:.0%} of trials,")
print("   and each subtree's vote between 2 leaves is a tie whenever they disagree; the star's hub polls only N-1 workers")

print("\n3. the chain's update rule ignores which answer is right")
for n in (3, 5, 7, 15):
    print(f"   chain N={n:2d}: accuracy {acc(run_chain, n, False):.2f}")
print("   a later agent replaces the current answer whenever it disagrees and a coin with its accuracy comes up,"
      " whether its own answer is right or wrong")

print("\n4. conformity in the graph: what the second round does")
fixed = broke = held = 0
for t in range(T):
    agents = make_agents(5, False, t); rng = random.Random(t * 31 + 7)
    first = [a.answer("RIGHT", rng) for a in agents]
    r = run_graph(agents, "RIGHT", random.Random(t * 31 + 7))
    before = majority(first) == "RIGHT"
    fixed += (not before) and r.accuracy(); broke += before and not r.accuracy(); held += before == bool(r.accuracy())
print(f"   N=5: round 2 fixed a wrong majority {fixed} times, broke a right one {broke} times, kept the round-1 "
      f"majority {held} of {T}; drifting toward the majority can only lock it in")
print(f"   so the graph's lead is its vote count: graph N=3 {acc(run_graph, 3, False):.2f} = majority of 3, "
      f"star N=3 {acc(run_star, 3, False):.2f} = majority of 2 workers, star N=4 {acc(run_star, 4, False):.2f} = majority of 3")

print("\n5. tokens per correct answer")
for name, run in (("star", run_star), ("graph", run_graph)):

        a = acc(run, n, True); tok = run(make_agents(n, True, 0), "RIGHT", random.Random(0)).tokens

Reading this output

The simulator's "monoculture" is not one. Its homogeneous agents draw their errors independently and only share the wrong label. When each agent copied one shared draw with probability 0.5, star with 7 agents fell from 0.87 to 0.79; at 0.9 it fell to 0.74, about one agent's accuracy. The "heterogeneous" gain, 0.82 to 0.91 for star with 5, comes only from wrong answers splitting across three labels.

Ties decide many results. In the tree with 5 agents, the root's vote between two subtree answers was tied in 49% of trials, and each subtree is a vote of two. majority() gives a tie to whichever answer came first.

The chain does not improve with length. Accuracy stayed between 0.70 and 0.73 from 3 to 15 agents, because a later agent replaces the current answer on a coin flip whether its own answer is right or wrong.

The graph's second round changes nothing. In 2,000 of 2,000 trials the round-1 majority survived, since agents only move toward it. Graph with 3 agents (0.81) equals star with 4 agents (0.81): both are a majority of 3 votes.

Cost. Graph with 7 agents used 5,729 tokens per correct answer, against 2,884 for star with 7, for one point of accuracy.

# Twenty two-digit multiplications. Ensemble A: three samples of one prompt (self-consistency). Ensemble B:
# three different phrasings of the question (prompt heterogeneity). Then one round of graph debate for B:
# each agent sees the other two answers and gives a final one. About 2 minutes on CPU.
import os, re, random, warnings
from collections import Counter
os.environ["HF_HUB_OFFLINE"] = "1"
warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")
import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM, logging as hf_logging
hf_logging.set_verbosity_error(); hf_logging.disable_progress_bar()
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct")
llm = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct").eval()

def ask(prompt, seed):
    ids = tok.apply_chat_template([{"role": "user", "content": prompt + "\nReply with only the number."}],
                                  add_generation_prompt=True, return_tensors="pt", return_dict=True)["input_ids"]
    torch.manual_seed(seed)
    with torch.no_grad():
        out = llm.generate(ids, max_new_tokens=10, do_sample=True, temperature=0.8, top_k=0, top_p=1.0)
    m = re.search(r"\d[\d,]*", tok.decode(out[0, ids.shape[1]:], skip_special_tokens=True))
    return int(m.group().replace(",", "")) if m else None

rng = random.Random(5)
QS = [(rng.randint(12, 49), rng.randint(12, 49)) for _ in range(20)]
PHRASE = [lambda a, b: f"What is {a} times {b}?",
          lambda a, b: f"Compute {b} x {a}.",
          lambda a, b: f"A box holds {a} rows of {b} eggs. How many eggs are in the box?"]
A, B, D = [], [], []
for q, (a, b) in enumerate(QS):
    A.append([ask(PHRASE[0](a, b), seed=7 * q + i) for i in range(3)])
    B.append([ask(PHRASE[i](a, b), seed=7 * q + i) for i in range(3)])
    D.append([ask(f"{PHRASE[i](a, b)}\nTwo other agents answered {B[-1][(i + 1) % 3]} and {B[-1][(i + 2) % 3]}. "
                  f"Your first answer was {B[-1][i]}. Give your final answer.", seed=7 * q + i + 100) for i in range(3)])
truth = [a * b for a, b in QS]

def report(name, E):
    single = sum(x == t for e, t in zip(E, truth) for x in e) / (3 * len(QS))
    vote = sum(Counter(e).most_common(1)[0][0] == t for e, t in zip(E, truth))
    both = same = 0
    for e, t in zip(E, truth):
        w = [x for x in e if x != t]
        for i in range(len(w)):
            for j in range(i + 1, len(w)):
                both += 1; same += w[i] == w[j]
    print(f"   {name:34s} single {single:.2f}  vote {vote:2d}/20  same wrong answer {same}/{both} wrong pairs")
print("accuracy of a single answer, of a 3-way vote, and how often two wrong answers coincide:")

report("B: three phrasings", B)

moved = sum(d[i] != b[i] for b, d in zip(B, D) for i in range(3))

Reading this output

Same-prompt samples make the same mistakes. With one prompt sampled three times, two wrong answers were the same number in 9 of 11 pairs. With three phrasings, only 2 of 9. That is the decorrelation heterogeneity promises.

But the vote did not improve. Ensemble A voted 15 of 20 right; ensemble B voted 14. The egg-box phrasing was much weaker (10 of 20, against 14 and 16), and a weak voter can cancel the benefit of independent errors. Heterogeneity helps when the variants are about equally good.

One debate round was destructive. 47 of 60 answers changed: 1 wrong answer was fixed and 30 right answers were broken, and the vote fell from 14 to 6. On the 9 questions where all three agents had agreed on the right answer, 22 of 27 answers changed anyway. The 0.5B model handles the longer prompt with peers' answers worse than the plain question, a failure of the debater, not of the topology, and a reason to test debate on your model before using it.

Build it

code/main.py implements:

  • run_star(agents, hub, question) — hub polls each worker, aggregates.
  • run_chain(agents, question) — sequential refinement.
  • run_tree(root, children, question) — hierarchical with depth-2 aggregation.
  • run_graph(agents, question, rounds) — all-to-all debate, bounded rounds.
  • A scripted heterogeneity dial: each agent has an error_bias indicating its systematic wrongness.
  • A measurement harness that runs each topology at N=3, 5, 7 and reports (accuracy, total_tokens, wallclock_simulated).

Part 1 of 4 · SimAgent, RunResult, majority, run_star, run_chain

"""Voting and debate topology harness, stdlib only.

Runs star / chain / tree / graph topologies under a scripted task. Each
agent has a base-accuracy probability and an error_bias direction (which
wrong answer it drifts to on miss). We simulate N agents, rounds of
refinement, and measure (accuracy, tokens, simulated latency).
"""
from __future__ import annotations

import random
from dataclasses import dataclass, field


@dataclass
class SimAgent:
    name: str
    base_accuracy: float
    error_bias: str
    tokens_per_call: int = 400

    def answer(self, correct: str, rng: random.Random) -> str:
        return correct if rng.random() < self.base_accuracy else self.error_bias


@dataclass
class RunResult:
    topology: str
    n: int
    final_answer: str
    correct: str
    tokens: int
    steps: int

    def accuracy(self) -> int:
        return 1 if self.final_answer == self.correct else 0


def majority(items: list[str]) -> str:
    counts: dict[str, int] = {}
    for it in items:
        counts[it] = counts.get(it, 0) + 1
    return max(counts, key=counts.get)


def run_star(agents: list[SimAgent], correct: str, rng: random.Random) -> RunResult:
    hub = agents[0]
    workers = agents[1:]
    answers = [w.answer(correct, rng) for w in workers]
    tokens = sum(w.tokens_per_call for w in workers) + hub.tokens_per_call
    final = majority(answers) if answers else hub.answer(correct, rng)
    return RunResult("star", len(agents), final, correct, tokens, steps=2)


def run_chain(agents: list[SimAgent], correct: str, rng: random.Random) -> RunResult:
    current = agents[0].answer(correct, rng)
    tokens = agents[0].tokens_per_call
    for a in agents[1:]:
        proposal = a.answer(correct, rng)
        current = proposal if proposal != current and rng.random() < a.base_accuracy else current
        tokens += a.tokens_per_call
    return RunResult("chain", len(agents), current, correct, tokens, steps=len(agents))

def run_tree(agents: list[SimAgent], correct: str, rng: random.Random) -> RunResult:
    root = agents[0]
    leaves = agents[1:]
    if len(leaves) <= 1:
        return run_star(agents, correct, rng)
    mid = len(leaves) // 2
    left_answers = [a.answer(correct, rng) for a in leaves[:mid]]
    right_answers = [a.answer(correct, rng) for a in leaves[mid:]]
    tokens = sum(a.tokens_per_call for a in leaves) + root.tokens_per_call
    left_consensus = majority(left_answers)
    right_consensus = majority(right_answers)
    final = majority([left_consensus, right_consensus])
    return RunResult("tree", len(agents), final, correct, tokens, steps=3)


def run_graph(agents: list[SimAgent], correct: str, rng: random.Random, rounds: int = 2) -> RunResult:
    # Every agent proposes, then every agent sees all proposals and may update
    # (scaled down accuracy if they drift toward consensus).
    positions = [a.answer(correct, rng) for a in agents]
    tokens = sum(a.tokens_per_call for a in agents)
    for _ in range(rounds - 1):
        majority_now = majority(positions)
        new_positions = []
        for pos, ag in zip(positions, agents):
            if pos != majority_now and rng.random() < 0.4:
                new_positions.append(majority_now)
            else:
                new_positions.append(pos)
            tokens += ag.tokens_per_call
        positions = new_positions
    return RunResult("graph", len(agents), majority(positions), correct, tokens, steps=rounds * 2)


def make_agents(n: int, heterogeneous: bool, seed: int) -> list[SimAgent]:
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    if heterogeneous:
        biases = ["WRONG-A", "WRONG-B", "WRONG-C"]
        accuracies = [0.72, 0.70, 0.74, 0.71, 0.73, 0.70, 0.72]
    else:
        biases = ["WRONG-A"]
        accuracies = [0.72] * 7
    return [
        SimAgent(f"agent-{i}", accuracies[i % len(accuracies)], biases[i % len(biases)])
        for i in range(n)
    ]


def bench(correct: str, trials: int, heterogeneous: bool) -> None:
    tag = "HETEROGENEOUS" if heterogeneous else "HOMOGENEOUS (monoculture)"
    print("\n" + "=" * 72)
    print(f"BENCHMARK: {tag}")
    print("=" * 72)
    print(f"{'topology':10s} {'N':>3s} {'acc':>8s} {'avg_tokens':>12s} {'steps':>6s}")
    for topology in ("star", "chain", "tree", "graph"):
        for n in (3, 5, 7):
            acc_sum = 0
            tok_sum = 0
            step_sum = 0
            for t in range(trials):
                agents = make_agents(n, heterogeneous, seed=t)
                rng = random.Random(t * 31 + 7)
                if topology == "star":
                    r = run_star(agents, correct, rng)
                elif topology == "chain":
                    r = run_chain(agents, correct, rng)
                elif topology == "tree":
                    r = run_tree(agents, correct, rng)
                else:
                    r = run_graph(agents, correct, rng)
                acc_sum += r.accuracy()
                tok_sum += r.tokens
                step_sum += r.steps
            print(f"{topology:10s} {n:>3d} {acc_sum/trials:>8.2f} {tok_sum//trials:>12d} {step_sum//trials:>6d}")

Break it on purpose

Your team moves a support-ticket classifier from one agent to five copies of the same model in a fully connected debate with three rounds. Accuracy on the test set drops two points, cost rises sixfold, and in the logs most agents end every debate on the first agent's label. Using this lesson, what went wrong, and what should the team try instead? (Solution B1.)

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Self-consistency is the baseline to beat. Sampling one model several times and voting is cheap and hard to beat; Smit et al. found debate strategies do not reliably outperform it.
  • Vary what errs. Different model families, prompts or tools lower error correlation; here three phrasings cut coinciding wrong answers from 9 of 11 pairs to 2 of 9.
  • Bounded debate with a judge. Frameworks such as AutoGen and LangGraph make debate graphs easy to build; keep rounds to one or two and let a separate judge or verifier decide.
  • On your laptop: with a small model, vote over samples and skip the debate round; its long context degraded answers here even when all agents agreed.

Sources: Wang et al., Self-Consistency (arXiv:2203.11171), MultiAgentBench (arXiv:2503.01935), Smit et al., Should we be going MAD? (arXiv:2311.17371), AgentVerse (ICLR 2024), Sibyl (arXiv:2407.10718).

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Voting helps only as much as voters' errors are independent; measure how often wrong answers coincide.
  • Varying prompts or models lowers correlation, but a weaker variant can cancel the gain.
  • Compare topologies at the same number of votes and tokens; an extra voter can look like a better topology.
  • Debate rounds pull agents toward the majority; with small models they can destroy right answers.
  • Report tokens per correct answer alongside accuracy.

MACheat sheet · Multi-Agent Systems

Ship it

For any ensemble:

  • Start with self-consistency at N=5 using one strong base model. It is the cheap baseline.
  • Upgrade to heterogeneous voting at N=3 if accuracy matters. Measure the delta.
  • Only upgrade to debate topology if the task has structure (research, multi-step) and bounded rounds are feasible.
  • Always log the minority cluster. When a minority is persistently right, you have a diversity signal.
  • Benchmark wall-clock and tokens alongside accuracy. "Better accuracy at 10x cost" is a business decision.

Given a task description, recommend a multi-agent topology and sizing.

Produce:

1. **Task fingerprint.** Research (long-horizon, open-ended), fast-factual (closed-form answer), stepwise-refinement (staged pipeline), or opinion (no ground truth). Pick one; if it spans two, pick the dominant shape.
2. **Topology.** Star, chain, tree, or graph. Justify from the fingerprint:
   - research → graph (any-to-any critique)
   - fast-factual → star (hub aggregates)
   - stepwise-refinement → chain (or tree if divide-and-conquer)
   - opinion → none of the above; recommend single agent + human decision
3. **N of agents.** 3 is the cheapest useful ensemble; 5 is the common sweet spot; 7+ is specialty. Above 5 on graph topology, warn about coordination tax.
4. **Heterogeneity profile.** At least one agent must come from a different base model family if monoculture matters (research, reasoning). Prefer 3 different base models at N=5.
5. **Round bound.** 1 round = vote. 2 rounds = one refinement. 3 rounds = maximum before conformity dominates. Never unbounded.
6. **Aggregation.** Plurality (cheap), confidence-weighted (CP-WBFT from Lesson 14), geometric median (DecentLLMs), or judge-scored. Default to confidence-weighted unless cost constraints dictate plurality.
7. **Escalation.** Below-threshold consensus → escalate where? Human, another ensemble with different base models, or abstention?

Hard rejects:

- Any recommendation of 10+ agents on graph topology. Coordination tax dominates; measure first.
- Star topology for open research questions. Star loses the benefit of any-to-any critique.
- Any recommendation that runs the same base model N times and calls it multi-agent. That is self-consistency in disguise; label it correctly.
- Unbounded rounds. Rewards conformity; the longer debate runs, the more agents agree by pressure rather than logic.

Refusal rules:

- If the task has no ground truth (opinion, synthesis, creative), state that voting is advisory. Recommend single agent + human decision.
- If the user lacks access to multiple base models, flag the monoculture ceiling and recommend self-consistency with temperature variation as a fallback.
- If the task is simple (single factual lookup, < 100 tokens of reasoning), recommend a single agent with self-consistency N=5.

Output: a one-page brief. Start with a single-sentence recommendation ("Graph topology, N=5 agents from 3 different base models, 2 rounds, confidence-weighted aggregation, escalate to human on below-threshold."), then the seven sections above. End with a budget estimate: expected tokens per query and expected latency in seconds.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Never

Never. An agent only switches to the current majority, so every switch adds a vote to the majority and takes one from a minority. The majority can only grow. In the run, round 2 kept the round-1 majority in 2,000 of 2,000 trials. The second round costs a full set of calls and cannot change the result.

SolutionB1 · Copies, rounds and conformity

What went wrong. Five copies of one model share most of their errors, so the vote adds little. Unbounded-looking debate with three rounds pulls every agent toward whoever spoke first, the conformity effect, so the final label is mostly the first agent's. The team paid for 15 calls to get roughly one agent's answer, and the drift sometimes overturned right answers.

What to try instead: - Start with self-consistency: sample the model five times without debate and vote; measure the gain against one agent. - Lower correlation: vary prompts or use a second model family, and check how often wrong labels coincide. - If you keep debate, bound it: one round, answers shown without names or order effects, and a separate judge. - Report tokens per correct label next to accuracy, so a sixfold cost increase is visible.

Interview questions

Questions from real AI engineering interviews that this lesson prepares you for. Answer out loud first, then open the worked answer.

Weigh a trade-offA team added an ensemble to a question-answering task: five agents on the same model debate until they agree, then answer. Accuracy fell below the single agent. They want to add more agents. What went wrong, and what would you change first?

Level 2 of 4 · about 10 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Whether debate helps depends on structure, not on the count: who talks to whom, how many rounds, whether the agents are different, and whether anyone argues against. Five copies of one model share their errors, so the vote is correlated, and debate until agreement rewards the most confident agent through conformity. Change the composition before the count: start from self-consistency with one strong model, then swap one agent for a different base model, bound the rounds at two or three, and add a judge that does not vote.

The baseline they skipped.

Self-consistency samples one model several times at a temperature above zero and takes the majority over the reasoning paths. On a grade-school math set it gave large gains with 40 samples over one greedy decode. Its limit is that one model's errors are correlated by construction. A systematic bias is in every sample. That is also the team's ensemble with extra coordination.

Heterogeneity is the knob.

Replacing one of the agents with a different base model gives a bigger gain than adding one more copy. Each independent source of error is worth more than another correlated sample. In the limit, three different models beat five copies of one on tasks with a clean ground truth. The literature's name for the design that leans on this is adversarial heterogeneous debate: different models, and one of them argues against.

Rounds and conformity.

Debate can also degrade accuracy. Agents adjust their stance to match a critic even when the critic is wrong, and debate until agreement rewards whoever pushes hardest. Bound the rounds, two to three is typical, and give the final call to a separate judge that scores the consensus and does not vote. A survey of debate strategies found that variants structurally like self-consistency often underperform it at the same budget, and that debate helps most when agents are heterogeneous and the structure is adversarial.

Topology and the tax.

Topology Best for
Graph, any to any Research, where agents critique each other
Star, one hub Fast factual consolidation
Chain Stepwise refinement

On a graph the coordination tax appears past about 4 agents: wall-clock and tokens grow faster than quality, because every window fills with every peer's output. The ceiling is empirical and tied to current context capacity, not fundamental.

A sequence that measures each step.

  1. Self-consistency at five samples on one strong model. This is the cheap baseline.
  2. Heterogeneous voting at three agents if accuracy matters. Measure the delta.
  3. Debate only when the task has structure and bounded rounds are feasible.
  4. Log the minority cluster every time. A minority that is persistently right is a diversity signal.
  5. Report tokens and wall-clock next to accuracy, because better accuracy at ten times the cost is a business decision.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Add more agents until the vote stabilizes." A stable wrong vote is still wrong, and past a few agents the cost grows faster than the quality; MultiAgentBench's overall score fell as agents were added beyond three.
  • "Let them debate until they agree, because agreement means confidence." Unbounded debate rewards conformity, and the agreed answer is the loudest one, not the right one.
  • "Use the same model at different temperatures for diversity." That is self-consistency, and the systematic bias of the model survives every temperature.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why does adding a sixth copy of the same model not help? Expected: it adds one more correlated sample, so the shared bias gets one more vote.
  • (level 2) What does the judge do that the voters cannot? Expected: it scores the final consensus without having taken a position, so conformity pressure does not move it.
  • (level 3) When is plain self-consistency the right final answer? Expected: a simple task with a clean ground truth, where five samples of one strong model are as accurate as any ensemble and far cheaper.
  • (level 3) The task is opinion-shaped. What happens to the vote? Expected: agents converge on the most confident-looking answer rather than the most correct one, so a vote is an opinion poll and should be labeled as one.
