Why this came about

Teams that shipped agents learned the same lessons repeatedly. Design reviews and practitioner playbooks collect those lessons so new systems avoid known failures.

In plain words first
Reviewing a design like a practitioner

A design review asks the questions that production will ask anyway: what happens when this fails, who can see this data, what does it cost at ten times the volume, how will we know it works, and how do we roll back. The practitioner playbook collects the lessons teams learned the hard way, so you can check a design against them before building.

Review a design like an architecture board

The same checks catch most of the gaps that later become incidents. Encode them, and run them against any design, including your own:

DESIGN = {
    "name": "ShopKart support assistant v2",
    "success_metrics": {"resolution_rate": 0.6, "p95_first_token_ms": 2000},
    "eval": {"offline_cases": 300, "online_judge_sample": 0.05},
    "model_access": {"gateway": True, "fallback_provider": True, "pinned_versions": True},
    "write_tools": {"cancel_order": {"approval": False, "idempotency_key": True},
                    "refund": {"approval": "above INR 2000", "idempotency_key": True}},
    "tenancy": {"isolation": "tenant_id filter in every query and cache key"},
    "data_residency": "ap-south-1 only",
    "observability": {"tracing": True, "cost_per_outcome": True},
    "budgets": {"max_steps": 8, "max_cost_usd_per_conversation": 0.25},
    "untrusted_inputs": ["customer messages", "product reviews"],
    "egress_controls": False,
}

def review(d: dict) -> list[str]:
    findings = []
    if not d.get("success_metrics"):
        findings.append("BLOCKER: no success metrics")
    if d.get("eval", {}).get("offline_cases", 0) < 100:
        findings.append("BLOCKER: offline eval set too small to gate releases")
    m = d.get("model_access", {})
    if not m.get("gateway"):
        findings.append("HIGH: model calls bypass a gateway (keys, budgets, logging)")
    if not m.get("fallback_provider"):
        findings.append("MEDIUM: single model provider; no failover")
    for tool, cfg in d.get("write_tools", {}).items():
        if not cfg.get("idempotency_key"):
            findings.append(f"HIGH: {tool} has no idempotency key; retries can repeat it")
        if not cfg.get("approval"):
            findings.append(f"MEDIUM: {tool} runs without approval; confirm it is reversible")
    if d.get("untrusted_inputs") and not d.get("egress_controls"):
        findings.append("HIGH: untrusted inputs with no egress controls (link and image allowlists)")
    if "max_steps" not in d.get("budgets", {}):
        findings.append("HIGH: no per-run step budget")
    if not d.get("data_residency"):
        findings.append("MEDIUM: data residency not decided")
    return findings

for f in review(DESIGN):
    print(f)
What you should see
MEDIUM: cancel_order runs without approval; confirm it is reversible
HIGH: untrusted inputs with no egress controls (link and image allowlists)

The design is strong on the basics (metrics, evals, a gateway with fallback, idempotent writes, budgets, residency) and has one real gap: it reads untrusted text and renders answers without restricting links and images, the exfiltration path from Lesson 15.24. The cancellation finding is a question rather than a defect: cancelling an unshipped order is reversible, so no approval may be the right call, and the review makes someone say so.

In production
Design mistakes that look fine on the whiteboard
  • Boxes without numbers: an architecture that never states traffic, tokens and cost cannot be checked; a quota or budget problem appears in production instead.
  • Multi-agent by default: impressive diagrams with five agents for a problem a router and one agent would solve, at several times the cost and with more failure points.
  • Evaluation drawn as a box called "evals": say what is measured, on what data, with which threshold, and what it blocks.
  • Security as a perimeter: firewalls around the system while untrusted text flows straight into an agent with credentials. Draw the data flow and check the trifecta.
  • The happy path only: no answer to "what happens when the model provider is down", "when retrieval finds nothing", or "when the user changes their mind halfway through a refund".

Presenting a design

In a 45-minute design session: spend the first ten minutes on clarifying, success metrics and numbers before drawing anything; draw the reference architecture adapted to the problem; walk one request through it end to end; then invite the others into the trade-offs ("I would start with a router plus one agent; if routing accuracy turned out low, I would..."). Leave five minutes for failure modes and what you would build next. Silence while you think is fine; silence while you draw is not, so narrate.

To leadership or a client (one slide and five minutes): the problem in business terms and its current cost; the architecture as five or six boxes, not thirty; the evidence (pilot or eval results, and what the error cases are); the cost per outcome next to today's cost; the risks and their controls; the decision you need from them. Keep the detailed architecture in the appendix for the technical reviewers.

Practice

DesignDesign an AI assistant for relationship managers at a bank that drafts client meeting briefs from CRM, portfolio and market data.

Clarify: 2,000 relationship managers, briefs before each meeting, read-only, no advice sent to clients without review; regulated data. Success: briefs rated usable without major edits 80% of the time, every number traceable to a source, generated within a minute, no data leaving the bank's cloud account. Estimate: a few thousand briefs a day, batchable the night before. Shape: a workflow, not an agent: fetch CRM notes, portfolio positions and relevant market news in parallel, summarize each with citations, assemble a brief with a fixed template, check every number against its source. Safety: the market-news step reads untrusted text, so it has no access to client data and returns only structured summaries (dual-LLM pattern); briefs are drafts for the manager. Operate: managers' edits are the main quality signal; evaluate numeric accuracy automatically; models inside the bank's tenancy. Trade-off: a workflow is less flexible than an agent but auditable, which a regulator will ask about.

DesignDesign an agent that reconciles payments against invoices for a mid-size company every night.

Clarify: about 30,000 transactions a night from bank feeds and the ERP; matches must be exact; mismatches go to accountants by morning. Shape: deterministic matching rules first (amount, reference, date window) handle most items; an agent handles the remainder by reading remittance emails and partial references, proposing matches with evidence, never posting them. Durable, resumable batch with a progress log so a failure at 3 a.m. resumes rather than restarts; idempotent writes of proposed matches. Success: share auto-matched by rules, precision of agent proposals accepted by accountants, completion before 7 a.m. Safety: remittance emails are untrusted; the agent has read-only access and writes only proposals. Trade-off: rules are cheaper and exact; the agent is for the long tail, measured by acceptance rate.

DesignDesign an internal platform so 30 product teams can ship agents safely.

A paved road rather than a shared agent: an LLM gateway with virtual keys, budgets and model allowlists; an MCP gateway with an approved server registry and per-agent tool allowlists; a deployment template with durable execution, tracing and guardrail defaults; an evaluation service with CI integration and release gates; a prompt registry; and an AI system register fed automatically from gateway traffic. Tenancy and cost attribution per team at the gateway. Success is time for a new team to ship a governed agent and the share of model traffic through the gateway. Trade-off: the platform becomes a critical dependency, so it needs its own SLOs and an emergency bypass.

DesignYour design must serve customers in India and the EU with data staying in each region. What changes?

Two regional stacks with the same code: models, indexes, logs, traces and memory in each region; a router at the edge by customer residency; no cross-region calls on the request path. Check model and feature availability in both regions first, because a model missing in one region forces a different choice there, and keep the eval suite per region. Shared components such as prompt registries and dashboards hold no personal data. Document the data flows for both DPDP and GDPR reviews, and test residency mechanically (egress rules, region checks in CI).

Lab 41A · Design, estimate, review
  1. Pick a system from your work or a client engagement. Write the nine-step design on two pages, with the numbers from estimate using your real volumes and prices.
  2. Draw it as the reference architecture with the layers you do not need crossed out, and walk one request through it.

From the field: shipping and running AI products

  • Plan around experiments, not features (Hamel Husain): time-boxed experiments with clear success criteria give stakeholders decision points without promising outcomes nobody can guarantee.
  • Let domain experts edit prompts and label data directly; the bottleneck is usually domain judgment, not code.
  • Bootstrap with synthetic data grounded in real constraints, then replace it with real traffic as it arrives.
  • Instrument from day one: traces, cost per task, and online evaluation (Lessons 14.120 to 14.117).
  • Measure business outcomes (resolution rate, handle time, revenue, errors avoided), not tokens or demo quality; the cost per resolved task is the number executives understand.
  • Keep a human in the loop where the cost of an error is high, and move the boundary only as evals and monitoring earn trust.

Anti-patterns to catch in design review

Anti-pattern Better
An agent where a workflow would do Fixed steps for known paths; an agent only for open-ended parts
One agent with 60 tools Fewer, task-shaped tools; tool search or skills; small focused agents
Rules piled into the system prompt Enforce rules in code at the point of action (hooks, steering, policy engineslater · 14.131; Lessons 14.97 and 14.131)
Removing tools mid-conversation Mask or constrain them; keep the prefix stable for caching
Raw API JSON returned to the model Concise, readable tool results with next-step hints in errors
Multi-agent by default One agent with good context management until parallel breadth is proven to pay
"It works on my five examples" Error analysis on real traces, then capability and regression evals with pass^k
A 1-to-10 LLM judge nobody checked Binary judgments with critiques, calibrated against an expert
Private data, untrusted input and external actions in one session Rule of Two: approvals or separation
No cost ceiling Per-run token and step limits, budgets and alerts
A framework or vendor choice made before the problem is understood Prototype the loop by hand, then choose on evidence (Lesson 14.70)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Review checklists

"What is the worst action this agent can take, and what bounds it?" Every design review should ask about blast radius.

SolutionB1 · Review early

Changes are expensive after launch, and risks are already live. Review at design time with a short checklist, and again before launch.

SolutionD1 · Turn findings into defaults

Encode common findings into platform defaults, templates and automated checks, so teams start correct.

SolutionM1 · A review checklist

Task and success metric; evals; worst action and its bounds; permissions; untrusted inputs; failure modes; cost per task; observability; data handling; ownership and on-call.

Check your understanding

Explain the mechanismWhat should a design review of an AI agent cover?

Worked answer. Purpose and success metrics, structure (workflow or agent), evaluation plan, safety and security (permissions, injection, data), reliability and failure modes, cost and scale, observability, governance and ownership. The goal is to find expensive mistakes early.

A plausible wrong answer. "Code quality." Design reviews are about system risks.

Follow-ups. Who should attend?

Debug a failureA reviewed design failed in production in a way nobody predicted. What do you do with the review process?

Worked answer. Add the failure mode to the checklist, share the postmortem, and check whether the review had the right people and information.

A plausible wrong answer. "Reviews do not work." They improve with feedback.

Follow-ups. How would you measure review effectiveness?

Defend a designAre formal design reviews worth it for small AI features?

Worked answer. A lightweight version is: a short checklist and a twenty-minute review catch the costly mistakes (unbounded actions, no evals, data leaks). Scale the depth with the risk tier.

A plausible wrong answer. "Only for big projects." Small features can still move money or leak data.

Follow-ups. What is the minimum review?
