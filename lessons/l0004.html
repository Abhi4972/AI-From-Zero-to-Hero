Why this came about

Every few years someone announces that AI has changed everything, and every few years a technology that was promised turns out to be harder than expected. Knowing the history helps you tell the two apart. It also explains the shape of this course: each chapter of the foundations corresponds to an era, and each era ended because of a limit the next one solved.

In plain words first
Seventy years in one paragraph

For thirty years people tried to make computers intelligent by writing rules by hand. That worked for chess and narrow puzzles but not for messy things like recognizing a face or understanding a sentence, because nobody can write down all the rules. So researchers switched to learning from examples (machine learninglater · 2.22). Learning got dramatically better once computers were fast enough and data plentiful enough to train deep neural networks (from 2012). A new design for those networks, the transformerlater · 7.19 (2017), could be trained on almost all the text on the internet, producing large language models. Teaching those models to follow instructions (2022) produced assistants; giving them tools and a loop (2023 onward) produced agents.

Era 1 · Rules and logic (1950s to 1970s)

In 1950 Alan Turing asked whether machines could think, and proposed judging them by whether their conversation could be told apart from a person's: the imitation game, now called the Turing test. In 1956 a summer workshop at Dartmouth College coined the phrase artificial intelligence. Early programs proved logic theorems, played checkers and solved algebra problems by search: trying many possibilities quickly. In 1958 Frank Rosenblatt built the perceptron, a machine that learned to recognize simple patterns: the first artificial neuron you will build in Lesson 3.15. In 1966 ELIZA imitated a therapist with simple pattern-matching rules, and people confided in it anyway: an early lesson that sounding intelligent and being intelligent are different things.

The limit. Real-world problems have too many exceptions to write down, and single-layer perceptrons provably could not learn some simple patterns. Funding collapsed in the mid-1970s: the first AI winter.

Era 2 · Expert systems (1980s)

Companies captured experts' knowledge as thousands of if-then rules ("if the infection is bacterial and the patient is allergic to penicillin, then..."). Some worked well in narrow domains. In 1986 a widely read paper by Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams showed how to train neural networks with several layers using backpropagationlater · 3.16, the algorithm you will build by hand in Lesson 3.16. But computers were too slow and datasets too small for it to shine.

The limit. Rule systems were expensive to build, brittle when the world changed, and could not learn. The market collapsed in the late 1980s: the second AI winter.

Era 3 · Statistical machine learning (mid-1990s to 2012)

The field moved from rules to statistics: models that learn patterns from data and report how confident they are. Spam filters, search ranking, product recommendations, credit scoring and fraud detection all came from this era, using methods such as logistic regression, decision trees and support vector machines (the Machine learning from zero section). In 1997 IBM's Deep Blue beat the world chess champion (mostly by search), and the LSTMlater · 5.35, a neural network that could remember across a sequence, was published (Lesson 5.35). The web made data plentiful; graphics cards (GPUslater · 3.20), built for video games, turned out to be very good at the arithmetic neural networks need.

The limit. These models needed people to hand-design the features they learned from ("count the exclamation marks", "is the sender in the address book"). For images, sound and language, nobody could design good enough features.

Era 4 · Deep learning (2012 to 2017)

In 2012 a neural network called AlexNet, trained on GPUs, won the ImageNet image-recognition competition by a wide margin. Deep networks learn their own features from raw data, and they keep improving with more data and computing power. Within a few years they led in speech recognition, translation and image recognition. For language, word2veclater · 5.33 (2013) learned a vector of numbers for every word, so that similar words get similar vectors (Lesson 5.33); sequence-to-sequence models (2014) translated whole sentences; and attention (2014) let a model look back at the most relevant words of the input (Lesson 5.36). In 2016 DeepMind's AlphaGo beat a world champion at Go.

The limit. Language models of this era read text one word at a time, in order. That was slow to train and they lost track of long passages.

Era 5 · Transformers, LLMs and agents (2017 to now)

In 2017 Google researchers published Attention Is All You Need, introducing the transformer: a network that uses attention everywhere and reads all the words of a passage at once (the Transformers, step by step section). Because it trains efficiently on parallel hardware, it could be scaled up enormously:

Why the jumps happened: three ingredients together

Progress in AI has come when three things improved at the same time:

Ingredient 1990s Today
Data Thousands to millions of examples Trillions of words of text, billions of images
Compute One processor, megaflops Clusters of tens of thousands of GPUs
Methods Hand-designed features Networks that learn their own features; transformers that use hardware efficiently

The researcher Richard Sutton summed up seventy years of experience in a 2019 essay called The Bitter Lesson: methods that make use of more computing power, such as learning and search, have repeatedly beaten methods that build in human knowledge by hand. It is a debated claim, but it explains why today's systems are trained, not programmed.

What history teaches someone building with AI

  • Hype runs ahead of reality. Both AI winters followed promises that were too large. When someone says a technology "will replace" a whole job next year, ask what it can reliably do today, measured how (the Production engineering and AI system design section teaches evaluation).
  • Sounding right is not being right. ELIZA fooled people in 1966; language models can be fluent and wrong today (Lesson 10.38). Always check.
  • Each limit created the next idea. Models without your data led to retrieval; models that cannot act led to tools and agents; agents with real permissions led to security and governance. Every chapter of this course is a step in that chain.
  • The fundamentals last. Products and model names change every few months; next-token prediction, attention, embeddingslater · 3.19, retrieval and evaluation have stayed central for years. That is why this course spends time on them.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Rules or learning

(a) is a hand-written rule: a person decided the rule. (b) is machine learning: the program found its own patterns from labelled examples. Lesson 2.22 builds both and compares them.

SolutionM1 · Placing a product

A typical example: an email assistant uses an LLM (era 5) to draft replies, a statistical classifier (era 3) to sort mail, and rules (era 1) for things that must never vary, such as "never send to external addresses without approval". Mixing eras is normal and often wise: rules where you need certainty, learning where rules cannot cope.

Recap
  • Five eras: rules, expert systems, statistical learning, deep learning, transformers and LLMs; each ended at a limit the next one addressed.
  • The big jumps came when data, computing power and methods improved together.
  • Assistants (2022) came from teaching language models to follow instructions; agents (2023 onward) from giving them tools and a loop.
  • Hype cycles are real: judge claims by what is measured today.
