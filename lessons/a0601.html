In plain words first
The idea in one line

Waveforms are the raw signal. Spectrograms are the representation. Mel features are the ML-friendly form. Every modern ASR and TTS pipeline walks this ladder, and the first rung is understanding sampling and Fourier.

The problem

In plain words first

Sound is air pressure changing over time. A microphone turns it into a voltage, and an analogue-to-digital converter measures that voltage thousands of times a second; each measurement is a sample, and the number per second is the sample rate. Everything a speech model sees is that list of numbers. Two facts decide whether it is usable. First, a sample rate can only describe frequencies up to half of itself; anything faster is not lost but disguised as a lower frequency (aliasing). Second, the useful structure of sound is easier to see as a mix of frequencies than as a wiggle in time, and the Fourier transform converts one view into the other. This lesson loads a real recording, builds the discrete Fourier transform by hand, checks it against the fast library version, and measures aliasing, spectral leakage and quantisation noise with numbers you can reproduce.

A microphone produces a pressure-vs-time signal. Your neural net consumes tensors. Between them sits a stack of conventions that, when violated, produce silent bugs: the model trains fine but the WER doubles, or TTS ships a hiss, or a voice cloning system memorizes the microphone instead of the speaker.

Every bug in speech systems traces back to one of three questions:

  1. What sample rate was the data recorded at, and what does the model expect?
  2. Is the signal aliased?
  3. Are you operating on raw samples or on a frequency representation?

Get these right and the rest of Phase 6 is tractable. Get them wrong and even Whisper large-v3 produces garbage.

The idea

Waveform. A one-dimensional array of floats in [-1.0, 1.0]. Indexed by sample number. To convert to seconds, divide by the sample rate: t = n / sr. A 10-second clip at 16 kHz is an array of 160,000 floats.

Sampling rate (sr). How many samples per second. Common rates in 2026:

Rate Use
8 kHz Telephony, legacy VoIP. Nyquist at 4 kHz removes most of the energy of fricatives such as /s/ and /f/. Use 16 kHz for ASR when you have it.
16 kHz ASR standard. Whisper, Parakeet, SeamlessM4T v2 all consume 16 kHz.
22.05 kHz TTS vocoder training for older models.
24 kHz Modern TTS (Kokoro, F5-TTS, xTTS v2).
44.1 kHz CD audio, music.
48 kHz Film, pro audio; full-band neural audio codecs (EnCodec has a 48 kHz model). Many TTS systems still work at 16–24 kHz.

Nyquist-Shannon. A sample rate of sr can unambiguously represent frequencies up to sr/2. The sr/2 boundary is the Nyquist frequency. Energy above Nyquist gets aliased — folded down into lower frequencies — and corrupts the signal. Always low-pass filter before downsampling.

Bit depth. 16-bit PCM (signed int16, range ±32,767) is the universal exchange format. 24-bit for music, 32-bit float for internal DSP. Libraries like soundfile read int16 but expose float32 arrays in [-1, 1].

Fourier Transform. Any finite signal is a sum of sinusoids at different frequencies. The Discrete Fourier Transform (DFT) computes, for N samples, N complex coefficients — one per frequency bin. bin k maps to frequency k · sr / N Hz. Magnitude is amplitude at that frequency, angle is phase.

FFT. Fast Fourier Transform: an O(N log N) algorithm for the DFT. The classic radix-2 version needs N to be a power of 2; library FFTs accept any N and are fastest when N has only small prime factors. Every audio library uses FFT under the hood. A 1024-sample real FFT at 16 kHz gives 513 bins from 0 to 8 kHz, 15.6 Hz apart.

Framing + window. We do not FFT an entire clip. We chop it into overlapping frames (typically 25 ms with 10 ms hop), multiply each frame by a window function (Hann, Hamming) to kill edge discontinuities, then FFT each frame. This is the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT). Lesson 6.2 picks up from here.

The same idea as a formula

Sampling. A continuous signal x(t) sampled every T=1/fs​ seconds gives x[n]=x(nT). The sampling theorem: if x(t) contains no frequency at or above fs​/2 (the Nyquist frequency), the samples determine it exactly, and it can be rebuilt by sinc interpolation, x(t)=∑n​x[n]sinc((t−nT)/T).

Aliasing. A tone at f and a tone at f+mfs​ (any integer m) give identical samples, because cos(2π(f+mfs​)nT)=cos(2πfnT+2πmn)=cos(2πfnT). Folding into the visible range, a tone at f shows up at

falias​=​f−fs​⋅round(f/fs​)​∈[0,fs​/2]

The discrete Fourier transform of N samples:

X[k]=n=0∑N−1​x[n]e−2πikn/N,k=0,…,N−1,fk​=Nkfs​​

For real x, X[N−k]=X[k]​, so only bins 0…N/2 carry information (the rfft). ∣X[k]∣ is the amplitude near fk​, argX[k] the phase. Parseval: ∑n​∣x[n]∣2=N1​∑k​∣X[k]∣2, energy is the same in both views.

Cost. Each of N outputs sums N terms: O(N2). The FFT splits the sum into even and odd samples, X[k]=E[k]+e−2πik/NO[k], and recurses: O(NlogN).

Leakage and windows. A DFT treats the N samples as one period of a repeating signal. A tone that does not complete a whole number of cycles in N samples has a jump at the seam, which spreads its energy over many bins. Multiplying by a window that tapers to zero, such as Hann, w[n]=21​(1−cosN−12πn​), removes the jump: wider main peak, far lower side lobes.

Quantisation. Rounding to b bits adds noise; for a full-scale sine the signal-to-noise ratio is

SNR≈6.02b+1.76 dB

Worked example
Bins, aliases and bits by hand

Bins. At fs​=16,000 Hz with N=1024: spacing 16000/1024=15.625 Hz, and the real FFT has 1024/2+1=513 bins from 0 to 8,000 Hz. A 440 Hz tone sits at bin 440/15.625=28.16: between bins 28 and 29, so it leaks without a window.

Aliases. 7 kHz sampled at 10 kHz: round(7/10)=1, ∣7000−10000∣=3000 Hz. 7 kHz sampled at 8 kHz: ∣7000−8000∣=1000 Hz, the value main.py checks in its last step. 12 kHz sampled at 16 kHz: ∣12000−16000∣=4000 Hz, right at Nyquist.

Bits. 16 bits: 6.02×16+1.76=98.1 dB. 8 bits: 49.9 dB, audible hiss. Each extra bit buys about 6 dB.

Cost. One second of 16 kHz audio as a single DFT: 160002=2.56×108 multiply-adds. An FFT needs about Nlog2​N≈16000×14=2.2×105, over 1,000 times fewer.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "A higher sample rate always means better recognition."
    ✓ The model needs the rate it was trained on. Feeding 44.1 kHz samples to a model that assumes 16 kHz lowers every frequency, and slows the speech, by a factor of 2.76.
  • ✗ "Downsampling means keeping every k-th sample."
    ✓ First remove everything above the new Nyquist frequency with a low-pass filter, or it folds into the result.
  • ✗ "The FFT only works on powers of two."
    ✓ That is one classic algorithm; library FFTs take any length.

Step 2 · synthesize a sine wave from first principles

import math

def sine(freq_hz, sr, seconds, amp=0.5):
    n = int(sr * seconds)
    return [amp * math.sin(2 * math.pi * freq_hz * i / sr) for i in range(n)]

A 440 Hz sine (concert A) at 16 kHz for 1 second is 16,000 floats. Write with wave.open(..., "wb") using 16-bit PCM encoding.

Step 3 · compute the DFT by hand

def dft(x):
    N = len(x)
    out = []
    for k in range(N):
        re = sum(x[n] * math.cos(-2 * math.pi * k * n / N) for n in range(N))
        im = sum(x[n] * math.sin(-2 * math.pi * k * n / N) for n in range(N))
        out.append((re, im))
    return out

O(N²) — fine for N=256 to confirm correctness, useless for real audio. Real code calls numpy.fft.rfft or torch.fft.rfft.

The hand DFT against the library FFT

main.py's dft (pure Python, O(N2)) against numpy.fft.fft on random signals: same numbers, very different cost.

import time
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for N in [64, 256, 1024]:
    x = rng.standard_normal(N)
    t0 = time.perf_counter(); slow = dft(list(x)); t_slow = time.perf_counter() - t0
    t0 = time.perf_counter(); fast = np.fft.fft(x); t_fast = time.perf_counter() - t0
    err = max(abs(complex(*s) - f) for s, f in zip(slow, fast))
    print(f"N={N:5d}  hand DFT {t_slow * 1000:8.1f} ms   numpy FFT {t_fast * 1000:6.3f} ms   max difference {err:.1e}")
x = rng.standard_normal(1000)                # not a power of two
print("FFT of N=1000 works too; difference from the hand DFT:",
      f"{max(abs(complex(*s) - f) for s, f in zip(dft(list(x)), np.fft.fft(x))):.1e}")
print("rfft of 1024 real samples returns", len(np.fft.rfft(np.zeros(1024))), "bins (0 Hz to sr/2 inclusive)")

The two agree to about 10−11, rounding error. The time does not: each fourfold increase in N multiplies the hand DFT's time by about 16 (1.6, 24 and 422 ms in this run), the N2 law, while the FFT stays under a millisecond. N=1000 is not a power of two and the library FFT handles it. The rfft line confirms the bin count from the worked example: 513 bins for 1,024 real samples.

Step 4 · find the dominant frequency

Magnitude peak index k_star maps to frequency k_star * sr / N. Running this on the 440 Hz sine should return a peak at bin 440 * N / sr.

Finding the peak is argmax over ∣X[k]∣, then f=kfs​/N; main.py's peak_freq does exactly that, and its output appears under Step 5. Peak-finding goes wrong when one tone's energy smears over the others, which is what windows are for.

Leakage: a quiet tone hidden by a loud one

A loud tone placed exactly halfway between two bins and a second tone 60 dB (1,000 times) quieter, 19 bins away. Spectrum with no window, and with a Hann window.

# Spectral leakage: a loud tone that falls between two bins, and a quiet tone 60 dB (1000 times) weaker.
sr8, N = 8000, 512
n = np.arange(N)
f_loud, f_quiet = 1007.8125, 1300.0             # 1007.8125 Hz sits exactly halfway between bins 64 and 65
x = np.sin(2 * np.pi * f_loud * n / sr8) + 0.001 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * f_quiet * n / sr8)
print(f"bin spacing {sr8 / N:.3f} Hz; loud tone at bin {f_loud * N / sr8:.2f}, quiet tone at bin {f_quiet * N / sr8:.2f}")
k = int(round(f_quiet * N / sr8))
for name, w in [("rectangular (no window)", np.ones(N)), ("Hann window", np.hanning(N))]:
    mag = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(x * w)); db = 20 * np.log10(mag / mag.max() + 1e-12)
    around = np.r_[db[k - 6:k - 3], db[k + 4:k + 7]].mean()      # level a few bins either side
    print(f"{name:24s} at 1300 Hz {db[k]:6.1f} dB, a few bins away {around:6.1f} dB "
          f"-> quiet tone {'stands out' if db[k] > around + 10 else 'buried in leakage'}")
What you should see
bin spacing 15.625 Hz; loud tone at bin 64.50, quiet tone at bin 83.20
rectangular (no window)  at 1300 Hz  -32.5 dB, a few bins away  -31.9 dB -> quiet tone buried in leakage
Hann window              at 1300 Hz  -58.6 dB, a few bins away  -83.4 dB -> quiet tone stands out
Reading this output

Without a window, the loud tone's leakage is still at about −32 dB nineteen bins away, so the quiet tone at −60 dB is invisible: the spectrum at 1,300 Hz is just leakage. With a Hann window the leakage a few bins away falls to −83 dB and the quiet tone stands out at −59 dB, its true level. This is why every STFT in the rest of this phase multiplies each frame by a window before the FFT.

ax[0].set_title("Sampled at 10 kHz, the 7 kHz and 3 kHz tones give the same samples")

# Downsampling a 0-8 kHz sweep from 16 kHz to 8 kHz: naive decimation vs a filtered resampler.
sr16 = 16000
tc = np.arange(sr16 * 2) / sr16
sweep = chirp(tc, f0=0, f1=8000, t1=2, method="linear").astype(np.float32)   # frequency rises 0 -> 8 kHz
naive = sweep[::2]
proper = resample_poly(sweep, up=1, down=2)                 # low-pass (anti-alias) filter, then keep every 2nd
upper = slice(int(0.625 * len(naive)), len(naive))         # last 0.75 s: the sweep is at 5-8 kHz, above the new Nyquist (4 kHz)
for name, y in [("naive [::2]", naive), ("resample_poly", proper)]:
    ratio = np.sqrt(np.mean(y[upper] ** 2) / np.mean(sweep[::2][: len(naive) // 2] ** 2))
    print(f"{name:14s} level while the sweep is above 4 kHz: {20 * np.log10(ratio + 1e-12):6.1f} dB")
f8, t8, S = __import__("scipy.signal", fromlist=["spectrogram"]).spectrogram(naive, fs=8000, nperseg=256)
ax[1].pcolormesh(t8, f8, 10 * np.log10(S + 1e-10), shading="auto", cmap="magma")
ax[1].set_xlabel("seconds"); ax[1].set_ylabel("Hz")
ax[1].set_title("Naive decimation of a 0-8 kHz sweep to 8 kHz: after 1 s the sweep folds back down")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

Every blue sample lies on both curves, so after sampling nothing can tell the two tones apart. The spectrogram shows the result on a sweep: up to 1 second it rises normally; once it passes the new Nyquist frequency of 4 kHz, it comes back down, a falling tone that was never in the input. Naive decimation keeps that folded energy at full level (0 dB); the filtering resampler removes it to −56 dB. In speech, the folded energy lands on top of real low-frequency content and cannot be removed afterwards.

How many bits?

A sine stored with 16, 12, 8 and 4 bits; the noise added by rounding, as a signal-to-noise ratio.

# Quantisation: how much noise does storing a sine with b bits add?
x = 0.99 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 440 * np.arange(48000) / 48000)
for bits in [16, 12, 8, 4]:
    levels = 2 ** (bits - 1) - 1
    q = np.round(x * levels) / levels
    snr = 10 * np.log10(np.mean(x ** 2) / np.mean((x - q) ** 2))
    print(f"{bits:2d} bits: measured SNR {snr:5.1f} dB   formula 6.02*b + 1.76 = {6.02 * bits + 1.76:5.1f} dB")

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • torchaudio has slimmed down. It entered a maintenance phase in 2025: audio decoding and encoding moved to TorchCodec (deprecated in torchaudio 2.8, removed in 2.9), while transforms such as Resample and MelSpectrogram stay. Hugging Face datasets also decodes audio through TorchCodec now; this lesson reads the raw FLAC bytes with soundfile instead.
  • Front ends are still classical. Whisper large-v3 still consumes 16 kHz audio as a log-mel spectrogram (128 mel bins, up from 80 in earlier versions). Neural audio codecs (Lesson 6.13) are the main alternative input for speech-language models.

Sources: torchaudio's future (GitHub issue 3902), Whisper large-v3 model card.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

The stack you will actually ship in 2026:

Decision rule: match sample rate before you match anything else. Whisper expects 16 kHz mono float32. Pass it 44.1 kHz stereo and you will get garbage that looks like a model bug.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • A waveform is samples at a sample rate; match the rate the model expects before anything else.
  • A sample rate fs​ represents frequencies below fs​/2; anything above folds down. Low-pass before downsampling.
  • The DFT gives one complex value per frequency bin, fk​=kfs​/N; the FFT computes it in O(NlogN).
  • Window each frame (Hann) before the FFT, or loud components hide quiet ones.
  • Each bit of depth adds about 6 dB of signal-to-noise ratio; 16-bit PCM gives about 98 dB.

Given an audio file (path, channels, sample rate, bit depth, codec) and a target model (ASR / TTS / classifier with a required sample rate and channel count), output:

1. Mismatches. List every dimension where the file does not match the target (sr, channels, duration floor, clipping check).
2. Resample plan. Source sr, target sr, resampling library (`torchaudio.transforms.Resample` or `librosa.resample`), anti-aliasing filter type.
3. Channel plan. Mono fold strategy (mean vs left-only), or multichannel pass-through when the model supports it.
4. Normalization. Peak vs RMS normalization, dBFS target, clipping guard.
5. Validation snippet. Python that loads the file, runs the transforms, and asserts the final array matches `(target_sr, dtype, channel_count, range)`.

Refuse to downsample without an anti-aliasing filter. Refuse to upsample beyond 2x without a reconstruction filter. Flag any input file with clipping peaks over ±0.999 or a DC offset above ±0.01.

Exercises

  1. Easy. Synthesize a 1-second mix of 220 Hz + 440 Hz + 880 Hz at 16 kHz. Run DFT. Confirm three peaks at the expected bins.
  2. Medium. Record a 3-second WAV of your voice at 48 kHz. Downsample to 16 kHz using torchaudio.transforms.Resample (with anti-aliasing), then to 16 kHz using naive decimation (every third sample). FFT both. Where does the aliasing appear?
  3. Hard. Build the STFT from scratch using only math and the DFT from Step 3. Frame size 400, hop 160, Hann window. Plot magnitudes with matplotlib.pyplot.imshow. This is the spectrogram of Lesson 02.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · 0 dB against −56 dB

Keeping every second sample leaves the upper half of the sweep at full strength (0 dB), folded down into 0–4 kHz as a falling tone. resample_poly low-pass filters before discarding samples, so the part above 4 kHz is reduced to −56 dB, inaudible under almost anything else.

SolutionB1 · The wrong rate is not an error message

Reading the 16 kHz clip and telling the model it is 8 kHz halves every frequency and doubles the duration: the 119 Hz voice becomes about 60 Hz and the speech slows down. Nothing crashes; the model just hears a different, slower, deeper speaker. Always check sr against the model's expected rate and resample with a filtering resampler.
