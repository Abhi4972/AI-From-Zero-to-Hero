In plain words first
The idea in one line

Batch pipelines process a file. Real-time pipelines process the next 20 milliseconds before the next 20 arrive. Every conversational AI, broadcast studio, and telephony bot lives and dies by this latency budget.

Terms you'll hear

The problem

In plain words first

Everything so far processed a finished recording. A live system gets audio 20 milliseconds at a time and must finish with each piece before the next arrives, forever, while a person waits for an answer. Three ideas carry it. A ring buffer holds the most recent audio in a fixed amount of memory. A voice activity detector decides, frame by frame, whether anyone is talking, so the expensive models only run when needed. And every model downstream works in streaming mode: it produces partial results from the audio seen so far, accepting some loss of accuracy for not knowing what comes next. The craft is a latency budget: how long each stage may take, and what you give up to meet it. This lesson measures two of those trade-offs on real speech: two voice activity detectors in rising noise, and a speech recogniser fed in chunks of different sizes, with and without look-ahead. Nothing is trained.

You want a voice assistant that feels alive. In conversation, the typical gap between one person stopping and the next starting is about 200 ms, across languages (Stivers et al., 2009). Delays well beyond that feel increasingly unnatural, and a second or more feels broken. A typical target budget for a full hear → understand → respond → speak loop:

Stage Budget
Mic → buffer 20 ms
VAD 10 ms
ASR (streaming) 150 ms
LLM (first token) 100 ms
TTS (first chunk) 100 ms
Render → speaker 20 ms
Total ~400 ms

Moshi (Kyutai, 2024) reports about 200 ms in practice, full-duplex. OpenAI reported GPT-4o answering spoken input in 320 ms on average (May 2024), against 2.8 s and 5.4 s for its earlier cascaded Voice Mode with GPT-3.5 and GPT-4. The 10× improvement came from three techniques: (1) streaming everywhere, (2) asynchronous pipelining with partial results, (3) interruptible generation.

The idea

Frame / chunk / window. Real-time audio flows as fixed-size blocks. Common choice: 20 ms (320 samples at 16 kHz). Everything downstream must keep up with this cadence.

Ring buffer. Fixed-size circular buffer. Producer thread writes new frames, consumer thread reads. Prevents allocations in the hot path. Size ≈ maximum-latency × sample-rate; a 2-second 16 kHz ring = 32,000 samples.

VAD (Voice Activity Detection). Gates downstream work when nobody is speaking. Silero VAD (v5, 2024) processes a 30 ms chunk in under 1 ms on one CPU thread. webrtcvad is the older alternative.

Streaming ASR. Models that emit partial transcripts as audio arrives. NVIDIA NeMo's cache-aware streaming FastConformer models are trained with limited look-ahead, so they stay accurate on chunks of a few hundred milliseconds; the run under Step 3 shows what happens to a model not trained that way. Whisper-Streaming (Macháček et al., 2023) re-runs Whisper on a growing buffer for near-streaming with a few seconds of latency.

Interruption. When the user speaks while the assistant is talking, you must (a) detect the barge-in, (b) stop the TTS, (c) discard the remaining LLM output. All within 100 ms, or the user perceives deaf assistant.

WebRTC Opus transport. Typically 20 ms frames at 48 kHz with adaptive bitrate (Opus supports 6 to 510 kbps). Standard for browser and mobile. LiveKit, Daily (with its open-source Pipecat framework) and Pion are common foundations for voice apps.

Jitter buffer. Network packets arrive out of order / late. The jitter buffer reorders and smooths; too small → audible gaps, too large → latency. Adaptive jitter buffers usually hold a few tens of milliseconds.

The same idea as a formula

Frames and buffers. A frame of Δ seconds at sample rate fs​ holds N=Δfs​ samples (20 ms at 16 kHz: 320). A ring buffer that can hold B seconds needs Bfs​ samples, and its fill level is the queueing delay it adds.

Keeping up. If each chunk of C seconds takes c seconds to process, the real-time factor is RTF=c/C. With RTF<1 the system keeps up; with RTF>1 the backlog after n chunks grows as n(c−C) and latency rises without bound.

Streaming delay. A word that ends at the start of a chunk is ready after the chunk fills, the look-ahead R arrives and the model runs:

delaymax​=C+R+c

Larger chunks and look-ahead give the model more context and lower error; smaller ones give lower delay.

End-to-end latency is the sum of the stages in the user's path: T=tcapture​+tVAD/end-of-turn​+tASR​+tLLM, first token​+tTTS, first audio​+tplayback​+tnetwork​. Streaming overlaps the stages so that each starts on partial input, which is how pipelines get under 500 ms.

VAD as detection. Each frame is a binary decision with a miss rate (speech labelled silence: clipped words) and a false-alarm rate (noise labelled speech: wasted compute, phantom turns), traded off by a threshold, like the speaker verification of Lesson 6.6.

Worked example
A latency budget by hand

Samples. 20 ms frames: 320 samples at 16 kHz, 960 at 48 kHz. A 2-second ring buffer at 16 kHz: 32,000 samples, 64 KB as 16-bit PCM.

Streaming ASR. 320 ms chunks, 320 ms look-ahead, 211 ms of compute per chunk: worst-case delay 320+320+211=851 ms of waiting and average compute (the run's worst case, 972 ms, uses the slowest chunk), plus the end-of-turn wait. RTF =211/320=0.66: it keeps up, with a third of the time to spare.

A backlog. If compute were 400 ms per 320 ms chunk (RTF 1.25), after one minute (187 chunks) the system would be 187×80=15,000 ms behind.

A full turn. VAD end-of-turn 300 ms + ASR final 150 ms + LLM first token 200 ms + TTS first audio 150 ms + network 100 ms = 900 ms. Streaming the LLM into the TTS and starting the LLM on a confident partial transcript are the usual ways to cut it in half.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "A bigger buffer makes the system safer."
    ✓ A buffer's fill level is added latency. Size it for real jitter, no more.
  • ✗ "Any ASR model can stream if you feed it chunks."
    ✓ Models trained on whole utterances degrade badly on short chunks without context; streaming models are trained with the limited context they will see.
  • ✗ "An energy threshold is a good enough VAD."
    ✓ It works in a quiet room and fails as soon as background noise is as loud as the threshold.

Idea 1 · Common gotchas

  • Thread contention. Python's GIL + heavy models can starve the audio thread. Use a C-callback audio library (sounddevice, PortAudio) and keep Python off the hot path.
  • Sample-rate conversion latency. Resampling filters add delay, from a few milliseconds to tens depending on the filter. Resample once, at the edge of the pipeline, with a streaming resampler.
  • TTS priming. The first request to any model is slower (memory allocation, kernel selection, caches). Load the model at start-up and warm it with a dummy run before the first real turn.
  • Echo cancellation. Without AEC, TTS output re-enters the mic and triggers ASR on the bot's own voice. WebRTC AEC3 is the open-source default.

Step 1 · ring buffer

import collections

class RingBuffer:
    def __init__(self, capacity):
        self.buf = collections.deque(maxlen=capacity)
    def write(self, frame):
        self.buf.extend(frame)
    def read(self, n):
        return [self.buf.popleft() for _ in range(min(n, len(self.buf)))]
    def level(self):
        return len(self.buf)

Capacity determines max buffering latency. 32,000 samples at 16 kHz = 2 s.

Two VADs on real speech in rising noise

Two LibriSpeech utterances with a 1 s pause, cut into 20 ms frames. Reference labels come from the clean signal's energy. Step 2's energy gate and WebRTC's VAD (aggressiveness 2) are run on the clean audio and with white noise at 20, 10 and 0 dB SNR.

import io, time
import numpy as np, soundfile as sf, webrtcvad
from datasets import load_dataset, Audio

libri = load_dataset("hf-internal-testing/librispeech_asr_dummy", "clean", split="validation").cast_column("audio", Audio(decode=False))
def clip(i):
    return sf.read(io.BytesIO(libri[i]["audio"]["bytes"]), dtype="float32")[0]

SR, FRAME = 16000, 320                                             # 20 ms frames
speech = np.concatenate([clip(0), np.zeros(SR, np.float32), clip(1)])   # two utterances with a 1 s pause
frames = lambda x: x[: len(x) // FRAME * FRAME].reshape(-1, FRAME)
# Reference labels from the clean signal: a frame is speech if its energy is within 35 dB of the loudest frame.
e_clean = 10 * np.log10((frames(speech) ** 2).mean(1) + 1e-12)
ref = e_clean > e_clean.max() - 35
print(f"{len(speech) / SR:.1f} s of audio = {len(ref)} frames of 20 ms; {ref.mean():.0%} labelled speech")

wvad = webrtcvad.Vad(2)                                            # aggressiveness 0 (lenient) to 3 (strict)
def webrtc_labels(x):
    pcm = (np.clip(x, -1, 1) * 32767).astype(np.int16)
    return np.array([wvad.is_speech(f.tobytes(), SR) for f in frames(pcm)])
def energy_labels(x, threshold=0.01):                              # Step 2's energy gate, vectorised
    return (frames(x) ** 2).mean(1) > threshold ** 2

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for snr in [None, 20, 10, 0]:
    if snr is None:
        x = speech
    else:
        noise = rng.standard_normal(len(speech)).astype(np.float32)
        x = speech + noise * np.sqrt((speech ** 2).mean() / (noise ** 2).mean() / 10 ** (snr / 10))
    for name, lab in [("energy gate", energy_labels(x)), ("WebRTC VAD", webrtc_labels(x))]:
        miss = np.mean(~lab[ref]); false_alarm = np.mean(lab[~ref])
        print(f"{'clean' if snr is None else f'{snr} dB SNR':>9s}  {name:11s}  speech frames missed {miss:5.1%}, "
              f"silence frames flagged {false_alarm:5.1%}")
t0 = time.perf_counter(); webrtc_labels(speech); per = (time.perf_counter() - t0) / len(ref)
print(f"WebRTC VAD cost: {per * 1e6:.0f} microseconds per 20 ms frame ({per / 0.02:.3%} of real time)")

Reading this output

The energy gate is the most accurate detector on clean audio (5% of speech frames missed, no false alarms) and fails abruptly: at 10 dB SNR the noise alone exceeds its threshold, and every silent frame is called speech. WebRTC's VAD looks at the spectrum, not just the level, so it degrades gradually (27% false alarms at 10 dB) until 0 dB, where it too flags everything. Of its false alarms on clean audio (40% of silent frames), about half fall within 200 ms after speech: like most production VADs, it keeps "speech" on for a moment after the voice stops (hangover) so that word endings are not clipped. The rest are in the gaps, where it reacts to breaths and room sound that the energy-based reference counts as silence. It costs 3 microseconds per 20 ms frame. Neural VADs such as Silero hold up much better in noise at a cost still well under a millisecond.

Streaming a recogniser that was not trained to stream

wav2vec 2.0 base on three longer LibriSpeech utterances. Each new chunk is processed with optional earlier audio (left context) and optional look-ahead, keeping only the new chunk's frames; WER against the reference, compute time per chunk and the worst-case delay before a word's text exists.

import torch
from transformers import Wav2Vec2Processor, Wav2Vec2ForCTC
proc = Wav2Vec2Processor.from_pretrained("facebook/wav2vec2-base-960h")
w2v = Wav2Vec2ForCTC.from_pretrained("facebook/wav2vec2-base-960h").eval()

def ctc_text(logits):
    ids = logits.argmax(-1).tolist(); out, prev = [], None
    for i in ids:
        if i != prev and i != proc.tokenizer.pad_token_id:
            out.append(i)
        prev = i
    return "".join(proc.tokenizer.convert_ids_to_tokens(out)).replace("|", " ").strip()

def stream(audio, chunk_s, left_s=2.0, right_s=0.0):
    """Process audio as it arrives: each new chunk is run with up to left_s seconds of earlier audio and right_s
    seconds of look-ahead (which the system must wait for), keeping only the new chunk's frames (one per 20 ms)."""
    C, L, R = int(chunk_s * SR), int(left_s * SR), int(right_s * SR)
    frames_out, compute = [], []
    for start in range(0, len(audio), C):
        ctx_start = max(0, start - L)
        seg = audio[ctx_start:start + C + R]
        seg = np.pad(seg, (0, (start - ctx_start) + C + R - len(seg)))   # the last chunk may be short: pad with silence
        x = proc(seg, sampling_rate=SR, return_tensors="pt").input_values
        t0 = time.perf_counter()
        with torch.no_grad():
            lg = w2v(x).logits[0]
        compute.append(time.perf_counter() - t0)
        skip = (start - ctx_start) // 320                           # frames that belong to the context, not the new chunk
        frames_out.append(lg[skip: skip + C // 320])
    return ctc_text(torch.cat(frames_out)), np.array(compute)

def wer(ref, hyp):
    r, h = ref.split(), hyp.split(); d = list(range(len(h) + 1))
    for i, a in enumerate(r, 1):
        prev, d[0] = d[0], i
        for j, b in enumerate(h, 1):
            prev, d[j] = d[j], min(d[j] + 1, d[j - 1] + 1, prev + (a != b))
    return d[-1] / len(r)

utts = [2, 5, 9]                                                     # three longer utterances
audio_all = {i: clip(i) for i in utts}
with torch.no_grad():
    offline = {i: ctc_text(w2v(proc(audio_all[i], sampling_rate=SR, return_tensors="pt").input_values).logits[0]) for i in utts}
off_wer = np.mean([wer(libri[i]["text"], offline[i]) for i in utts])
print(f"offline (whole utterance at once): WER {off_wer:.1%}")
for chunk_s, left_s, right_s in [(0.32, 0.0, 0.0), (0.32, 2.0, 0.0), (0.32, 2.0, 0.32), (1.0, 2.0, 0.0), (1.0, 2.0, 0.5)]:
    res = [stream(audio_all[i], chunk_s, left_s, right_s) for i in utts]
    w = np.mean([wer(libri[i]["text"], txt) for i, (txt, _) in zip(utts, res)])
    comp = np.concatenate([c for _, c in res])
    print(f"chunk {chunk_s * 1000:4.0f} ms, left {left_s:.0f} s, look-ahead {right_s * 1000:3.0f} ms: WER {w:5.1%}; "
          f"compute {comp.mean() * 1000:4.0f} ms per chunk ({comp.mean() / chunk_s:.2f}x real time); "
          f"worst-case delay {(chunk_s + right_s + comp.max()) * 1000:5.0f} ms")
print("example, 320 ms chunks without context:", repr(stream(audio_all[2], 0.32, 0.0)[0][:80]))

Reading this output

Offline, the model scores 4.4%. Fed isolated 320 ms chunks it collapses to 90% WER: most words are cut by a chunk boundary, and the model never saw such fragments in training. Two seconds of earlier audio halves the damage (51%); adding just 320 ms of look-ahead brings it to 12%, because the model can now see how each word ends. With 1 s chunks and 500 ms of look-ahead it reaches 2.9%, even below offline on these three utterances, at a worst-case delay of about 1.9 s. The other numbers are the budget: compute stays below the chunk length (RTF 0.23–0.66 on this CPU), so every setting keeps up. Streaming-trained models (NeMo's cache-aware FastConformers, Parakeet) are built to reach the accuracy of the last rows at the delay of the first.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Voice-agent frameworks. LiveKit Agents and Pipecat (by Daily) are widely used open frameworks that wire VAD, streaming STT, an LLM and streaming TTS over WebRTC, with turn detection and interruption handling built in.
  • Turn detection beyond VAD. Production stacks add a small model that decides whether the user has finished a thought (not just stopped making sound) before replying; Lesson 6.14 covers it.
  • Speech-to-speech APIs. OpenAI's Realtime API (gpt-realtime) and Gemini's Live API take audio in and give audio out over a streaming connection, removing the separate STT and TTS stages (Lesson 6.15).

Sources: Stivers et al. 2009, turn-taking across languages, OpenAI GPT-4o announcement, Silero VAD, LiveKit Agents, Pipecat.

Pitfalls

  • Buffering 500 ms to be safe. The buffer is your latency floor. Shrink it.
  • Not pinning threads. Audio callback on a priority-lower-than-UI thread = glitches under load.
  • TTS chunks too small. Very short chunks can leave audible seams at chunk boundaries; stream at the chunk size your TTS was built for, and crossfade if needed.
  • No jitter buffer. Real networks are jittery; without smoothing you get pops.
  • Single-shot error handling. Audio pipelines must be crash-proof. One exception kills the session.
Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Live audio arrives in fixed frames (20 ms); every stage must keep its real-time factor below 1.
  • Buffers and chunk sizes are latency; size them deliberately.
  • Streaming accuracy depends on context and look-ahead, and on whether the model was trained to stream; delay = chunk + look-ahead + compute.
  • Energy VADs fail in noise; spectral and neural VADs degrade more gracefully. Tune hangover and thresholds on your audio.
  • Overlap stages (stream LLM into TTS), support barge-in, and measure end-to-end latency, not stage averages.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Context and look-ahead buy accuracy with delay

Only the settings with look-ahead come close: 320 ms chunks with 2 s of left context and 320 ms of look-ahead give 12%, and 1 s chunks with 500 ms of look-ahead give 2.9%, at a worst-case delay of about 0.9 s and 1.9 s respectively. Without look-ahead, even 1 s chunks give 23%. Isolated 320 ms chunks give 90%.

SolutionB1 · Offline numbers, online delay

The WER is the 12% of the look-ahead row: the model sees exactly the same audio. But a live system cannot have audio from the future, so it must wait for it: the 320 ms of look-ahead adds 320 ms to every word's delay. An evaluation that reads ahead in the file reports the accuracy of the slower system with the latency of the faster one. Always simulate streaming by feeding audio in real time, or account for the look-ahead in the reported delay.
