Why this came about

Training a model means adjusting its numbers until its predictions are good. With a handful of numbers you could try values by hand; with millions you need a rule that says, for every number at once, which way to move it and roughly how much. Calculus, developed by Newton and Leibniz in the 1600s, provides that rule through the derivative. In 1847 Augustin-Louis Cauchy described gradient descent: repeatedly take a small step downhill. Combined with the chain rule and computers, this same idea trains everything from the line fit in Lesson 2.23 to the largest language models.

In plain words first
Walking down a hill in fog

Imagine standing on a hillside in thick fog, trying to reach the bottom of the valley. You cannot see the valley, but you can feel the slope under your feet. So you take a small step in the downhill direction, feel again, step again. The slope (or derivative) tells you which way is down and how steep it is. With many directions to choose from, the slopes in each direction together form the gradient. The learning rate is your step size: too small and you take forever, too large and you overshoot the valley and may end up higher than you started. A model's "valley" is the lowest possible loss, its measure of how wrong it is.

Step 1 · A slope: how much the output changes when you nudge the input

Here is a loss that depends on one setting, w. It is lowest (1.0) at w = 3.

import numpy as np

def loss(w):
    return 0.5 * (w - 3) ** 2 + 1

def slope(f, w, h=1e-5):
    """Numerical derivative: nudge w a tiny bit and see how much f changes."""
    return (f(w + h) - f(w - h)) / (2 * h)

for w in [-1, 0, 2, 3, 5]:
    print(f"w = {w:>2}   loss = {loss(w):5.2f}   slope = {slope(loss, w):6.3f}")
What you should see
w = -1   loss =  9.00   slope = -4.000
w =  0   loss =  5.50   slope = -3.000
w =  2   loss =  1.50   slope = -1.000
w =  3   loss =  1.00   slope =  0.000
w =  5   loss =  3.00   slope =  2.000
Reading this output
  • The slope at a point is "change in loss ÷ change in w" for a tiny nudge. The code nudges w by 0.00001 in each direction and divides.
  • A negative slope means the loss goes down as w goes up: move w up. A positive slope means move w down. At the bottom (w = 3) the slope is 0: no direction improves things.
  • The size of the slope says how steep it is: far from the minimum (w = -1) the slope is −4, close to it (w = 2) only −1.

For this particular curve the exact slope has a simple formula, w - 3 (you can check it against the table). You will rarely need such formulas: libraries compute slopes for you automatically (Lesson 3.15). What you need is the meaning: the slope tells you which way is downhill and how steep it is.

Step 2 · The chain rule: slopes through several steps

Models compute their output in many steps, each feeding the next. The chain rule says how a nudge at the start travels through: multiply the local slopes of each step.

def step1(w): return 3 * w           # local slope 3
def step2(u): return u ** 2          # local slope 2u
def step3(y): return y + 1           # local slope 1
def whole(w): return step3(step2(step1(w)))

w = 1.0
u = step1(w)
chain = slope(step1, w) * slope(step2, u) * slope(step3, step2(u))
print(round(chain, 4), round(slope(whole, w), 4))     # chain rule vs nudging the whole thing
What you should see
18.0 18.0

Both give 18. The chain rule lets a computer find the slope of a huge calculation by working backwards through it, step by step, multiplying local slopes as it goes. That backward pass is backpropagationlater · 3.15, and Lesson 3.15 builds it in about sixty lines.

Step 3 · Many inputs: the gradient

Real models have many settings. The slope with respect to one setting, holding the others still, is a partial derivative. Collect them all into a vector and you have the gradient. It points in the direction of steepest increase; so gradient descent steps the opposite way.

def loss2(w):                              # a bowl with its lowest point at (3, -1)
    return (w[0] - 3) ** 2 + 2 * (w[1] + 1) ** 2

def gradient(f, w, h=1e-5):
    g = np.zeros_like(w)
    for i in range(len(w)):                # nudge one coordinate at a time
        e = np.zeros_like(w); e[i] = h
        g[i] = (f(w + e) - f(w - e)) / (2 * h)
    return g

w = np.array([0.0, 0.0])
print(loss2(w), gradient(loss2, w).round(3))
What you should see
11.0 [-6.  4.]

At (0, 0) the gradient is [-6, 4]: the loss rises fastest if you move w[0] down and w[1] up, so to reduce it, move w[0] up and w[1] down. That is towards the lowest point (3, -1).

Nudging each coordinate one at a time costs two loss evaluations per setting. For a model with a billion settings that would be two billion evaluations for one step, which is why real training uses backpropagation, which gets every partial derivative in roughly the cost of one extra pass.

Step 4 · Gradient descent

The whole algorithm fits in one line, repeated: move every setting a small step against its slope.

w ← w − learning_rate × gradient

The same thing in code, for the 2-D bowl:

def descend(f, w0, lr, steps):
    w = np.array(w0, dtype=float)
    for _ in range(steps):
        w = w - lr * gradient(f, w)
    return w

for lr in [0.01, 0.1, 0.45, 0.55]:
    w = descend(loss2, [0.0, 0.0], lr, steps=50)
    print(f"learning rate {lr:<5} -> w = {np.round(w, 3)}   loss = {loss2(w):.4g}")
What you should see
learning rate 0.01  -> w = [ 1.907 -0.87 ]   loss = 1.227
learning rate 0.1   -> w = [ 3. -1.]   loss = 1.833e-09
learning rate 0.45  -> w = [ 3. -1.]   loss = 4.074e-10
learning rate 0.55  -> w = [3.000000e+00 9.099437e+03]   loss = 1.656e+08
Reading this output
  • 0.01: too small. After 50 steps w has moved only part of the way; the loss is still well above its minimum of 0.
  • 0.1: good. w reaches (3, -1) and the loss is essentially 0.
  • 0.45: still converges, but by bouncing back and forth across the valley in the steep direction, each bounce a little smaller.
  • 0.55: too large. Each step overshoots by more than it corrects, the steps grow, and the loss explodes. This is called divergence. The steep direction (the 2 * (w[1] + 1) ** 2 term) sets the limit: there each step multiplies the distance from the bottom by 1 - 4 × learning rate, so any rate above 0.5 makes it grow.

Choosing the learning rate is the most important practical decision in training. In Lesson F4.4 you will meet the standard remedies: optimizers such as Adam that adapt the step for each setting, and schedules that start small, warm up, then decay.

Step 5 · What this means for real models

  • The loss is the hill. For a language model it is the cross-entropy of Lesson F2.3, averaged over training text. The settings are the millions or billions of parameters.
  • Each training step computes the loss on a small batch of examples, gets the gradient by backpropagation, and moves every parameter a little against it. That is called stochastic gradient descent: stochastic (random) because each batch is a random sample of the data, so each gradient is a noisy estimate of the true one.
  • Real landscapes are not bowls. They have flat regions, saddle points and many valleys. In practice, for large networks, gradient descent with good settings finds valleys that are good enough, though nobody can prove it finds the best one.
The same idea as a formula
  • derivative: f′(w) ≈ (f(w + h) − f(w − h)) / 2h for a tiny h
  • chain rule: if y = g(u) and u = h(w), then dy/dw = (dy/du) · (du/dw)
  • gradient: ∇f(w) = (∂f/∂w₁, ∂f/∂w₂, …, ∂f/∂wₙ)
  • gradient descent: w ← w − η ∇f(w), with learning rate η
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "The gradient points to the minimum."
    ✓ It points uphill, in the steepest direction at your current spot. You step the opposite way, and only a little, because the direction changes as you move.
  • ✗ "A bigger learning rate always trains faster."
    ✓ Past a limit, steps overshoot and training diverges.
  • ✗ "You need calculus formulas to train models."
    ✓ You need to understand slopes; libraries compute exact gradients automatically with the chain rule.

Exercises

Predict before you run

In the Try it figure with learning rate 1.0, how many steps does it take to reach the minimum from w = -1? With 2.0? (Solution P1.)

Make it your own

Change loss in Step 1 to abs(w - 3) + 1 (a V shape instead of a bowl) and run gradient descent with learning rate 0.3 from w = -1 for 30 steps. What happens near the bottom, and why? (Solution M1.)

Debugging exercise

A colleague's training loss goes 2.3, 2.1, 5.8, 41.0, inf, nan. What is the most likely cause, and what is the first thing to try? (Solution D1.)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Special learning rates

With 1.0: one step. The slope at w is w - 3, so w - 1.0 × (w - 3) = 3 exactly: for this bowl, a learning rate of 1 jumps straight to the bottom. With 2.0: w - 2(w - 3) = 6 - w, so w flips between -1 and 7 forever, never settling. Above 2.0 it flies off (diverges).

SolutionM1 · A V-shaped loss

The slope of abs(w - 3) is −1 on the left and +1 on the right, never smaller, so every step has the same size, 0.3. Near the bottom w keeps hopping back and forth across 3 (for example between 2.9 and 3.2) and never settles. This is why learning-rate schedules shrink the step size over training: with constant steps, you cannot land exactly at a sharp minimum.

SolutionD1 · Exploding loss

Almost always a learning rate that is too large (or a sudden very large gradient). Try reducing the learning rate by a factor of 3 to 10 first. Other standard fixes, covered in Lesson F4.4: gradient clipping (capping the gradient's size), a warm-up period with a small learning rate, and checking the input data for extreme values.

Recap
  • A derivative is a slope: how much the output changes per unit change of the input; its sign says which way is downhill.
  • The chain rule multiplies local slopes through a sequence of steps; backpropagation applies it to whole networks.
  • The gradient collects the slopes for every setting and points uphill.
  • Gradient descent repeats w ← w − learning rate × gradient; too small is slow, too large diverges.
  • Training a neural network is stochastic gradient descent on the loss, with gradients from backpropagation.
