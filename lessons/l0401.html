Why this came about

Models know only what they saw in training, which ends at a cutoff date and never includes your private documents, and they fill gaps with plausible guesses. The 2020 retrieval-augmented generation paper proposed fetching relevant passages and giving them to the model, and after ChatGPT (2022) it became the most common way to build company assistants.

In plain words first
An open-book exam

A model only knows what it was trained on, which stops at a date and never includes your company's private documents. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) turns a closed-book exam into an open-book one: for each question, find the few passages that matter in your own documents and put them in the prompt, then ask the model to answer from them and cite them. Most of the work, and most of the failures, are in the finding.

Why RAG exists

A model knows only what was in its training data, up to its knowledge cutoff, and nothing about your company. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) fixes that at question time: find the relevant passages in your own content, put them in the prompt, and have the model answer from them, with citations. It is the most deployed pattern in enterprise AI because it is cheaper than fine-tuning, updates as soon as documents change, and can respect who is allowed to see what.

Read the figure as two separate pipelines. The indexing pipeline (top) runs ahead of time, whenever documents change: load each document, split it into passages, and store each passage in a form that can be searched quickly. The query pipeline (bottom) runs on every question: search the stored passages, put the best few into the prompt, and have the model answer from them.

RAG in thirty lines

Before the chapter takes each part seriously, here is the whole thing working, so that every later lesson has a place to fit. It uses two things you already have: the TF-IDF search of Lesson 5.5 to find a document, and the small open model of Lessons 10.10 and 11.6 to write the answer. Nothing here is a stand-in; the outputs are real.

The four steps are numbered in the code: index the documents ahead of time, retrieve the best match for a question, augment the prompt with it, and generate the answer.

import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM
from sklearn.feature_extraction.text import TfidfVectorizer
from sklearn.metrics.pairwise import cosine_similarity

name = "HuggingFaceTB/SmolLM2-360M-Instruct"       # the small open model from Lessons F7.2 and 2.6
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(name)
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(name, dtype=torch.float32).eval()

def ask(user, system=None, max_new_tokens=50):
    messages = ([{"role": "system", "content": system}] if system else []) + [{"role": "user", "content": user}]
    ids = tok.apply_chat_template(messages, add_generation_prompt=True, return_tensors="pt", return_dict=True)
    with torch.no_grad():
        out = model.generate(**ids, max_new_tokens=max_new_tokens, do_sample=False)
    return tok.decode(out[0, ids["input_ids"].shape[1]:], skip_special_tokens=True).strip()

# 1. Index: turn every document into a vector, once, ahead of time (TF-IDF, from Lesson F5.3)
names, texts = list(DOCS), list(DOCS.values())
vectorizer = TfidfVectorizer(stop_words="english")
doc_vectors = vectorizer.fit_transform(texts)

def retrieve(question, k=1):
    """2. Retrieve: the k documents most similar to the question."""
    scores = cosine_similarity(vectorizer.transform([question]), doc_vectors)[0]
    best = scores.argsort()[::-1][:k]
    return [(names[i], round(float(scores[i]), 2), texts[i]) for i in best]

def rag(question):
    """3. Augment the prompt with what was found, and 4. generate the answer from it."""
    found = retrieve(question)
    context = "\n\n".join(f"[{name}] {text}" for name, _, text in found)
    system = ("You answer questions for ShopKart customers. Use only the help-centre text below. "
              "If the answer is not in it, say you do not know.\n\n" + context)
    return ask(question, system), [(name, score) for name, score, _ in found]

question = "My kettle arrived broken two weeks ago. Can I still get a refund?"
print("WITHOUT RETRIEVAL:", ask(question))
answer, sources = rag(question)
print("\nRETRIEVED:", sources)
print("WITH RETRIEVAL:", answer)
What you should see
WITHOUT RETRIEVAL: As an AI, I don't have real-time access to your specific situation or the details of your kettle. However, I can provide general guidance on how to handle a broken kettle. 

If you've already contacted the manufacturer or the store

RETRIEVED: [('refund-policy', 0.34)]
WITH RETRIEVAL: Yes, you can still get a refund for the kettle.
Reading this output
  • Without retrieval the model does what Lesson 10.32 predicted. It has never seen ShopKart's policy, so it offers general advice about broken kettles.
  • Retrieved shows what the search found: the refund policy, with a similarity score of 0.34. The question and that document share the word "refund", and no other document contains that exact word.
  • With retrieval the answer is right, and it is right because the policy text was in the prompt: "damaged or defective items are always refundable ... if reported within 30 days", and two weeks is inside 30 days.

The model is the same in both runs. The only difference is 60 words of context. That is the whole idea of RAG.

Three ways it fails, on the same thirty lines

A working demo on one question proves little. Ask three more, and each one breaks in a different place.

for q in ["How much is express delivery?", "Do you ship to Nepal?", "How do I get my money back?"]:
    answer, sources = rag(q)
    print(f"Q: {q}\n   retrieved {sources}\n   A: {answer}\n")

Reading this output
Reading this output: which half failed?

A RAG system has two halves, retrieval and generation, and they fail separately. Always ask which one failed before changing anything.

  1. "How much is express delivery?" Retrieval was right; generation was wrong. The shipping document was found, and it says "Express delivery costs INR 99". The model answered "free for orders above INR 499", blending two neighbouring sentences. The fix is on the generation side: a stronger model, a shorter and more focused passage (chunking, Lesson 14.25), and an answer that quotes its source so the mistake is visible (citations, Lesson 14.27).
  2. "Do you ship to Nepal?" The answer is not in the documents at all. Retrieval returned the closest thing it had (shipping, at a weak 0.19), and the model, to its credit, declined instead of inventing an answer. This is the behaviour you want, and you have to test for it, because many models will guess.
  3. "How do I get my money back?" Retrieval failed. Look at the score: 0.0. The question says "money back"; the policy says "refund". They share no words, so word-matching search found nothing and returned an arbitrary document (account and login). The model then improvised from text that had nothing to do with the question. No prompt can fix this, because the right passage never reached the model.

The third failure is the most common one in real systems, and it is why this chapter spends six lessons on search: keyword search done properly (Lesson 5.21), embeddings that match "money back" to "refund" by meaning (Lesson 14.26), both together with a reranker (Lesson 5.22), and then a way to measure retrieval so you know which questions it fails on (Lesson 14.27).

Why not just put everything in the prompt?

For six short documents, you could. The case for retrieval appears as the knowledge base grows:

Paste every document into every prompt Retrieve a few passages per question
Six help pages (about 500 tokens) Works, and is simpler Unnecessary
A help centre of 2,000 pages (about 1.5 million tokens) Does not fit most context windows A few hundred tokens per question
Cost per question at $3 per million input tokens About $4.50 with 1.5 million tokens of context, where it fits at all Well under one cent
Speed Slow: every token is read on every call (Lesson 11.21) Fast
Accuracy Falls as context grows: facts in the middle get missed (Lesson 11.8) The model reads only what is relevant
Permissions Everyone's prompt contains everything Filter per user before the model sees anything (Lesson 14.28)

Long context windows and prompt caching have moved the line: for a knowledge base that fits comfortably and rarely changes, pasting it all in (sometimes called cache-augmented generationlater · 14.30, Lesson 14.30) is a reasonable design. Past that size, or when permissions differ per user, retrieval is not optional.

RAG, fine-tuning, or both?

Newcomers often ask why the model is not simply trained on the company's documents. They solve different problems:

You want the model to Use Why
Know facts that change, or that are private RAG Update a document and the next answer changes. Nothing is retrained, and sources can be cited
Follow a format, tone or narrow task more reliably Fine-tuning (the Models: training, evaluation and fine-tuning section) Behaviour is learned from examples; facts learned this way go stale and cannot be cited
Do both RAG for the facts, and a fine-tuned or well-prompted model for the behaviour They combine without conflict

Exercises

Predict before you run

A customer asks "Do you deliver on Sundays?" and the help center has no page about it. What does a model do without retrieval, and what should a RAG system do? (Solution P1.)

Break it on purpose

Paste the whole help center into every prompt instead of retrieving. It fits today. What breaks as the help center grows to 3,000 pages? (Solution B1.)

Debugging exercise

ShopKart updated its refund window from 7 to 10 days, but the assistant still says 7. The model was not changed. Where do you look first? (Solution D1.)

Make it your own

Write five questions about ShopKart policies, and for each, the help-center document that answers it. This is the start of the eval set used through the Retrieval (RAG) section. (Solution M1.)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Not in the corpus

Without retrieval, the model answers from its general training, which may be plausible and wrong for ShopKart. A RAG system retrieves nothing relevant and should say it does not know and offer a next step, which requires instructing the model to answer only from the context and testing that case.

SolutionB1 · Stuffing does not scale

Cost and latency grow with every page on every request, the context limit is eventually exceeded, and answers get worse as the relevant page is buried among irrelevant ones (Lesson 11.8). Retrieval sends only what the question needs, and its results can be cited.

SolutionD1 · Freshness, not the model

At the index: the updated page was not re-ingested, or the old chunk is still there next to the new one. Check what the retriever returned for the question. Every change to the source needs an indexing path, and old chunks must be deleted (Lesson 14.28).

SolutionM1 · A first eval set

Pairs like ("How long does a UPI refund take?", refund-policy) are what Lesson 14.27 scores retrieval against. Include one question with no answer in the corpus and one phrased in Hinglish, because those are where RAG systems fail first.

Check your understanding

Explain the mechanismWhat problem does RAG solve, and what does it not solve?

Worked answer. RAG gives a model access to information it was not trained on, such as private or recent documents, by retrieving relevant passages at question time and putting them in the context, so answers can be grounded and cited and updated by updating documents. It does not make the model reason better, fix bad or contradictory documents, or guarantee the model uses the context; those need good data, prompts and evaluation.

A plausible wrong answer. "RAG stops hallucinations." It reduces them when retrieval is good and the model is told to stay within the context; it does not eliminate them.

Follow-ups. When would you fine-tune instead of using RAG?

Debug a failureA RAG assistant gives a confident answer that is not in any document. How do you investigate?

Worked answer. Separate the halves: check what was retrieved for that question. If the right passage was missing, it is a retrieval problem (Lessons 5.21 to 5.22). If it was there, the model ignored or misread it, so check the prompt's instruction to answer only from the context, the order of passages, and whether citations are checked (Lesson 14.27).

A plausible wrong answer. "The model hallucinated; try another model." First find which half failed.

Follow-ups. How would you catch this automatically?

Defend a designRAG, long context, or fine-tuning for a company help assistant?

Worked answer. RAG first: the knowledge changes often, answers must cite sources, and access may differ by user. Long context works for a small, stable corpus and for one-off analysis of a long document. Fine-tuning teaches style and formats, not facts that change. Often the answer is RAG with caching of stable context.

A plausible wrong answer. "Fine-tune the model on the help center." It would memorize unevenly, could not cite, and would need retraining for every update.

Follow-ups. What would make you switch to long context?
