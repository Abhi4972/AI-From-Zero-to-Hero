In plain words first
The idea in one line

2026 audio generation is almost all tokens. EnCodec, SNAC, Mimi, and DAC turn continuous waveforms into discrete sequences that a transformer can predict. The semantic-vs-acoustic token split, first codebook for content and the rest for sound, is one of the most important design ideas in audio generation since 2023.

The problem

In plain words first

Language models predict the next item from a fixed vocabulary. Audio has no vocabulary: it is a stream of numbers. A neural audio codec creates one. An encoder squeezes each short slice of sound (13 to 75 slices per second) into a vector; a quantizer replaces that vector with the nearest entries from small learned "codebooks", so every slice becomes a handful of integers; a decoder turns the integers back into sound. Stacking codebooks, each fixing what the previous ones missed, is residual vector quantisation. Those integers are the tokens that music, speech and voice models predict. Two design choices matter most: how many slices per second (fewer means shorter sequences), and whether the first codebook is trained to capture what is said rather than how it sounds. This lesson runs two real codecs on LibriSpeech speech: EnCodec at five bitrates, scored by a perceptual metric and by Whisper, and Kyutai's Mimi with its first or last codebook scrambled. Nothing is trained.

Language models work on discrete tokens. Audio is continuous. If you want an LLM-style model for speech / music — MusicGen, Moshi, Sesame CSM, Orpheus — you first need a neural audio codec: a learned encoder that discretizes audio into a small vocabulary of tokens, and a matching decoder that reconstructs the waveform.

Two families have emerged:

  1. Reconstruction-first codecs — EnCodec, DAC. Optimize perceptual audio quality. Tokens are "acoustic" — they capture everything including speaker identity, timbre, background noise.
  2. Semantic-first codecs — Mimi (Kyutai), SpeechTokenizer. Force the first codebook to encode linguistic / phonetic content (often by distilling from WavLM). Subsequent codebooks are acoustic detail.

The 2024-2026 insight: a pure reconstruction codec gives you blurry speech when you try to generate from text. The LLM over codec tokens has to learn both language structure AND acoustic structure in the same codebook, which doesn't scale. Separating them — semantic codebook 0, acoustic codebooks 1-N — is what makes Moshi and Sesame CSM work.

The idea

The same idea as a formula

Residual vector quantisation. The encoder gives one vector zt​ per frame. With K codebooks C1​,…,CK​ of V entries each:

r0​=zt​,qk​=argjmin​∥rk−1​−ck,j​∥,rk​=rk−1​−ck,qk​​

and the decoder receives z^t​=∑k=1K​ck,qk​​. Each stage quantises what the earlier ones missed, so ∥rk​∥ shrinks with k, with diminishing returns. K codebooks of size V act like one codebook of VK entries that is never stored.

Bitrate and tokens. At frame rate f:

bitrate=f×K×log2​V bits/s,tokens per second=f×K

Training. Reconstruction losses on waveform and multi-scale spectrograms, adversarial losses from discriminators (for realistic detail), a commitment loss ∥z−sg(z^)∥2 that keeps encoder outputs near their codes, and, in Mimi, a semantic distillation loss: the first codebook's output is projected and pushed (by cosine similarity) towards features of a self-supervised speech model (WavLM), so codebook 1 carries content.

Quality metrics. Waveform SNR punishes any phase difference, which a generative decoder freely changes, so it says little. Perceptual metrics such as PESQ (1 to 4.5, designed for speech) and ViSQOL, ASR word error rate (intelligibility) and speaker similarity are used instead.

Worked example
Bitrates and compression ratios by hand

EnCodec at 6 kbps. 75×8×log2​1024=75×8×10=6,000 bits/s, 600 tokens per second.

Mimi in Moshi. 12.5×8×log2​2048=12.5×8×11=1,100 bits/s, 100 tokens per second. Ten seconds: 1,000 tokens.

Compared with raw audio. 24 kHz, 16-bit mono PCM is 24,000×16=384 kbps. EnCodec at 6 kbps compresses 64 times; Mimi at 1.1 kbps, about 350 times.

Vocabulary. Eight codebooks of 1,024 entries: 10248=280≈1.2×1024 combinations per frame, from 8×1024=8,192 stored vectors.

Idea 1 · The core trick: Residual Vector Quantization (RVQ)

Rather than one big codebook (which would need millions of codes for good quality), all modern audio codecs use RVQ: a cascade of small codebooks. The first codebook quantizes the encoder output; the second quantizes the residual; etc. Each codebook is 1024 codes. 8 codebooks = effective vocabulary of 10248=1024.

At inference time, the decoder sums all chosen codes per frame to reconstruct.

Idea 2 · The four codecs that matter in 2026

EnCodec (Meta, 2022). The baseline. Encoder-decoder over waveform, RVQ bottleneck. The 24 kHz model runs at 75 frames per second with 1,024-entry codebooks: 2 codebooks at 1.5 kbps up to 32 at 24 kbps. A convolutional encoder and decoder with LSTM layers, trained with reconstruction and adversarial losses. Used by MusicGen.

DAC (Descript, 2023). RVQ with L2-normalized codebooks, periodic activation functions, improved losses. Among the highest-fidelity open codecs; the 44.1 kHz model uses up to 9 codebooks (8 kbps). 44.1 kHz full-band.

SNAC (Hubert Siuzdak, 2024). Multi-scale RVQ — the coarse codebooks operate at a lower frame rate than fine ones. Effectively models audio hierarchically: for the 24 kHz speech model, codebooks at about 12, 23 and 47 Hz (0.98 kbps in total): a coarse sketch plus finer detail. Used by Orpheus-3B because the hierarchical structure maps well onto LM-based generation.

Mimi (Kyutai, 2024). 12.5 Hz frame rate, very low for a codec. Moshi uses 8 codebooks of 2,048 entries: 1.1 kbps (the run under Step 3 computes it). Codebook 0 is distilled from WavLM — trained to predict WavLM's speech-content features. Codebooks 1-7 are acoustic residuals. This split powers Moshi (Lesson 6.15) and Sesame CSM.

Idea 3 · Frame rates matter for language modeling

Lower frame rate = shorter sequence = faster LM.

Codec Frame rate 1 s = N frames Good for
EnCodec-24k 75 Hz 75 music, general audio
DAC-44.1k 86 Hz 86 high-fidelity music
SNAC-24k (coarse) ~12 Hz 12 AR-LM efficient
Mimi 12.5 Hz 12.5 streaming speech

At 12.5 Hz, a 10-second utterance is only 125 codec frames — a transformer can easily predict them.

Idea 4 · Semantic vs acoustic tokens

frame_t → [semantic_token_t, acoustic_token_0_t, acoustic_token_1_t, ..., acoustic_token_6_t]
  • Semantic token (codebook 0 in Mimi). Encodes what was said — phonemes, words, content. Distilled from WavLM via an auxiliary prediction loss.
  • Acoustic tokens (codebooks 1-7). Encode timbre, speaker identity, prosody, background noise, fine detail.

An AR LM predicts the semantic token first (conditioned on text), then predicts acoustic tokens (conditioned on semantic + speaker reference). This factorization is why modern TTS can zero-shot-clone voices: the semantic model handles content; the acoustic model handles timbre.

Idea 5 · 2026 reconstruction quality (bits per sec, lower bitrate is better)

Published PESQ and ViSQOL figures for these codecs vary with the test set, the bitrate and the version, so no single table is reliable; the run under Step 2 measures EnCodec on speech directly. Opus, a classic codec, remains the choice for transmitting audio; neural codecs win where a model needs discrete tokens (which Opus does not produce) to generate from.

Step 1 · encode with EnCodec

import io
import numpy as np, torch, librosa, soundfile as sf
from datasets import load_dataset, Audio
from transformers import EncodecModel, AutoProcessor

# The repository's sketch used the `encodec` package; the same model loads through transformers.
model = EncodecModel.from_pretrained("facebook/encodec_24khz").eval()
processor = AutoProcessor.from_pretrained("facebook/encodec_24khz")

libri = load_dataset("hf-internal-testing/librispeech_asr_dummy", "clean", split="validation").cast_column("audio", Audio(decode=False))
def clip(i):
    return sf.read(io.BytesIO(libri[i]["audio"]["bytes"]), dtype="float32")[0]

speech16 = clip(1)                                                       # 16 kHz LibriSpeech
wav = torch.tensor(librosa.resample(speech16, orig_sr=16000, target_sr=24000))[None, None]   # (batch, channels, samples)
with torch.no_grad():
    encoded = model.encode(wav, bandwidth=6.0)                           # kbps
codes, scale = encoded.audio_codes, encoded.audio_scales
print("codes:", tuple(codes.shape), codes.dtype, "values", int(codes.min()), "to", int(codes.max()))
# codes: (chunks, batch, n_codebooks, n_frames)

EnCodec at five bitrates, measured

Five LibriSpeech utterances through EnCodec 24 kHz at 1.5 to 24 kbps, scored with wide-band PESQ (a standard perceptual speech-quality metric, 1 to 4.5) and with Whisper base.en's WER on the decoded audio.

from pesq import pesq
from transformers import WhisperProcessor, WhisperForConditionalGeneration
wproc = WhisperProcessor.from_pretrained("openai/whisper-base.en")
asr = WhisperForConditionalGeneration.from_pretrained("openai/whisper-base.en").eval()
def transcribe(w16):
    with torch.no_grad():
        return wproc.batch_decode(asr.generate(wproc(w16, sampling_rate=16000, return_tensors="pt").input_features), skip_special_tokens=True)[0]
def wer(ref, hyp):
    r, h = wproc.tokenizer.normalize(ref).split(), wproc.tokenizer.normalize(hyp).split(); d = list(range(len(h) + 1))
    for i, a in enumerate(r, 1):
        prev, d[0] = d[0], i
        for j, b in enumerate(h, 1):
            prev, d[j] = d[j], min(d[j] + 1, d[j - 1] + 1, prev + (a != b))
    return d[-1] / len(r)

utts = [0, 1, 3, 4, 6]
refs16 = {i: clip(i) for i in utts}
print("bitrate  codebooks  tokens/s   PESQ (1-4.5)   Whisper WER")
for bw in [1.5, 3.0, 6.0, 12.0, 24.0]:
    p, e, n = [], 0, 0
    for i in utts:
        x24 = torch.tensor(librosa.resample(refs16[i], orig_sr=16000, target_sr=24000))[None, None]
        with torch.no_grad():
            enc = model.encode(x24, bandwidth=bw)
            y24 = model.decode(enc.audio_codes, enc.audio_scales)[0][0, 0, : x24.shape[-1]].numpy()
        y16 = librosa.resample(y24, orig_sr=24000, target_sr=16000)[: len(refs16[i])]
        p.append(pesq(16000, refs16[i], y16, "wb"))
        nw = len(libri[i]["text"].split()); e += wer(libri[i]["text"], transcribe(y16)) * nw; n += nw
    k = enc.audio_codes.shape[2]
    print(f"{bw:5.1f} kbps  {k:6d}     {k * 75:6d}       {np.mean(p):5.2f}          {e / n:6.1%}")
print(f"original audio, Whisper WER {sum(wer(libri[i]['text'], transcribe(refs16[i])) * len(libri[i]['text'].split()) for i in utts) / sum(len(libri[i]['text'].split()) for i in utts):.1%}")

The waveform SNR of the 6 kbps reconstruction above is only 6 dB, yet Whisper's WER on it (9.5%) is close to the original audio (8.8%): the decoder recreates sound, not samples. PESQ climbs from 1.55 at 1.5 kbps (2 codebooks) to 3.90 at 24 kbps (32), with each doubling of the bitrate buying less. Intelligibility saturates much earlier: by 6 kbps Whisper is as accurate as on the original (the differences between 9.5%, 11.8% and 10.3% are a word or two on about 150 words). A generative model needs tokens that are intelligible and compact, not perfect, which is why 4–8 codebooks is common.

Step 3 · the semantic-acoustic split (Mimi-style)

from transformers import MimiModel, AutoFeatureExtractor

mimi = MimiModel.from_pretrained("kyutai/mimi").eval()            # Kyutai's codec behind Moshi, in transformers
mfe = AutoFeatureExtractor.from_pretrained("kyutai/mimi")
x24 = librosa.resample(clip(1), orig_sr=16000, target_sr=24000)
inputs = mfe(raw_audio=x24, sampling_rate=24000, return_tensors="pt")

with torch.no_grad():
    codes = mimi.encode(inputs["input_values"], num_quantizers=8).audio_codes   # shape (1, 8, frames@12.5Hz)

semantic = codes[:, 0]
acoustic = codes[:, 1:]
print(f"{len(x24) / 24000:.2f} s -> codes {tuple(codes.shape)}: {codes.shape[-1] / (len(x24) / 24000):.1f} frames per second")
print("semantic codebook, first 12 frames:", semantic[0, :12].tolist())

Scrambling Mimi's codebooks

Mimi (in transformers as MimiModel) on the same five utterances: all 8 codebooks; the semantic codebook 0 replaced with random codes; the last acoustic codebook 7 replaced; only 1 codebook; all 32. Scored by Whisper WER and by speaker similarity (WavLM base+ SV) to the original.

from transformers import AutoFeatureExtractor as AFE, WavLMForXVector
sfe = AFE.from_pretrained("microsoft/wavlm-base-plus-sv")
sv = WavLMForXVector.from_pretrained("microsoft/wavlm-base-plus-sv").eval()
def spk(w16):
    with torch.no_grad():
        return torch.nn.functional.normalize(sv(**sfe(w16, sampling_rate=16000, return_tensors="pt")).embeddings[0], dim=-1)

def mimi_roundtrip(x16, n_q=8, corrupt=None, seed=0):
    x = mfe(raw_audio=librosa.resample(x16, orig_sr=16000, target_sr=24000), sampling_rate=24000, return_tensors="pt")["input_values"]
    with torch.no_grad():
        c = mimi.encode(x, num_quantizers=n_q).audio_codes
        if corrupt is not None:                                        # replace one codebook with random codes
            g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed)
            c[:, corrupt] = torch.randint(0, mimi.config.codebook_size, c[:, corrupt].shape, generator=g)
        y = mimi.decode(c)[0][0, 0].numpy()
    return librosa.resample(y, orig_sr=24000, target_sr=16000)[: len(x16)]

print(f"Mimi: {mimi.config.codebook_size} codes per codebook, 12.5 frames/s -> "
      f"{12.5 * 8 * np.log2(mimi.config.codebook_size) / 1000:.2f} kbps with 8 codebooks")
rows = [("all 8 codebooks intact", dict()), ("codebook 0 (semantic) randomised", dict(corrupt=0)),
        ("codebook 7 (last acoustic) randomised", dict(corrupt=7)), ("only 1 codebook used", dict(n_q=1)),
        ("32 codebooks used", dict(n_q=32))]
for name, kw in rows:
    e = n = 0; secs = []
    for i in utts:
        y = mimi_roundtrip(refs16[i], **kw)
        nw = len(libri[i]["text"].split()); e += wer(libri[i]["text"], transcribe(y)) * nw; n += nw
        secs.append(float(spk(y) @ spk(refs16[i])))
    print(f"{name:40s} Whisper WER {e / n:6.1%}   speaker similarity to the original {np.mean(secs):.2f}")

Reading this output

With all 8 codebooks (1.1 kbps) Whisper's WER is 11.0%, near the original, and speaker similarity is 0.98. Randomising the semantic codebook triples the WER (36.6%) while the voice stays recognisable (0.95): content lives there, as the distillation loss intends. Randomising codebook 7 barely moves WER (14.0%) or identity (0.97). With codebook 0 alone the decoder produces unintelligible audio (Whisper hallucinates long texts, so WER exceeds 100%) and the voice drifts (0.80): one codebook gives content structure without enough detail to render it. Using all 32 codebooks matches 8 on these measures. This split is what lets a model like Moshi predict the semantic token first and the acoustic ones after.

Step 4 · why AR LM over codec tokens works

For a 10 s speech clip at Mimi's 12.5 Hz × 8 codebooks:

Ntokens​=10×12.5×8=1000 tokens

1000 tokens is a trivial context for a transformer. A transformer handles that easily; schemes such as MusicGen's delay pattern or Moshi's small "depth" transformer, which predicts a frame's codebooks one after another, cut the number of expensive sequential steps to one per frame.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

Map problem → codec:

Task Codec
General music generation EnCodec-24k
Highest-fidelity reconstruction DAC-44.1k
AR LM over speech (TTS) SNAC or Mimi
Streaming full-duplex speech Mimi (12.5 Hz)
Sound-effect library with text EnCodec + T5 condition
Fine-grained audio editing DAC + inpainting

Rule of thumb: if you're building a generative model, start with Mimi or SNAC. If you're building a compression pipeline, use Opus.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Codecs in shipping systems. EnCodec (MusicGen and many research systems), DAC (high-fidelity music and sound), SNAC (Orpheus TTS), and Mimi (Kyutai's Moshi and Sesame's CSM) are the open codecs most often used; transformers includes EnCodec, DAC and Mimi.
  • Lower frame rates. The trend is fewer frames per second (Mimi's 12.5 Hz) and a semantically trained first codebook, so that speech language models can handle minutes of audio.
  • Classic codecs still carry the audio. Opus remains the codec for transmitting audio (WebRTC, VoIP); neural codecs are chosen for generation, where discrete tokens matter more than bits per second.

Sources: EnCodec, DAC, SNAC, Moshi and Mimi, Mimi in transformers.

Pitfalls

  • Too many codebooks. Every extra codebook adds the same number of tokens but less quality than the one before (the run under Step 2 shows the diminishing returns). Most generative systems use 4–8 for speech.
  • Frame-rate mismatch. Training LM on 12.5 Hz Mimi then fine-tuning on 50 Hz EnCodec fails silently.
  • Assuming all codebooks equal. In Mimi, codebook 0 carries content; losing it destroys intelligibility. Losing codebook 7 is barely noticeable.
  • Using reconstruction quality as the only metric. A codec can have great reconstruction but be useless for LM-based generation if the semantic structure is bad.
Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • A neural codec = encoder + residual vector quantiser + decoder; its integers are the tokens audio LMs predict.
  • Bitrate = frame rate × codebooks × bits per code; tokens per second = frame rate × codebooks.
  • Judge codecs by perceptual quality, intelligibility and speaker similarity, not waveform SNR.
  • Earlier codebooks matter more; Mimi's first codebook carries content by design.
  • Low frame rates make long audio tractable for language models.

  2. Medium. Install encodec and compare 1, 4, 8, 32 codebooks on a held-out speech clip. Plot PESQ or MSE vs bitrate.
  3. Hard. Load Mimi. Encode a clip. Replace codebook 0 with random integers; decode. Then replace codebook 7 similarly. Compare the two corruptions — codebook 0 corruption should destroy intelligibility; codebook 7 corruption should barely change anything.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Content in codebook 0, little in codebook 7

Scrambling codebook 0 raises Whisper's WER from 11.0% to 36.6% while the speaker similarity stays high (0.95); scrambling codebook 7 changes little (14.0%, 0.97). Neither destroys the voice, because the remaining acoustic codebooks still describe it; the semantic codebook is where intelligibility lives.

SolutionB1 · Wrong rate, no error

The codec accepts any array; it assumes 24 kHz. Treating 16 kHz samples as 24 kHz speeds the speech up by 1.5× and raises every frequency by the same factor, and the reconstruction, read back at 16 kHz, is the codec's version of that distorted sound. Whisper sees speech at the wrong speed and pitch and its transcript degrades. As in every lesson of this phase, check the sample rate at every boundary; feature extractors that take sampling_rate will raise an error, raw model calls will not.
