Why this came about

Early RAG systems were judged by reading answers, which hid whether failures came from search or from the model. Measuring retrieval with labeled questions (recall, ranks) and requiring citations in answers separated the two problems and made improvement systematic.

In plain words first
Check the search before blaming the answer

A RAG system has two halves that fail differently: retrieval (did we find the right passages?) and generation (did the model use them faithfully?). Measure them separately. For retrieval, label which passages answer each test question and compute recall at k (did a right one appear in the top k?) and ranks. For generation, require citations so every claim points to a passage you can check.

Evaluate the two halves separately

A wrong RAG answer has one of two causes, and the fix differs completely:

  • Retrieval failed: the right passage was not in the context. Fix chunking, search, rewriting or the content itself.
  • Generation failed: the passage was there, but the model ignored it, misread it or added things. Fix the prompt, the model or the context order.

So measure both, per question.

Step 1 · Measure before you tune

You now have three searches: keyword (Lesson 5.37), vector (Lesson 14.71) and hybrid (Lesson 5.38). Which is best on these documents? Reading a few results and forming an impression is how teams fool themselves. The reliable way takes ten minutes:

  1. Write down some questions in the words a real customer would use.
  2. Next to each, write the ID of the chunk that answers it. This pair list is a labelled question set. You will also hear it called a gold set or ground truth.
  3. Run every question through each search and count how often the right chunk comes back.

Two numbers summarise the result:

  • Recall@k is the share of questions whose right chunk appears in the top k results. Recall@3 = 0.88 means that for 88% of the questions, the right chunk was among the first three.
  • Reciprocal rank scores one question by where the right chunk landed: 1 if it was first, 1/2 if second, 1/3 if third, 0 if it was not found. The mean over all questions is the mean reciprocal rank (MRR). It rewards putting the right chunk near the top, not just somewhere in the list.
# A labelled question set and two retrieval metrics
GOLD = [
    ("My mixer arrived broken two weeks ago, can I still get my money back?", "refund-policy#1"),
    ("How long does a UPI refund take?", "refund-policy#1"),
    ("Do I pay for shipping on a 600 rupee order?", "shipping#0"),
    ("Can I stop an order that hasn't left the warehouse yet?", "cancellation#0"),
    ("Is there an extra charge if I pay cash when it arrives?", "cod#0"),
    ("I forgot my password", "account#0"),
    ("The TV was dead when it came out of the box", "warranty#1"),
    ("What does faster delivery cost?", "shipping#1"),
]

def recall_at_k(results, gold): return float(gold in results)
def reciprocal_rank(results, gold): return 1 / (results.index(gold) + 1) if gold in results else 0.0

for name, fn in [("BM25", search_bm25), ("Dense (LSA)", search_dense), ("Hybrid (RRF)", search_hybrid)]:
    r3 = [recall_at_k(fn(q, 3), g) for q, g in GOLD]
    mrr = [reciprocal_rank(fn(q, 10), g) for q, g in GOLD]
    print(f"{name:13} recall@3 = {sum(r3)/len(r3):.2f}   MRR = {sum(mrr)/len(mrr):.2f}")
What you should see
BM25          recall@3 = 0.88   MRR = 0.64
Dense (LSA)   recall@3 = 1.00   MRR = 0.73
Hybrid (RRF)  recall@3 = 1.00   MRR = 0.79

On this tiny set, hybrid beats both. Eight questions prove nothing statistically; the habit of measuring is the point. Lesson 14.77 builds a proper evaluation set.

For RAG, watch recall first: the model cannot use or cite a passage that was never retrieved. MRR matters next, because the passages at the top of the prompt get the most attention and because a better ranking lets you send fewer passages.

Step 2 · Four retrieval metrics, for questions with more than one right chunk

Step 1 assumed one right chunk per question. Real questions often have several: "what does delivery cost?" is answered by the standard-shipping chunk and the express-delivery chunk together. With a set of relevant chunks, four metrics are in common use, and you will see all four in papers, tools and vendor dashboards:

Metric The question it answers Read it as
Precision@k Of the top k results, what share is relevant? How much of what the model reads is useful
Recall@k Of all the relevant chunks, what share is in the top k? How much of what the model needs is there
MRR How high is the first relevant result? 1 for rank 1, 1/2 for rank 2, and so on
nDCG@k Are the relevant results near the top, all of them? 1.0 for the ideal order, lower as relevant results sink

nDCG (normalised discounted cumulative gain) is the only one that needs arithmetic to understand. Each relevant result earns a gain that shrinks with its rank: 1 / log2(rank + 1), which is 1.0 at rank 1, 0.63 at rank 2 and 0.5 at rank 3. Add the gains (that is the DCG), then divide by the best total any ordering could have earned, so the result lies between 0 and 1.

The functions below have their own names so that they do not replace Step 1's one-answer versions, which later lessons keep using.

import math

def precision_at_k(results: list[str], relevant: set[str], k: int) -> float:
    return sum(r in relevant for r in results[:k]) / k

def recall_set_at_k(results: list[str], relevant: set[str], k: int) -> float:
    return sum(r in relevant for r in results[:k]) / len(relevant)

def mrr_set(results: list[str], relevant: set[str]) -> float:
    return next((1 / i for i, r in enumerate(results, start=1) if r in relevant), 0.0)

def ndcg_at_k(results: list[str], relevant: set[str], k: int) -> float:
    """Discounted cumulative gain: relevant items count less the lower they appear, normalized to 0..1."""
    dcg = sum(1 / math.log2(i + 1) for i, r in enumerate(results[:k], start=1) if r in relevant)
    ideal = sum(1 / math.log2(i + 1) for i in range(1, min(len(relevant), k) + 1))
    return dcg / ideal

results = ["shipping#0", "cod#0", "shipping#1", "refund-policy#0", "account#0"]
relevant = {"shipping#1", "shipping#0"}
for name, value in [("precision@3", precision_at_k(results, relevant, 3)), ("recall@3", recall_set_at_k(results, relevant, 3)),
                    ("MRR", mrr_set(results, relevant)), ("nDCG@5", ndcg_at_k(results, relevant, 5))]:
    print(f"{name:12} {value:.2f}")
What you should see
precision@3  0.67
recall@3     1.00
MRR          1.00
nDCG@5       0.92

Check each number against the list. The top three results hold two relevant chunks, so precision@3 is 2/3. Both relevant chunks are in the top three, so recall@3 is 1.0. The first relevant chunk is at rank 1, so MRR is 1.0. For nDCG, the relevant chunks sit at ranks 1 and 3 and earn 1.0 + 0.5 = 1.5; the ideal order would put them at ranks 1 and 2 and earn 1.0 + 0.63 = 1.63; 1.5 / 1.63 = 0.92.

Which to watch? For RAG, recall@k first, for the reason in Step 1. Use precision when prompts are getting long and you want to send fewer passages, and nDCG when you are comparing rerankers, because it is the one that notices every change in order.

Step 3 · Generate with citations, then check them

Retrieval has found the passages. Now the model writes the answer from them. Two rules make that answer checkable:

  1. The prompt contains only the retrieved passages, each wrapped in a tag that carries its ID, and it tells the model to answer from those passages alone and to say it does not know when they do not contain the answer.
  2. Every claim cites a passage ID in square brackets, such as [refund-policy#1].

With those two rules, ordinary code can check an answer: every cited ID must be one of the passages that was actually sent, and an answer with no citation at all is suspect. So that this step runs without an API key, the model's reply is written in by hand below; to use a real model, send prompt with the client from Lesson 11.20.

# Generate an answer that cites its sources, then check the citations
TEXT = {cid: text for cid, _, text in CHUNKS}

def build_prompt(question: str, chunk_ids: list[str]) -> str:
    sources = "\n".join(f'<source id="{cid}">{TEXT[cid]}</source>' for cid in chunk_ids)
    return (f"Answer using only the sources below. Cite each claim like [source-id]. "
            f"If the sources do not contain the answer, say you don't know.\n\n{sources}\n\nQuestion: {question}")

def check_citations(answer: str, chunk_ids: list[str]) -> dict:
    cited = re.findall(r"\[([a-z\-]+#\d+)\]", answer)
    return {"cited": cited,
            "invalid": [c for c in cited if c not in chunk_ids],       # cited something not retrieved
            "uncited_answer": not cited}

question = "My mixer arrived broken two weeks ago, can I still get my money back?"
hits = search_hybrid(question)
prompt = build_prompt(question, hits)
answer = ("Yes. Damaged items are refundable if reported within 30 days of delivery [refund-policy#1]. "
          "UPI refunds usually arrive in 2 days [refund-policy#1].")          # what a model might return
print(hits)
print(check_citations(answer, hits))
What you should see
['refund-policy#1', 'refund-policy#0', 'shipping#0']
{'cited': ['refund-policy#1', 'refund-policy#1'], 'invalid': [], 'uncited_answer': False}

The reply cited one passage twice, that passage was among the three retrieved, and nothing was left uncited.

Be clear about what this check proves. It catches two failures cheaply: an answer that cites a source that was never retrieved (an invented reference), and an answer with no citation. It does not prove that the cited passage really supports the sentence next to it. Checking that needs a reader, human or model, and is the faithfulness metric of Lesson 14.77.

In production
Design a RAG system right from day one

Most RAG systems that struggle in production were missing one of these from the start, and each is far cheaper to build in than to retrofit: 1. A labeled question set before any tuning: 30 to 100 real questions with the chunks that answer them, split by question type. Without it, every later change is a guess. 2. Parsing you have looked at: open twenty parsed documents and read them. Most quality problems start here. 3. Chunks with context: every chunk carries its document title, section path, date and source URL, both as metadata and in the indexed text. 4. Hybrid search from the start: keyword plus vectors. Codes, names and exact terms are a large share of real questions. 5. Filters for permissions and tenants inside the search, never after it (Lesson 14.73). 6. Versioned indexes behind an alias, so re-chunking or a new embedding model is a side-by-side switch, not a migration. 7. Citations in every answer and a refusal path ("I couldn't find that in our policies"), so wrong answers are visible and missing content becomes a ticket, not a hallucination. 8. Incremental ingestion with deletions, so stale and removed documents leave the index (Lesson 14.73). 9. Traces of query, retrieved chunks and answer, so every complaint leads to one inspectable run (Lesson 14.145).

From the field
Uber: fix the documents before tuning the retriever

Uber's internal on-call copilot, Genie, answered security and privacy questions from policy documents. Their write-up on the improved version (May 2025) starts with ingestion, not models: they moved source documents from PDFs to Google Docs because the structure survived extraction, had an LLM convert tables into Markdown, kept table chunks intact through chunking, and attached a summary, keywords and generated FAQs to every chunk as metadata for keyword search. Together with the agentic retrieval steps described in Lesson 14.76, the redesign raised acceptable answers by a relative 27% and cut incorrect advice by a relative 60% on a golden set of more than 100 questions over 40-plus policy documents. Exercises

Predict before you run

A question's gold chunk is retrieved at rank 3. What are recall@1, recall@3 and the reciprocal rank? (Solution P1.)

Break it on purpose

Let the model answer with citations, but skip check_citations. A reply cites [refund-policy#1] for a statement that is not in that chunk. Who notices? (Solution B1.)

Debugging exercise

Retrieval recall@3 is 0.95, but answer accuracy is 0.70. Using "Evaluate the two halves separately", where is the problem and what do you check next? (Solution D1.)

Make it your own

Add two questions to GOLD that have no answer in the corpus, and a metric for whether the assistant correctly says it does not know. (Solution M1.)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Read the metrics

recall@1 = 0, recall@3 = 1, reciprocal rank = 1/3. Recall@k asks whether the answer is anywhere in the top k; MRR rewards putting it first.

SolutionB1 · Unchecked citations

Nobody, until a customer or an auditor does. A citation is a claim; checking that cited chunks were retrieved and that their text supports the sentence turns it into evidence (and catches invented chunk IDs).

SolutionD1 · Generation, not retrieval

Retrieval mostly finds the answer, so the loss is in generation: the model ignores, misreads or overrides the context. Check faithfulness (are claims supported by the retrieved text), the prompt's instruction to use only the context, passage order, and conflicting passages.

SolutionM1 · Measure abstention

Gold entries with no chunk need an abstention metric: the share of such questions where the answer says it cannot find the information, rather than inventing one. Without them, an eval rewards confident guessing.

Check your understanding

Explain the mechanismHow do you evaluate a RAG system?

Worked answer. In two halves. Retrieval: recall@k, MRR and nDCG against labeled relevant chunks. Generation: faithfulness to the retrieved context, answer correctness, citation validity, and abstention on unanswerable questions. Then end to end, by segment (question types, languages), on a fixed set that grows from production failures (Lesson 14.77).

A plausible wrong answer. "Ask users for thumbs up." Useful as a signal, too sparse and biased to be the eval.

Follow-ups. What is the difference between faithfulness and correctness?

Debug a failureAfter a corpus update, answer quality fell but retrieval metrics are unchanged. Why?

Worked answer. The eval questions may not cover the changed documents, or the gold labels point to chunks that changed meaning. Check failing production questions against the new documents, update gold labels for changed chunks, and add questions for new content. Also check for duplicate old and new versions retrieved together.

A plausible wrong answer. "The model got worse." The model did not change; the data did.

Follow-ups. How do you keep gold labels valid as documents change?

Defend a designLLM-as-judge or human labels for RAG evaluation?

Worked answer. Both: humans label a core set and calibrate the judge; the judge scales to every change and every segment. Check the judge's agreement with humans and re-check when models change. For critical domains, keep human review on a sample of production answers.

A plausible wrong answer. "The judge is enough." An uncalibrated judge can be confidently wrong.

Follow-ups. How many labeled questions do you need to compare two systems (Lesson 14.77)?
