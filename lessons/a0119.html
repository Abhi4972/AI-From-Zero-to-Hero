The problem

In plain words first

A complex number is a point on a plane, a + bi, where i is defined by i² = −1. The useful fact is what multiplication does: multiplying by a complex number of length 1 rotates a point, and multiplying by any complex number rotates and scales it. Euler's formula, e^(iθ) = cos θ + i sin θ, packs a rotation by θ into one number. That is why signals are analysed with complex numbers (the Fourier transform) and why transformers encode positions with rotations (RoPE).

You open a paper on Fourier transforms and there is i everywhere. You look at transformer positional encodings and see sin and cos at different frequencies -- the real and imaginary parts of complex exponentials. You read about quantum computing and find everything expressed in complex vector spaces.

Complex numbers seem abstract. A number system built on the square root of -1 feels like a mathematical trick. But it is not a trick. It is the natural language of rotations and oscillations. Every time something spins, vibrates, or oscillates, complex numbers are the right tool.

Without understanding complex numbers, you cannot understand the Discrete Fourier Transform. You cannot understand FFT. You cannot understand how RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding) works in modern language models. You cannot understand why sinusoidal positional encodings in the original Transformer paper use the frequencies they do.

This lesson builds complex arithmetic from scratch, connects it to geometry, and shows you exactly where complex numbers appear in machine learning.

Idea 1 · What is a complex number?

A complex number has two parts: a real part and an imaginary part.

z = a + bi

where:
  a is the real part
  b is the imaginary part
  i is the imaginary unit, defined by i^2 = -1

That is it. You extend the number line into a plane. The real numbers sit on one axis. The imaginary numbers sit on the other. Every complex number is a point in this plane.

Idea 2 · Complex arithmetic

Addition. Add the real parts together, add the imaginary parts together.

(a+bi)+(c+di)Example: (3+2i)+(1+4i)​=(a+c)+(b+d)i=4+6i​

Multiplication. Use the distributive law and remember that i2=−1.

(a+bi)(c+di)Example: (3+2i)(1+4i)​=ac+adi+bci+bdi2=ac+adi+bci−bd=(ac−bd)+(ad+bc)i=3+12i+2i+8i2=3+14i−8=−5+14i​

Conjugate. Flip the sign of the imaginary part.

conjugate of (a+bi)=a−bi

The product of a complex number and its conjugate is always real:

(a+bi)(a−bi)=a2+b2

Division. Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator.

c+dia+bi​=c2+d2(a+bi)(c−di)​

This eliminates the imaginary part from the denominator, giving you a clean complex number.

Idea 3 · The complex plane

The complex plane maps every complex number to a 2D point. The horizontal axis is the real axis, the vertical axis is the imaginary axis.

z = 3 + 2i  corresponds to the point (3, 2)
z = -1 + 0i corresponds to the point (-1, 0) on the real axis
z = 0 + 4i  corresponds to the point (0, 4) on the imaginary axis

A complex number is simultaneously a point and a vector from the origin. This dual interpretation is what makes complex numbers useful for geometry.

Idea 4 · Polar form

Any point in the plane can be described by its distance from the origin and its angle from the positive real axis.

zwhere: rθ​=r(cosθ+isinθ)=∣z∣=a2+b2​(magnitude, or modulus)=atan2(b,a)(phase, or argument)​

Rectangular form (a+bi) is good for addition. Polar form (r,θ) is good for multiplication.

Multiplication in polar form. Multiply the magnitudes, add the angles.

z1​z2​z1​z2​​=r1​eiθ1​=r2​eiθ2​=(r1​r2​)ei(θ1​+θ2​)​

This is why complex numbers are perfect for rotations. Multiplying by a complex number with magnitude 1 is a pure rotation.

Worked example
Worked by hand

(1 + i)² = 1 + 2i + i² = 1 + 2i − 1 = 2i. In polar form, 1 + i has length √2 and angle 45°; squaring multiplies lengths (√2 × √2 = 2) and adds angles (45° + 45° = 90°), and 2 at 90° is exactly 2i. Multiplying by i (length 1, angle 90°) four times turns any point a full circle back to where it started, which is why i⁴ = 1.

Idea 5 · Euler's formula

The bridge between complex exponentials and trigonometry:

eiθ=cosθ+isinθ

This is the most important formula in this lesson. When θ=π:

eiπTherefore: eiπ+1​=cosπ+isinπ=−1+0i=−1=0​

Five fundamental constants (e, i, π, 1, 0) linked in one equation.

Idea 6 · Why Euler's formula matters for ML

Euler's formula says that eiθ traces the unit circle as θ varies. At θ=0, you are at (1,0). At θ=π/2, you are at (0,1). At θ=π, you are at (−1,0). At θ=3π/2, you are at (0,−1). A full rotation is θ=2π.

This means complex exponentials ARE rotations. And rotations are everywhere in signal processing and ML.

Idea 7 · Connection to 2D rotations

Multiplying the complex number (x+yi) by eiθ rotates the point (x,y) by angle θ around the origin.

​Rotation via complex multiplication:(x+yi)(cosθ+isinθ)=(xcosθ−ysinθ)+(xsinθ+ycosθ)iRotation via matrix multiplication:[cosθsinθ​−sinθcosθ​][xy​]=[xcosθ−ysinθxsinθ+ycosθ​]​

They produce identical results. Complex multiplication IS 2D rotation. The rotation matrix is just complex multiplication written in matrix notation.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Imaginary numbers are not real, so they cannot matter for real data."
    ✓ They are a bookkeeping device for rotations and oscillations. Every result you use, such as a spectrum's magnitude, comes out as a real number.
  • ✗ "Multiplying complex numbers multiplies their angles."
    ✓ Lengths multiply; angles add. That is what makes e^(iθ) a rotation by θ.
  • ✗ "A DFT coefficient's size equals the amplitude of that frequency."
    ✓ For a real sine wave of amplitude A over N samples, the peak is A × N / 2. The run in Step 4's picture shows peaks of 16 and 8 for amplitudes 1 and 0.5 with N = 32.

Idea 8 · Phasors and rotating signals

A complex exponential eiωt is a point rotating around the unit circle at angular frequency ω. As t increases, the point traces the circle.

The real part of this rotating point is cos(ωt). The imaginary part is sin(ωt). A sinusoidal signal is the shadow of a rotating complex number.

eiωtReal part: Imaginary part: ​=cos(ωt)+isin(ωt)cos(ωt)– a cosine wavesin(ωt)– a sine wave​

This is the phasor representation. Instead of tracking a wiggly sine wave, you track a smoothly rotating arrow. Phase shifts become angle offsets. Amplitude changes become magnitude changes. Addition of signals becomes vector addition.

Idea 9 · Roots of unity

The N-th roots of unity are N points equally spaced on the unit circle:

wk​=e2πik/Nfor k=0,1,2,…,N−1

For N=4, the roots are: 1,i,−1,−i (the four compass points). For N=8, you get the four compass points plus the four diagonals.

Roots of unity are the foundation of the Discrete Fourier Transform. The DFT decomposes a signal into components at these N equally-spaced frequencies.

Idea 10 · Connection to the DFT

The Discrete Fourier Transform of a signal x[0],x[1],…,x[N−1] is:

X[k]=n=0∑N−1​x[n]e−2πikn/N

Each X[k] measures how much the signal correlates with the k-th root of unity -- a complex sinusoid at frequency k. The DFT breaks a signal into N rotating phasors and tells you the amplitude and phase of each one.

Idea 11 · Why i is not imaginary

The word "imaginary" is a historical accident. Descartes used it dismissively. But i is no more imaginary than negative numbers were when people first rejected them. Negative numbers answer "what do you subtract 5 from 3 to get?" The imaginary unit answers "what do you square to get -1?"

More usefully: i is a 90-degree rotation operator. Multiply a real number by i once, you rotate 90 degrees to the imaginary axis. Multiply by i again (i2), you rotate another 90 degrees -- now you are pointing in the negative real direction. That is why i2=−1. It is not mysterious. It is a half-turn built from two quarter-turns.

This is why complex numbers are everywhere in engineering. Anything that rotates -- electromagnetic waves, quantum states, signal oscillations, positional encodings -- is naturally described by complex numbers.

Idea 12 · Complex exponentials vs trigonometric functions

Before Euler's formula, engineers wrote signals as Acos(ωt+ϕ) -- amplitude A, frequency ω, phase ϕ. This works but makes arithmetic painful. Adding two cosines with different phases requires trigonometric identities.

With complex exponentials, the same signal is Aei(ωt+ϕ). Adding two signals is just adding two complex numbers. Multiplying (modulating) is just multiplying magnitudes and adding angles. Phase shifts become angle additions. Frequency shifts become multiplications by phasors.

The entire field of signal processing switched to complex exponential notation because the math is cleaner. The "real signal" is always just the real part of the complex representation. The imaginary part is carried along as bookkeeping, making all the algebra work out naturally.

Idea 13 · Connection to transformers

Sinusoidal positional encodings (original Transformer paper):

PE(pos,2i)PE(pos,2i+1)​=sin(pos/100002i/d)=cos(pos/100002i/d)​

The sin and cos pairs are the real and imaginary parts of complex exponentials at different frequencies. Each frequency provides a different "resolution" for encoding position. Low frequencies change slowly (coarse position). High frequencies change quickly (fine position). Together they give each position a unique frequency fingerprint.

RoPE (Rotary Position Embedding) takes this further. It explicitly multiplies query and key vectors by complex rotation matrices. The relative position between two tokens becomes a rotation angle. Attention is computed using these rotated vectors, making the model sensitive to relative position through complex multiplication.

Division multiply by conjugate Reverse rotation and rescale

The same idea as a formula

Two forms of one number. z=a+bi=r(cosθ+isinθ)=reiθ, with r=∣z∣=a2+b2​ and θ=argz=atan2(b,a). Euler's formula eiθ=cosθ+isinθ follows from the power series of ex, cosx and sinx.

Multiplication is rotation and scaling.

r1​eiθ1​⋅r2​eiθ2​=r1​r2​ei(θ1​+θ2​)

Multiplying by eiϕ rotates by ϕ; as a real 2×2 matrix acting on (a,b) it is (cosϕsinϕ​−sinϕcosϕ​). The conjugate zˉ=a−bi gives zzˉ=∣z∣2.

Relative angles survive a product with a conjugate. If qm​=qeimθ and kn​=keinθ, then

qm​kn​​=qkˉei(m−n)θ

depends on positions only through m−n. RoPE in transformers uses exactly this (Lesson 7.6).

Roots of unity. ωNk​=e2πik/N, k=0,…,N−1, are the N equally spaced points on the unit circle; for N>1 they sum to 0. The DFT measures how much of a signal lines up with each of them: Xk​=∑n​xn​e−2πikn/N.

See it drawn
Rotations that only remember the distance

Rotary position embeddings (RoPE) treat each pair of features in a query or key as a complex number and rotate it by an angle proportional to the token's position. Because rotations add angles, the dot product of a query at position m and a key at position n depends only on m − n:

import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
q, k = rng.standard_normal(2), rng.standard_normal(2)

def rotate(v, position, theta=0.3):
    z = complex(v[0], v[1]) * np.exp(1j * position * theta)
    return np.array([z.real, z.imag])

for m, n in [(2, 5), (10, 13), (40, 43), (5, 2)]:
    print(f"query at {m:2}, key at {n:2}: score {rotate(q, m) @ rotate(k, n):+.6f}")
What you should see

query at 10, key at 13: score -0.035165
query at 40, key at 43: score -0.035165
query at  5, key at  2: score +0.118041

Positions 2 and 5, 10 and 13, 40 and 43 all give the same score: only the offset of 3 matters. Swapping the order (5 and 2) gives a different score, so direction still counts.

Step 1 · Complex class

Build a Complex number class that supports arithmetic, magnitude, phase, and conversion between rectangular and polar forms.

import math

class Complex:
    def __init__(self, real, imag=0.0):
        self.real = real
        self.imag = imag

    def __add__(self, other):
        return Complex(self.real + other.real, self.imag + other.imag)

    def __mul__(self, other):
        r = self.real * other.real - self.imag * other.imag
        i = self.real * other.imag + self.imag * other.real
        return Complex(r, i)

    def __truediv__(self, other):
        denom = other.real ** 2 + other.imag ** 2
        r = (self.real * other.real + self.imag * other.imag) / denom
        i = (self.imag * other.real - self.real * other.imag) / denom
        return Complex(r, i)

    def magnitude(self):
        return math.sqrt(self.real ** 2 + self.imag ** 2)

    def phase(self):
        return math.atan2(self.imag, self.real)

    def conjugate(self):
        return Complex(self.real, -self.imag)

Step 2 · Polar conversion and Euler's formula

def to_polar(z):
    return z.magnitude(), z.phase()

def from_polar(r, theta):
    return Complex(r * math.cos(theta), r * math.sin(theta))

def euler(theta):
    return Complex(math.cos(theta), math.sin(theta))

Verify: euler(theta).magnitude() should always be 1.0. euler(0) should give (1, 0). euler(pi) should give (-1, 0).

Step 3 · Rotation

Rotating a point (x,y) by angle θ is one complex multiplication:

point = Complex(3, 4)
rotated = point * euler(math.pi / 4)

The magnitude stays the same. Only the angle changes.

Step 4 · DFT from complex arithmetic

def dft(signal):
    N = len(signal)
    result = []
    for k in range(N):
        total = Complex(0, 0)
        for n in range(N):
            angle = -2 * math.pi * k * n / N
            total = total + Complex(signal[n], 0) * euler(angle)
        result.append(total)
    return result

This is the O(N2) DFT. Each output X[k] is the sum of the signal samples multiplied by roots of unity.

Step 5 · Inverse DFT

The inverse DFT reconstructs the original signal from its spectrum. The only changes from the forward DFT: flip the sign in the exponent and divide by N.

def idft(spectrum):
    N = len(spectrum)
    result = []
    for n in range(N):
        total = Complex(0, 0)
        for k in range(N):
            angle = 2 * math.pi * k * n / N
            total = total + spectrum[k] * euler(angle)
        result.append(Complex(total.real / N, total.imag / N))
    return result

This gives you perfect reconstruction. Apply DFT, then IDFT, and you get back the original signal to machine precision. No information is lost.

Step 6 · Roots of unity

def roots_of_unity(N):
    return [euler(2 * math.pi * k / N) for k in range(N)]

Verify two properties: - Every root has magnitude exactly 1. - The sum of all N roots is zero (they cancel out by symmetry).

These properties are what make the DFT invertible. The roots of unity form an orthogonal basis for the frequency domain.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Three points on the circle

euler(0) = 1 + 0i, euler(π/2) = 0 + 1i (that is i), and euler(π) = −1 + 0i, up to rounding errors around 10⁻¹⁶ in the zero parts. All three have magnitude 1, because cos²θ + sin²θ = 1. euler(π) = −1 is Euler's identity, e^(iπ) + 1 = 0.

SolutionB1 · The missing 1/N

Every value comes back N times too large: a 4-sample signal returns 4 times its values. The forward DFT sums N terms without dividing, so the inverse must divide by N to undo it. (Some libraries split it as 1/√N in each direction, the "orthonormal" convention; NumPy's default puts the whole 1/N in the inverse, like this lesson.)

You are an expert in complex number arithmetic for machine learning and signal processing.

When someone asks about complex numbers, Fourier transforms, rotations, or positional encodings:

1. Identify which representation is best: rectangular (a + bi) for addition, polar (r * e^(i*theta)) for multiplication and rotation.

2. Key conversions:
   - Rectangular to polar: r = sqrt(a^2 + b^2), theta = atan2(b, a)
   - Polar to rectangular: a = r*cos(theta), b = r*sin(theta)
   - Euler's formula: e^(i*theta) = cos(theta) + i*sin(theta)

3. Common operations and their geometric meaning:
   - Addition: vector addition in the complex plane
   - Multiplication: rotate by arg(z2) and scale by |z2|
   - Conjugate: reflect over the real axis
   - Division: reverse rotation and rescale

4. ML connections:
   - DFT uses roots of unity: e^(-2*pi*i*k*n/N)
   - Positional encodings: sin/cos pairs are real/imag parts of complex exponentials
   - RoPE: explicit complex multiplication for position-dependent rotation of query/key vectors
   - FFT: recursive DFT using symmetry of roots of unity, O(N log N)

5. Quick checks:
   - |e^(i*theta)| = 1 always
   - z * conj(z) = |z|^2 (always real)
   - Sum of N-th roots of unity = 0
   - e^(i*pi) + 1 = 0 (Euler's identity)
   - Multiplying by e^(i*theta) rotates by theta radians

6. Python quick reference:
   - Built-in: z = 3+2j, abs(z), z.conjugate(), z.real, z.imag
   - cmath: cmath.phase(z), cmath.exp(1j*theta), cmath.polar(z)
   - numpy: np.abs(z), np.angle(z), np.conj(z), np.fft.fft(signal)

Hints

Exercise 1. Expand the product with i2=−1, then divide by multiplying the top and the bottom by the conjugate 1+3i. Check the product with Complex(2, 3) * Complex(4, -1): its magnitude() must equal the product of the two magnitudes, and its phase() must equal the sum of the two phases.

Exercise 2. Make the step with euler(math.pi / 6) and multiply 12 times. At each step, check that the magnitude stays 1 and that the angle grows by 30 degrees. The final point must be within about 1e-12 of (1, 0).

Exercise 3. Make the time points with t=n/32 and call dft. Look only at bins 0 to 15, because the upper half is a mirror image. The magnitude at bin k for a sine of amplitude A is AN/2, so expect about 16 at bin 3 and about 8 at bin 7.

Exercise 4. Call roots_of_unity(8) and add the roots: the magnitude of the sum must be near 0. Divide each root by the previous root and check that every result equals euler(2 * math.pi / 8).

Exercise 5. The rotation matrix is [[cos t, -sin t], [sin t, cos t]]. For each angle and point, compute euler(t) * Complex(x, y) and the matrix product, then record the largest absolute difference. Expect a value near 10−15.
