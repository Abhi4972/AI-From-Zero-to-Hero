Cross-Encoder Reranker

Lesson goals
What you will be able to do

  • Implement a small cross-encoder from scratch as a transformer block that consumes a packed (query, document) sequence and emits a single relevance scalar.

In plain words first
The idea in one line

A bi-encoder embeds query and document independently. A cross-encoder concatenates them and reads both at once. The cross-encoder is the smartest reader and the slowest. Used as a second stage on the bi-encoder's top-k, it pays for itself.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
Bi-encoder "Vector retriever" Encodes query and doc independently; cosine ranks them
Cross-encoder "Reranker" Encodes (query, doc) jointly; outputs one relevance scalar
Two-stage pipeline "Retrieve and rerank" Cheap retriever returns N, expensive reranker keeps K
N (candidate budget) "Rerank pool" The number of candidates the cross-encoder scores per query
Mean-pooling head "Mean of last hidden" Average the encoder's last-layer outputs into one vector

The problem

A bi-encoder maps query and document into the same vector space and ranks by cosine. The two encodings never see each other. The model has to compress everything useful about a document into a single vector, blind to the query. This is fast - one embedding per document at index time and one per query at query time - and it is the only way to rank at corpus scale.

The cost is precision. Two documents that have the same overall topic can have nearly identical embeddings even when one of them answers the query and the other does not. The bi-encoder cannot tell them apart.

A cross-encoder solves this by reading the query and the document together. The model receives [query] [SEP] [document] as a single sequence, runs full attention across the join, and produces one relevance scalar. Every token of the document can attend to every token of the query. The model decides the score with full context.

The cost is throughput. Where the bi-encoder embeds once and queries forever, the cross-encoder runs once per (query, document) pair. For a 10-million-document corpus that is 10 million forward passes per query. Unrunnable in a request budget.

The solution is staging. Use the bi-encoder to retrieve the top-N. Use the cross-encoder to rerank the N to a top-K. N is small (50 to 200) and the cross-encoder's quality lift is concentrated where it matters. The total latency stays in the request budget. The total quality is the cross-encoder's quality, capped by the bi-encoder's recall at N.

The idea

Idea 1 · The cross-encoder's input shape

The standard packing is [CLS] query_tokens [SEP] document_tokens [SEP]. The CLS-position output is fed into a single linear head that outputs the relevance scalar. Some implementations use mean-pooling instead of CLS; the difference is small. The point is that the model produces one number per pair.

A 22M-parameter cross-encoder (the published ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2 weight class) is the typical production point. Smaller models lose quality faster than they save latency. Larger models (e.g. bge-reranker-v2-m3 at 568M parameters) are reserved for offline reranking or for first-page reranking where K is small.

Idea 2 · Why this lesson trains a tiny one

A real cross-encoder is a finetuned encoder transformer. In production you load a checkpoint and run it. In this lesson the goal is to show you the shape of the model and the shape of the latency-quality curve, not to train a state-of-the-art ranker. So we build a small nn.Module with one transformer block, multi-head attention (4 heads by default), and one regression head. It is initialized deterministically from a seed so the demo is reproducible without weights on disk.

The toy model learns the right shape from the fixture corpus: relevant query-document pairs have higher predicted scores than irrelevant pairs. The end-to-end pipeline reranks the bi-encoder's output and the rerank's top-k correlates with the gold labels.

Idea 3 · Latency vs quality

The two-stage pipeline has one tunable: N. Sweep N from 5 to 100 on a held-out query set and you get the curve.

N Recall@1 of stage 2 Cross-encoder forward passes per query Latency
5 0.62 5 low
20 0.81 20 medium
50 0.86 50 high
100 0.86 100 very high

The numbers above are illustrative of the shape, not measurements from this fixture. The shape is real. There is always a knee around 20 to 50 candidates where the rerank lift saturates. Past the knee you are paying for nothing.

Pick N from the eval curve plus the latency budget. The cross-encoder cannot raise recall above the bi-encoder's recall at N, so a low N caps quality, not just latency.

Build it

code/main.py implements:

  • CrossEncoder - a small torch.nn.Module: token embedding, one transformer block with multi-head attention and feedforward, mean-pooled head producing one scalar.
  • tokenize_pair(query, document) - packs the two strings into a single id sequence with type ids that mark the boundary, deterministic and stdlib.
  • train_tiny(pairs) - one pass of supervised training on a hand-labeled (query, document, relevance) triple list, so the model produces sensible scores on the fixture.
  • rerank(query, candidates, top_k) - the production interface.
  • pipeline(query, retriever, top_n, top_k) - the two-stage flow.
  • A demo main() that loads the corpus from lesson 65's pattern, retrieves top-N, reranks to top-K, prints both lists side by side, and reports the latency of each stage.

Run it:

python3 code/main.py

The output shows the bi-encoder's top-N, the cross-encoder's top-K, and a timing summary. The cross-encoder takes longer per call but does not run on the full corpus. The two-stage total stays within the request budget while picking the answer that the bi-encoder ranked second or third.

Failure modes the demo will hide

Cross-encoder is not symmetric. rerank(q, d) and rerank(d, q) are different scores. Always feed the query first. If you accidentally swap, recall collapses.

N is too low to expose the bug. If you set N = K, the cross-encoder can reorder the K candidates but cannot bring a new one into the top K. Recall at K cannot move, so the lift looks zero. Pick N at least three times K.

Training data leaks into the eval. If the hand-labeled training pairs include the eval queries, the rerank looks magical. Strictly separate train and eval, even on a fixture.

Production weights are dense. A 22M-parameter cross-encoder is 88MB at float32. Plan the model server's memory before promising sub-100ms p95.

Batching matters. A real cross-encoder runs the N candidates in one batch. This lesson does that in _batch_encode, which builds the batched id and type-id tensors with torch.tensor(...) and runs one forward pass. Skip batching and the latency multiplies by N.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

Production patterns:

  • Pin the bi-encoder, cross-encoder, and N together. Changing any one invalidates the eval.
  • Cache the reranker's output by (query, document_id) hash. The same query against a stable corpus reranks to the same order; cache hits buy you a free latency cut.
  • Log the rank-1 cross-encoder score. A query whose top-1 score is below a corpus-specific threshold is an out-of-domain hit; surface it to the LLM as "I am not confident".

Interview questions

Try answering out loud first, then open one answer at a time.

Questions from real AI engineering interviews that this lesson prepares you for. Answer out loud first, then open the worked answer.

Weigh a trade-offYou add a cross-encoder behind a hybrid retriever. The prompt receives K chunks. The team wants a rule for N, the number of candidates the reranker scores, and one engineer proposes a small N to protect the latency budget. How do you choose N, and what do you refuse?

Level 3 of 4 · about 10 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Choose N from the recall curve of the reranked output and the latency budget together. Sweep N on a held-out query set; the lift saturates at a knee somewhere around 20 to 50 candidates, and past it every extra candidate costs a forward pass for nothing. Keep N at least three times K, because the reranker can only promote a chunk that the retriever put in the pool.

The one tunable.

The two-stage pipeline has one knob. The bi-encoder returns N candidates, the cross-encoder scores each pair and keeps K. The lesson shows the shape of the curve with illustrative values: recall at 1 rises from N of 5 to N of 20, rises less to N of 50, and does not change at N of 100. The values are not measurements from the fixture; the knee is real.

The cap.

The cross-encoder cannot raise recall above the bi-encoder recall at N. A low N caps quality, not only latency. If N equals K, the reranker can reorder the K candidates but cannot bring a new one in, so recall at K cannot move and the lift looks like zero.

The cost side.

  • Batch the N pairs into one forward pass. Scored one by one, the latency multiplies by N.
  • Plan the memory. A 22M parameter cross-encoder takes 88 MB at float32 before any batch.
  • Cache reranker output by the hash of the query and the document id. A stable corpus reranks the same way, so hits buy a free latency cut.

Pin the three together.

The bi-encoder, the cross-encoder and N are one choice. A change in any of them invalidates the eval, so the sweep runs again after a model change.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Set N equal to K to save time." Then the reranker can only reorder the K chunks and never bring a new one in, so recall at K cannot move.
  • "The larger N, the better." Past the knee the lift saturates and each extra candidate costs one forward pass.
  • "Pick N once and keep it." N is pinned with the two models; a change in either one invalidates the choice.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) Why does the curve flatten? Expected: past the knee the extra candidates are rarely relevant, so the reranker has nothing more to promote while each one still costs a forward pass.
  • (level 3) You cut N from 50 to 10 to save latency. What else moves? Expected: the recall cap, because the reranker cannot see a chunk outside the pool; run the eval again instead of assuming.
  • (level 4) The embedder is upgraded. Is the chosen N still valid? Expected: no; a new bi-encoder changes recall at N, so the sweep must run again before the release.
  • (level 4) How do you get the latency back without a smaller N? Expected: one batched forward pass for the N pairs, and a cache keyed on the query and the document id.

Where it is taught. The lesson cross-encoder gives the latency against quality curve, the three times K rule and the batching and memory notes.

Debug a failureA new cross-encoder reranker lifts MRR on the offline fixture to a near-perfect value. In production the same reranker makes the top result worse than the retriever alone. List the causes you check, in order, and say how each one shows up.

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Check the training data first: if the hand-labeled training pairs include the eval queries, the eval is a memory test and the lift is fake. Then check the pair order in production, because a cross-encoder is not symmetric and a swapped query and document collapses recall. Then check the candidate pool, because production candidates come from the real bi-encoder, and its recall at N caps what the reranker can see.

Cause 1: the eval leaked into training.

The reranker lesson warns that if the hand-labeled training pairs include the eval queries, the rerank looks magical. The end-to-end lesson makes the point concrete: its 14 training triples resemble the eval queries, so the demo keeps the eval queries disjoint on purpose. A leak shows as a near-perfect fixture and a production result that looks like a different model.

Cause 2: the pair is swapped.

The score for the query followed by the document is not the score for the document followed by the query. Always feed the query first. A swap shows as a collapse, not a drift, because every score is computed in the wrong direction.

Cause 3: the pool is not the production pool.

An offline fixture can hand the reranker a made-up pool that always contains the gold chunk. In production the pool comes from the bi-encoder, and the reranker cannot raise recall above the bi-encoder recall at N. The lesson's fix is to pin the bi-encoder, the cross-encoder and N together; a change in any of them invalidates the eval.

What to log so the next one is cheaper.

Log the rank-1 cross-encoder score per query. A top score below a corpus threshold marks an out-of-domain query, and a shift in the score distribution after a release marks a regression before users do.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The production model is a different build." Check the data and the inputs before the weights; a leak and a swapped pair both reproduce with the same weights.
  • "The fixture is too small to mean anything." Size does not create a leak; overlap between training and eval queries does.
  • "Lower N in production to match the fixture." A smaller N lowers the recall cap and makes the pipeline worse.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) How do you make the leak impossible? Expected: hold the eval queries out of the training triples strictly, even on a fixture, as the end-to-end lesson does.
  • (level 2) What does a swapped pair look like in the numbers? Expected: a collapse rather than a drift, because every score is computed for the wrong direction.
  • (level 3) Why does a synthetic candidate pool inflate the offline result? Expected: it can contain the gold chunk every time, while the real bi-encoder's recall at N caps what the reranker sees in production.
  • (level 3) Which one number do you log per query to catch the next regression? Expected: the rank-1 cross-encoder score; a top score below a corpus threshold marks an out-of-domain query.

Where it is taught. The lesson cross-encoder names the leak, the asymmetry and the pool cap, and the lesson end-to-end RAG keeps its training triples disjoint from its eval queries.
