In plain words first
The idea in one line

Transfusion mixes continuous and discrete representations. Show-o (Xie et al., August 2024) goes the other way: text tokens use causal next-token prediction, image tokens use masked discrete diffusion in the spirit of MaskGIT. Both sit inside one transformer with a hybrid attention mask. The result unifies VQA, text-to-image, inpainting, and mixed-modality generation on one backbone, one tokenizer per modality, one loss formulation (next-token extended to masked prediction). Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
Masked discrete diffusion "MaskGIT-style" Training to predict masked tokens; at inference, iteratively unmask the most-confident predictions
Cosine schedule "Unmask schedule" Decay of mask ratio over inference steps; concentrates confidence growth at mid-range
Parallel decoding "All tokens at once" Every step predicts the full sequence of masked tokens in one forward pass, then commits top-K
Hybrid attention "Causal + bidirectional" Mask that is causal over text tokens and bidirectional within image blocks
Inpainting "Fill-in generation" Condition on an image with some tokens masked, predict the missing ones; free from the training objective
Commitment rate "Top-K per step" How many tokens are declared "done" per iteration; controls inference vs quality trade-off

The problem

In plain words first

Generating an image one code at a time, as Chameleon does, takes hundreds or thousands of steps. Show-o (August 2024) borrows a faster trick from MaskGIT. Start with an image made entirely of "blank" [MASK] tokens. Predict every blank at once, keep the predictions the model is most sure about, blank the rest again, and repeat. A 256-code image is done in about 16 rounds instead of 256. Show-o does this inside one transformer that also reads and writes text normally, left to right. The attention mask lets image tokens see each other in every direction while text stays causal. One model then answers questions about images, generates images, and fills in masked regions (inpainting). This lesson explains masked prediction, the mask, the two losses and the sampling schedule. It checks a working sampler loop and a miniature training step. No model is trained.

Transfusion's two-loss training works but has trickier dynamics — the continuous diffusion loss lives on a different numerical scale from the discrete NTP loss. Balancing loss weights is a hyperparameter search. The architecture is effective but complex.

Show-o's answer: keep both modalities discrete (like Chameleon), but generate images in parallel via masked discrete diffusion instead of sequentially. The training objective becomes a single masked-token-prediction that generalizes next-token-prediction naturally.

Idea 1 · Masked discrete diffusion (MaskGIT)

The original Chang et al. (2022) MaskGIT trick is elegant. Start from a fully-masked image (every token is the special <MASK> id). At each step, predict all masked tokens in parallel, then keep the top-K most confident predictions and re-mask the rest. After ~8-16 iterations, all tokens are filled in. The schedule of how many tokens to unmask per step is tuned — cosine schedules work well.

Training is simple: draw a masking ratio from the cosine schedule (so every level from a few masked tokens to all of them is seen), apply it to the image's VQ tokens, and train the transformer to recover the masked ones. Exactly what BERT did for text, scaled to image generation.

Idea 2 · Show-o: one transformer, hybrid mask

Show-o puts MaskGIT inside a causal-language-model transformer. The attention mask is:

  • Text tokens: causal (standard LLM).
  • Image tokens: full bidirectional within the image block (so the masked tokens can see every other image token during prediction).
  • Text-to-image: text attends to prior images, image attends to prior text.

Training alternates between: 1. Standard NTP on text sequences. 2. T2I samples: text → image with masked image tokens, masked-token-prediction loss. 3. VQA samples: image → text with masked text tokens (really just NTP).

Show-o uses two cross-entropy losses over one vocabulary: mask token prediction (MTP) on the masked image tokens, and next token prediction (NTP) on text, combined as L=LMTP​+αLNTP​. The base model is Phi-1.5 (1.3B), and images are tokenized MAGVIT-v2-style with a lookup-free quantizer: a 256×256 image becomes 16×16 = 256 codes from 8,192.

Idea 3 · Parallel sampling

Show-o generates an image in a small number of parallel steps instead of one step per token. The paper shows samples at 2, 8 and 16 steps (quality rises with steps), against 256 sequential steps for an autoregressive model at the same resolution. At each step, predict all masked tokens in parallel; commit the most confident; repeat.

Compare: - Chameleon / Emu3 (autoregressive over tokens): N_tokens forward passes, typically 1024-4096 per image. - Transfusion (continuous diffusion): 250 denoising steps in the paper (fewer with modern samplers), each a full transformer pass over all patches. - Show-o (masked discrete diffusion): a handful to a few dozen steps (16 in the paper's examples), each a full transformer pass.

Show-o needs far fewer forward passes than Chameleon-style decoding at the same resolution (the paper claims roughly 20 times fewer steps). Its images stay limited by the discrete tokenizer, while continuous diffusion can reach higher quality.

Idea 4 · Tasks in one checkpoint

Show-o supports four tasks at inference, selected by prompt format:

  • Text generation: standard autoregressive text output.
  • VQA: image in, text out.
  • T2I: text in, image out via masked discrete diffusion.
  • Inpainting: image with some tokens masked, fill in.

The inpainting capability comes for free from the masked-prediction training. Mask a region of the VQ-token grid, feed the rest plus a text prompt, predict the masked tokens.

Idea 5 · Masking schedule

The schedule of how many tokens to unmask per step shapes quality. Show-o recommends cosine:

mask_ratio(t)=cos(2Tπt​)t=0,…,T

At step 0, all tokens masked (ratio 1.0). At step T, none masked. Cosine concentrates mass on mid-range ratios where prediction is most informative. Linear schedules also work but plateau faster.

The same idea as a formula

Cosine schedule. After step s of T, the number of image tokens still masked out of N is

ms​=⌊Ncos(2π​⋅Ts​)⌋,m0​=N, mT​=0.
Few tokens are committed early (when the model knows little) and many late.

Confidence selection. At each step, every masked position gets a prediction and a confidence ci​=maxk​p(k∣visible tokens); the ms​ least confident are masked again.

Training loss on one sequence with masked image positions M and text positions T:

L=−i∈M∑​logpθ​(xi​∣x∖M​)−αi∈T∑​logpθ​(xi​∣x<i​).

Worked example
A 256-code image in 16 steps
  • N=256, T=16. After step 1: ⌊256cos(π/32)⌋=254 still masked, so only 2 are committed.
  • After step 8 (half way): ⌊256cos(π/4)⌋=181 masked; 75 committed so far.
  • The last steps commit about 25 tokens each, when most of the image is already visible.
  • Forward passes: 16 instead of 256 for token-by-token decoding, 16 times fewer.

See it drawn
The MaskGIT sampler, step by step

A working MaskGIT-style sampler (cosine schedule, confidence ranking, re-masking) for a 16 × 16 grid of codes, with a stand-in predictor that knows a target image and is more often right when confident. The run reports tokens committed per step for 4, 8 and 16 steps and the final agreement with the target. This checks the sampler logic, not image quality. Instant.

import math, torch
torch.manual_seed(0)
# MaskGIT-style parallel decoding, the sampler inside Show-o, on a 16x16 grid of codes (a 256-px image, 8,192 codes).
# The "model" is a stand-in that knows a target image and is right more often for tokens it is confident about:
# this checks the sampler logic (schedule, confidence ranking, re-masking), not image quality.
N, K, MASK = 256, 8192, -1
target = torch.randint(0, K, (N,))
def fake_model(tokens):
    conf = torch.rand(N)                                  # confidence per position
    guess = torch.where(torch.rand(N) < 0.5 + 0.5 * conf, target, torch.randint(0, K, (N,)))
    return guess, conf

def sample(steps):
    tokens = torch.full((N,), MASK)
    log = []
    for s in range(steps):
        guess, conf = fake_model(tokens)
        masked = tokens == MASK
        keep_masked = math.floor(N * math.cos(math.pi / 2 * (s + 1) / steps))   # cosine schedule
        conf = torch.where(masked, conf, torch.tensor(float("inf")))          # already-fixed tokens stay fixed
        order = conf.argsort()                                                # least confident first
        new = tokens.clone()
        new[masked] = guess[masked]
        new[order[:keep_masked]] = MASK                                       # re-mask the least confident
        log.append(int((tokens == MASK).sum() - (new == MASK).sum()))
        tokens = new
    return tokens, log

for steps in [4, 8, 16]:
    tokens, log = sample(steps)
    acc = (tokens == target).float().mean()
    print(f"{steps:>2} steps: tokens fixed per step {log}; masked left {int((tokens == MASK).sum())}; "
          f"agreement with the target {acc:.0%}")
print(f"\nforward passes per image: autoregressive {N}, MaskGIT / Show-o {16} at 16 steps ({N // 16}x fewer)")

 8 steps: tokens fixed per step [5, 15, 24, 31, 39, 45, 48, 49]; masked left 0; agreement with the target 88%
16 steps: tokens fixed per step [2, 3, 7, 8, 11, 13, 15, 16, 19, 20, 22, 23, 23, 25, 24, 25]; masked left 0; agreement with the target 90%

forward passes per image: autoregressive 256, MaskGIT / Show-o 16 at 16 steps (16x fewer)
Reading this output

The schedule commits few tokens early and many late. With 16 steps the sampler fixes 2, 3, 7, … tokens at first and about 25 per step at the end; with 4 steps, 20, 55, 84 and 97. Early on almost nothing is visible, so the model's guesses are weakest. Committing only a few lets later steps condition on them. Every run ends with no masked tokens left, so the loop always finishes.

Confidence ranking does the work. The stand-in predictor is right 75% of the time on average, but more often when confident. Because the sampler keeps the most confident guesses, the final image agrees with the target on 88–90% of tokens, better than the predictor's average. A real model gains more from extra steps, since each step's predictions condition on the tokens committed before. That is why Show-o's samples improve from 2 to 8 to 16 steps.

16 passes instead of 256. Each step is one full forward pass over the whole grid, so a 256-code image costs 16 passes instead of 256 for token-by-token decoding. A step is a little more expensive (the whole grid is processed), but it is far fewer passes in total.

See it drawn
Show-o's training step in miniature

One vocabulary of 1,000 text ids, 8,192 image codes and [MASK]; an 8-token caption and an 8 × 8 code grid; masking at three ratios; Show-o's omni-attention mask; and the loss MTP + α·NTP from a random 2-layer transformer. Instant.

import math, torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F
torch.manual_seed(0)
# Show-o's training step in miniature: one transformer, one vocabulary of text + 8,192 image codes + [MASK],
# omni-attention (causal for text, full inside the image), loss = MTP on masked image tokens + alpha * NTP on text.
TEXT_V, IMG_V = 1000, 8192
V, MASK_ID, D = TEXT_V + IMG_V + 1, TEXT_V + IMG_V, 64
n_text, n_img = 8, 64                                   # an 8-token caption, then an 8x8 grid of image codes
ids = torch.cat([torch.randint(0, TEXT_V, (n_text,)), TEXT_V + torch.randint(0, IMG_V, (n_img,))])
is_img = torch.arange(len(ids)) >= n_text

for ratio in [0.25, 0.6, 1.0]:                          # MaskGIT trains on masking ratios across the range
    m = torch.zeros(len(ids), dtype=torch.bool)
    m[n_text + torch.randperm(n_img)[: math.ceil(ratio * n_img)]] = True
    print(f"masking ratio {ratio:.2f}: {int(m.sum())} of {n_img} image tokens replaced by [MASK]")
inp = torch.where(m, torch.tensor(MASK_ID), ids)        # the last (fully masked) case is the text-to-image start

L = len(ids)
mask = torch.tril(torch.ones(L, L, dtype=torch.bool)) | (is_img[:, None] & is_img[None, :])
emb, head = nn.Embedding(V, D), nn.Linear(D, V)
body = nn.TransformerEncoder(nn.TransformerEncoderLayer(D, 4, 4 * D, batch_first=True), 2)
logits = head(body(emb(inp)[None], mask=~mask))[0]

mtp = F.cross_entropy(logits[m], ids[m])                 # predict the codes under [MASK] (all at once)
ntp = F.cross_entropy(logits[: n_text - 1], ids[1:n_text])  # next text token
alpha = 1.0
print(f"\nomni-attention: {int(mask.sum())} allowed pairs (causal alone {L * (L + 1) // 2})")
print(f"MTP loss {mtp:.3f} over {int(m.sum())} masked codes, NTP loss {ntp:.3f} over {n_text - 1} text tokens "
      f"(random model: ln {V:,} = {math.log(V):.3f}); total MTP + alpha*NTP = {mtp + alpha * ntp:.3f} with alpha = {alpha}")
What you should see
masking ratio 0.25: 16 of 64 image tokens replaced by [MASK]
masking ratio 0.60: 39 of 64 image tokens replaced by [MASK]
masking ratio 1.00: 64 of 64 image tokens replaced by [MASK]

omni-attention: 4644 allowed pairs (causal alone 2628)
MTP loss 9.215 over 64 masked codes, NTP loss 9.165 over 7 text tokens (random model: ln 9,193 = 9.126); total MTP + alpha*NTP = 18.380 with alpha = 1.0
Reading this output

Masking ratio decides the task. Masking a quarter of the image tokens is close to inpainting (most of the image is given). Masking all 64 is the starting point of text-to-image generation, where only the caption is visible. Training on the whole range is what lets one checkpoint do both.

The omni-attention mask. A causal mask over 72 positions allows 2,628 pairs. Show-o's mask allows 4,644, because the 64 image tokens see each other fully (64 × 64 instead of 64 × 65 / 2). Every masked token can use every visible code around it, which is what parallel prediction needs.

Two losses, one vocabulary. The model predicts the codes under all masked positions (MTP) and the next caption token (NTP) from the same logits over 9,193 ids. With random weights both losses sit near ln 9,193 = 9.13, chance level. In training α sets the balance between the two, as λ does in Transfusion.

Idea 6 · Show-o2

Show-o2 (June 2025, arXiv 2506.15564) changes the recipe rather than only scaling it: images and videos live in a 3D causal VAE latent space (continuous, not discrete codes), text keeps autoregressive prediction on a language head, and images and video are generated by flow matching on a separate flow head. It is built on Qwen2.5 language models (1.5B and 7B).

Idea 7 · Where Show-o sits

In the 2026 taxonomy:

  • Discrete tokens + NTP: Chameleon, Emu3, LlamaGen, Janus-Pro. Simple but slow inference.
  • Discrete tokens + masked diffusion: Show-o, MaskGIT, Muse. Parallel sampling, still lossy by tokenizer.
  • Continuous + diffusion: Transfusion, MMDiT, DiT. Highest quality, more complex training.
  • Continuous + flow matching in a VLM: JanusFlow, Show-o2, BAGEL, InternVL-U. Newest.

Pick by task: Show-o when you want T2I + inpainting + VQA in one open model with reasonable speed; Transfusion when quality is paramount and you can afford the two-loss plumbing.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Show-o2 (June 2025): continuous 3D causal VAE latents, flow matching for images and video, autoregressive text, on Qwen2.5 1.5B and 7B.
  • Masked generative models remain the fast discrete option (MaskGIT, Muse, Meissonic); discrete diffusion is also being explored for text itself (diffusion language models such as LLaDA and Mercury).
  • Unified models mostly use continuous latents with diffusion or flow heads for image quality (Transfusion family, BAGEL, Show-o2).
  • Laptop reality: Show-o's 1.3B base fits an 8 GB GPU in bf16, making it one of the few unified models you can try locally.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · 2 tokens first, about 25 at the end

2 tokens in the first step and about 25 in the last. With N=256 and T=16, ⌊256cos(π/32)⌋=254 positions stay masked after step 1. In the last steps the curve is steep, and the run committed 23 to 25 tokens per step. The model commits the bulk of the image only when most of it is already visible.

SolutionB1 · Early guesses lock in and the image falls apart

If most tokens are committed in the first step, they are chosen when the model sees nothing but the caption. Each guess is made independently, with no neighbours to agree with, so committed tokens contradict each other: a sky patch next to a grass patch next to half a face. Committed tokens are never revisited, so these mistakes are locked in. The few remaining steps cannot repair a scene whose layout was decided by guesses that ignored each other. The result is incoherent or noisy images, even from a good model. The cosine schedule does the opposite. It commits a few high-confidence tokens first (often the global layout), then fills in more and more, each step conditioned on what is already fixed. This is also why very small step counts (2 steps) give poor images.
