In plain words first
The idea in one line

Speech recognition is audio classification at every timestep, glued together by a sequence model that knows English and silence. CTC, RNN-T, and attention are the three ways to do it. Pick one and understand why.

The problem

In plain words first

A speech recogniser turns about 100 frames of audio features per second into a few words per second, and nobody tells it which frames belong to which letter. Three designs solve that. CTC lets the network output a symbol or a special "blank" for every frame and defines the answer as whatever remains after merging repeats and deleting blanks; training adds up the probability of every frame-by-frame path that gives the right text. RNN-T (transducer) adds a small network that remembers what has been written so far, so each new symbol can depend on the previous ones. Attention encoder-decoders such as Whisper read the whole clip and then write the text token by token, like a translator. This lesson decodes real model outputs: a CTC model (wav2vec 2.0) and an encoder-decoder (Whisper) on LibriSpeech recordings, with greedy decoding, a correct beam search, the CTC probability computed two ways, and word error rate measured with and without text normalisation. Nothing is trained; both models are used as published.

You have a 10-second 16 kHz clip. You want a string: "turn on the kitchen lights". The challenge is structural: audio frames do not align one-to-one with characters. The word "okay" might take 200 ms or 1200 ms. Silence punctuates the utterance. Some phonemes are longer than others. The number of output tokens is not known in advance.

Three formulations solve this:

  1. CTC (Connectionist Temporal Classification). Emit per-frame token probabilities including a special blank. Collapse repeats and blanks at decode time. Non-autoregressive, fast. Used by wav2vec 2.0, MMS.
  2. RNN-T (Recurrent Neural Network Transducer). Joint network predicts next token given encoder frame and previous tokens. Streamable. Used by Google's on-device ASR, NVIDIA Parakeet.
  3. Attention encoder-decoder. Encoder compresses audio to hidden states, decoder cross-attends to generate tokens autoregressively. Used by Whisper, SeamlessM4T.

On LibriSpeech test-clean the best open models are now below 2% WER (NVIDIA's Parakeet-TDT-0.6B v2: 1.69%). Across many datasets the accuracy differences between top models are small; the differences in speed, streaming and languages are large.

The idea

CTC intuition. Let the encoder output T frame-level distributions over V+1 tokens (V chars + blank). For a target string y of length U < T, any frame alignment that collapses to y counts. CTC loss sums over all such alignments. Inference: per-frame argmax, collapse repeats, remove blanks.

Advantages: non-autoregressive, streamable, zero lookahead. Drawback: conditional independence assumption — each frame prediction is independent of the others, so there is no internal language model. Fix with an external LM via beam search or shallow fusion.

RNN-T intuition. Adds a predictor network that embeds the token history and a joiner that combines predictor state with encoder frame into a joint distribution over V+1 (the +1 is a null / no-emit). Explicitly models the conditional dependence CTC ignored. Streamable because each step conditions only on past frames and past tokens.

Advantages: streamable + internal LM. Drawback: training is more complex and memory-hungry (3D loss lattice); RNN-T loss kernels are a whole library category on their own.

Attention encoder-decoder. Encoder (6-32 transformer layers) over log-mel frames. Decoder (6-32 transformer layers) cross-attends to encoder outputs to generate tokens autoregressively. No alignment constraint — attention can look anywhere in the audio. Non-streamable unless you restrict attention or re-run on growing chunks (Whisper-Streaming, 2023).

Advantages: highest quality on offline ASR, easy to train with standard seq2seq tooling. Drawback: autoregressive latency is proportional to output length; cannot stream without engineering.

The same idea as a formula

CTC. The encoder gives, for each of T frames, a distribution pt​(k) over the V symbols plus blank. A path π=(π1​,…,πT​) picks one symbol per frame, with probability ∏t​pt​(πt​) (frames are treated as independent given the audio). The map B merges repeats, then deletes blanks: B(aa_ab_)=aab. The probability of a text y is the sum over all paths that collapse to it:

P(y∣x)=π:B(π)=y∑​ t=1∏T​pt​(πt​),LCTC​=−logP(y∣x)

The forward algorithm computes that sum without listing paths. Write y′=(_,y1​,_,y2​,…,yU​,_), of length 2U+1. Let αt​(s) be the probability of all path prefixes of length t ending at position s of y′:

αt​(s)=(αt−1​(s)+αt−1​(s−1)+[ys′​=_ and ys′​=ys−2′​]αt−1​(s−2))pt​(ys′​)

and P(y∣x)=αT​(2U+1)+αT​(2U). The skip over a blank is forbidden between equal labels, which is why a double letter needs a blank between its two copies.

RNN-T defines P(y∣x) over a lattice of (frame t, tokens emitted u): a joiner combines encoder frame ht​ with a prediction-network state gu​ (a summary of y<u​) and outputs P(k∣t,u) for each token or "move to the next frame". Unlike CTC, each token's probability depends on earlier tokens.

Attention encoder-decoder. P(y∣x)=∏u​P(yu​∣y<u​,Enc(x)), with the decoder cross-attending to all encoder frames: no alignment is imposed, and none is guaranteed.

Word error rate. With S, D, I the substitutions, deletions and insertions of the minimum edit alignment against N reference words, WER=(S+D+I)/N. It can exceed 100%. A corpus WER adds errors and words over all utterances before dividing.

Worked example
Collapsing paths and counting errors by hand

Collapse. Frames M M _ I S S _ T _ E _ R merge repeats to M _ I S _ T _ E _ R, then lose blanks: MISTER. For MANNER, the path must contain N _ N: without the blank, N N merges to one N.

How many paths? For 6 frames and the 1-letter text "a" over {blank, a, b}: a path is some blanks, a run of a's, some blanks. Choosing where the run of a's starts and ends gives (26​)+6=21 paths, out of 36=729. The run under "The idea" confirms 21 and the same total as the forward algorithm.

WER. Reference "THE CONTESTANTS IN THE TWENTIES" (5 words), hypothesis "THE CONTESTANTS INTO TWENTIES". Best alignment: IN → INTO (substitution), THE deleted. WER =(1+1+0)/5=40%.

Corpus vs average. Utterance A: 2 words, 1 error (50%). Utterance B: 50 words, 1 error (2%). Average of WERs: 26%. Corpus WER: 2/52=3.8%. Short utterances dominate the average; report the corpus figure.

The CTC sum, by brute force and by the forward algorithm

Six frames, a three-symbol vocabulary (blank, a, b) and random frame distributions. Every one of the 36 paths is listed and collapsed; the paths that give each text are added up and compared with the forward algorithm.

import numpy as np
import itertools

def collapse(path, blank=0):
    out, prev = [], None
    for s in path:
        if s != prev and s != blank:
            out.append(s)
        prev = s
    return tuple(out)

def ctc_forward(P, y, blank=0):
    """P(y | x) = sum over all alignments, by dynamic programming over the blank-extended label y' = _ y1 _ y2 _ ... _"""
    ext = [blank]
    for c in y:
        ext += [c, blank]
    T, S = len(P), len(ext)
    alpha = np.zeros((T, S))
    alpha[0, 0], alpha[0, 1] = P[0, blank], P[0, ext[1]]
    for t in range(1, T):
        for s in range(S):
            a = alpha[t - 1, s] + (alpha[t - 1, s - 1] if s >= 1 else 0)
            if s >= 2 and ext[s] != blank and ext[s] != ext[s - 2]:
                a += alpha[t - 1, s - 2]                      # skip the blank between two different labels
            alpha[t, s] = a * P[t, ext[s]]
    return alpha[-1, -1] + alpha[-1, -2]

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
T, V = 6, 3                                                   # 6 frames, tokens {blank, a, b}
P = rng.dirichlet(np.ones(V), size=T)
for y in [(1,), (1, 2), (1, 1), (2, 1, 2)]:
    paths = [p for p in itertools.product(range(V), repeat=T) if collapse(p) == y]
    brute = sum(np.prod([P[t, p[t]] for t in range(T)]) for p in paths)
    name = "".join("_ab"[c] for c in y)
    print(f"y = {name:4s}: {len(paths):3d} alignments of {V ** T}; brute-force sum {brute:.6f}, forward algorithm {ctc_forward(P, y):.6f}")
total = sum(np.prod([P[t, p[t]] for t in range(T)]) for p in itertools.product(range(V), repeat=T))
print(f"all {V ** T} paths together: {total:.6f} (every path collapses to some string, so the probabilities sum to 1)")

The brute-force sums and the forward algorithm agree to six decimals for every text, including "aa", which needs a blank between its two a's (35 paths against 21 for "a"). Check your mental model
  • ✗ "CTC's blank means silence."
    ✓ Blank means "no new symbol on this frame". A trained CTC model outputs blank on most frames, including in the middle of words.
  • ✗ "WER is a percentage of words, so it is between 0 and 100%."
    ✓ Insertions count too; a hypothesis with many extra words can exceed 100%.
  • ✗ "Whisper got 100% WER, so it is broken."
    ✓ Check normalisation first: casing, punctuation and spellings such as "Mr." against "MISTER" count as errors otherwise.

Idea 1 · WER: the one number

Word Error Rate = (S+D+I)/N, where S=substitutions, D=deletions, I=insertions, N=reference word count. Matches Levenshtein edit distance at the word level. Lower is better. For scale: Baidu's Deep Speech 2 paper measured human transcribers at 5.8% WER on LibriSpeech test-clean and 12.7% on test-other. Results from the Hugging Face Open ASR Leaderboard paper (2025): average WER over eight English test sets (LibriSpeech, TED-LIUM, earnings calls, meetings and more) and speed as RTFx, seconds of audio transcribed per second of compute:

Model Formulation Avg. WER RTFx
NVIDIA Canary-Qwen-2.5B Conformer encoder + LLM decoder 5.63% 418
NVIDIA Parakeet-TDT-0.6B v2 Transducer (TDT) 6.05% 3,386
NVIDIA Canary-1B-Flash Attention encoder-decoder 6.35% 1,046
NVIDIA Parakeet-CTC-1.1B CTC 7.40% 2,729
OpenAI Whisper large-v3 Attention encoder-decoder 7.44% 146

The pattern the paper reports: LLM-style decoders are most accurate on average but slower; CTC and transducer models are many times faster.

Step 1 · greedy CTC decode

def ctc_greedy(frame_logits, blank=0, vocab=None):
    # frame_logits: list of per-frame probability vectors
    preds = [max(range(len(p)), key=lambda i: p[i]) for p in frame_logits]
    out = []
    prev = -1
    for p in preds:
        if p != prev and p != blank:
            out.append(p)
        prev = p
    return "".join(vocab[i] for i in out) if vocab else out

Two rules: collapse consecutive repeats, drop blanks. Example: a a _ _ a b b _ c → a a b c.

Step 2 · beam-search CTC

def ctc_beam(frame_logits, beam=8, blank=0):
    import math
    beams = [([], 0.0)]  # (tokens, log_prob)
    for p in frame_logits:
        log_p = [math.log(max(pi, 1e-10)) for pi in p]
        candidates = []
        for seq, lp in beams:
            for t, lpt in enumerate(log_p):
                new = seq[:] if t == blank else (seq + [t] if not seq or seq[-1] != t else seq)
                candidates.append((new, lp + lpt))
        candidates.sort(key=lambda x: -x[1])
        beams = candidates[:beam]
    return beams[0][0]

Production uses prefix tree beam search with LM fusion; this is the conceptual skeleton.

Step 3 · WER

def wer(ref, hyp):
    r, h = ref.split(), hyp.split()
    dp = [[0] * (len(h) + 1) for _ in range(len(r) + 1)]
    for i in range(len(r) + 1):
        dp[i][0] = i
    for j in range(len(h) + 1):
        dp[0][j] = j
    for i in range(1, len(r) + 1):
        for j in range(1, len(h) + 1):
            cost = 0 if r[i - 1] == h[j - 1] else 1
            dp[i][j] = min(
                dp[i - 1][j] + 1,
                dp[i][j - 1] + 1,
                dp[i - 1][j - 1] + cost,
            )
    return dp[len(r)][len(h)] / max(1, len(r))

A real CTC model on real speech

wav2vec 2.0 base, fine-tuned on 960 hours of LibriSpeech (95 million parameters), on the 73 LibriSpeech utterances used throughout this phase. One frame per 20 ms, 32 symbols (letters, apostrophe, word separator |, and <pad> as the blank). Decoded with the Step 1 greedy rule and scored with the Step 3 WER.

import numpy as np, soundfile as sf, torch
from datasets import load_dataset, Audio
from transformers import Wav2Vec2Processor, Wav2Vec2ForCTC

libri = load_dataset("hf-internal-testing/librispeech_asr_dummy", "clean", split="validation").cast_column("audio", Audio(decode=False))

    return sf.read(io.BytesIO(libri[i]["audio"]["bytes"]), dtype="float32")[0]

    with torch.no_grad():
        return torch.softmax(w2v(x).logits[0], -1).numpy()                   # (frames, 32): one distribution per 20 ms

Greedy, the lesson's beam, and a correct prefix beam search

The same frame probabilities for the first 12 utterances. The prefix beam search keeps, for every prefix, the probability of paths ending in blank and ending in a letter, so it can tell a doubled letter from a held one.

from collections import defaultdict

def ctc_prefix_beam(P, beam=8, blank=0, prune=1e-3):
    """Proper CTC prefix beam search: each prefix keeps two probabilities, ending in blank / not in blank."""
    beams = {(): (1.0, 0.0)}                                   # prefix -> (p_blank, p_nonblank)
    for p in P:
        nxt = defaultdict(lambda: [0.0, 0.0])
        cand = np.where(p > prune)[0]
        for prefix, (pb, pnb) in beams.items():
            for c in cand:
                pc = p[c]
                if c == blank:                                 # stay on the same prefix, now ending in blank
                    nxt[prefix][0] += (pb + pnb) * pc
                elif prefix and c == prefix[-1]:
                    nxt[prefix][1] += pnb * pc                 # repeat without a blank: same character continues
                    nxt[prefix + (c,)][1] += pb * pc           # blank in between: a genuine double letter
                else:
                    nxt[prefix + (c,)][1] += (pb + pnb) * pc
        top = sorted(nxt.items(), key=lambda kv: -sum(kv[1]))[:beam]
        z = sum(sum(v) for _, v in top)
        beams = {k: (v[0] / z, v[1] / z) for k, v in top}      # renormalise to avoid underflow
    return list(max(beams.items(), key=lambda kv: sum(kv[1]))[0])

def to_text(tok):
    return "".join(show.get(k, vocab[k]) for k in tok).strip()

An attention encoder-decoder on the same speech, and why normalisation matters

Whisper base.en (74 million parameters, English only) through transformers on the first 20 utterances, scored four ways.

from transformers import WhisperProcessor, WhisperForConditionalGeneration
from transformers.models.whisper.english_normalizer import BasicTextNormalizer

wproc = WhisperProcessor.from_pretrained("openai/whisper-base.en")           # 74M parameters, English only
whisper = WhisperForConditionalGeneration.from_pretrained("openai/whisper-base.en").eval()
def transcribe(audio):
    feats = wproc(audio, sampling_rate=16000, return_tensors="pt").input_features
    with torch.no_grad():
        ids = whisper.generate(feats, max_new_tokens=128)
    return wproc.batch_decode(ids, skip_special_tokens=True)[0].strip()

basic = BasicTextNormalizer()                                                # lower-case, drop punctuation
english = wproc.tokenizer.normalize                                          # Whisper's English normaliser: also "mister" -> "mr", "1st" -> "first", ...

                           "wav2vec 2.0, English normaliser"]}

    if i < 2:
        print(f"ref: {ref[:75]}\nhyp: {hyp[:75]}")
    for name, r, h in [("Whisper, raw text", ref, hyp), ("Whisper, basic normaliser", basic(ref), basic(hyp)),
                       ("Whisper, English normaliser", english(ref), english(hyp)),
                       ("wav2vec 2.0, English normaliser", english(ref), english(hyps[i]))]:
        tot[name][0] += wer(r, h) * len(r.split()); tot[name][1] += len(r.split())
for name, (e, n) in tot.items():
    print(f"{name:32s} WER on 20 utterances {e / n:.2%}")
print(f"({time.time() - t0:.0f} s)")

Reading this output

Whisper writes "Mr. Quilter is the apostle of the middle classes, and we..." where LibriSpeech's reference says "MISTER QUILTER IS THE APOSTLE OF THE MIDDLE CLASSES AND WE". Unnormalised, every word differs in case and the WER is 100%. Lower-casing and removing punctuation gives 8.8%; Whisper's own English normaliser, which also maps spellings such as "mister" and "mr", gives 6.8%. That 2-point difference comes from text conventions, not hearing. The CTC model scores 5.5% on the same 20 utterances: it was fine-tuned on LibriSpeech itself, whose transcripts are upper-case with no punctuation, while Whisper base.en is a general model that writes formatted text. Compare models only with the same normaliser on the same data.

Whisper on audio with no speech

Ten seconds of silence, two levels of white noise, and a steady tone.

# Whisper on audio that contains no speech at all.
sr = 16000
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for name, audio in [("10 s of digital silence", np.zeros(10 * sr, np.float32)),
                    ("10 s of quiet white noise", (0.01 * rng.standard_normal(10 * sr)).astype(np.float32)),
                    ("10 s of louder white noise", (0.1 * rng.standard_normal(10 * sr)).astype(np.float32)),
                    ("a 440 Hz tone", (0.3 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 440 * np.arange(10 * sr) / sr)).astype(np.float32))]:
    print(f"{name:28s} -> {transcribe(audio)!r}")
What you should see

10 s of quiet white noise    -> 'the'

a 440 Hz tone                -> 'The End'
Reading this output

Whisper returns text for every one: "you" for silence, "the" for noise, "The End" for the tone. A decoder trained to always produce a transcript of 30-second windows has no reliable "nothing here" output, and on long recordings with gaps these phantom words accumulate (larger models produce longer phantoms, such as subtitle credits). A voice activity detector (Lesson 6.14) in front of the recogniser removes the problem at its source.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Open ASR models to know. Whisper large-v3 and large-v3-turbo (OpenAI; turbo cuts the decoder from 32 layers to 4 for speed), NVIDIA's Parakeet (CTC and TDT transducers, the fastest accurate English models) and Canary (encoder-decoder, multilingual), all with leaderboard results on the Hugging Face Open ASR Leaderboard. Its 2025 paper reports that conformer encoders with LLM decoders are most accurate on average, and CTC and TDT models are much faster.
  • Practical tooling. faster-whisper (CTranslate2) and whisper.cpp for fast CPU and edge inference; WhisperX for long-form transcription with VAD, word timestamps and speaker labels; NVIDIA NeMo for Parakeet and Canary.

Sources: Open ASR Leaderboard paper, Parakeet-TDT-0.6B v2, Whisper large-v3-turbo, faster-whisper, WhisperX.

Pitfalls that still ship in 2026

  • No VAD. Running Whisper on silence produces hallucinations ("Thanks for watching!"). Always gate with VAD.
  • Character vs word vs subword WER. Report word-level WER after normalization (lowercase, punctuation stripped).
  • Language ID drift. Whisper's auto LID mis-routes noisy clips to Japanese or Welsh; force language="en" when you know.
  • Long clips without chunking. Whisper has a 30-second window. With the transformers pipeline, pass chunk_length_s=30 (and optionally stride_length_s), or use the model's sequential long-form generation.
Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • CTC: per-frame symbols plus blank; merge repeats, drop blanks; the loss sums all paths with the forward algorithm.
  • RNN-T conditions each token on earlier tokens and streams; attention encoder-decoders read the whole clip and write freely.
  • Greedy CTC decoding is strong; a beam search must track blank and non-blank endings, and gains most with a language model.
  • WER =(S+D+I)/N; report corpus WER after the same normalisation for every system.
  • Encoder-decoders hallucinate on non-speech; gate with VAD.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Yes, if it is wrong

Yes, when the search itself is wrong. The lesson's simplified beam has a modelling error: it cannot represent a double letter, so it scores 15.5% WER against greedy's 6.6%. The full prefix beam search, which tracks blank and non-blank endings, gets 5.9%.

SolutionB1 · The processor checks the label, not the audio

Telling the processor sampling_rate=8000 while giving it 16 kHz audio raises an error: Whisper's feature extractor only accepts 16 kHz and compares the rate you declare with that. Actually resampling to 8 kHz and declaring 8000 raises the same error; you must resample up to 16 kHz first (librosa.resample(audio, orig_sr=8000, target_sr=16000)). The dangerous case is the one no check can catch: 8 kHz audio declared as 16 kHz, which plays at double speed with every frequency doubled.
