In plain words first
The idea in one line

GPT-4o's product demo in May 2024 was disruptive not because of the underlying model but because of the product shape — a voice interface where you talk, the model sees what the camera sees, and it talks back in about a third of a second (OpenAI reported as little as 232 ms, 320 ms on average). The open ecosystem spent the rest of 2024 and 2025 racing to reach that product surface. Qwen2.5-Omni (March 2025) is the reference open design: a Thinker (large text-generating transformer) plus a Talker (parallel speech-generating transformer), linked by streaming speech tokens. The problem

In plain words first

A voice assistant that sees and talks, like GPT-4o's voice mode, must answer within about a third of a second, and keep talking smoothly while it thinks. Omni models split the job in two. A large "Thinker" model reads text, images, audio and video and decides what to say. A smaller "Talker" model turns that into speech codes as the words come out, and a fast decoder turns the codes into sound. Qwen2.5-Omni (March 2025) and Qwen3-Omni (September 2025) are the main open versions. They also line up sound and video by real time (TMRoPE), so the model knows that a wave and a "hello" happened together. This lesson covers the split, time alignment, streaming speech, turn-taking and latency budgets. It simulates a thinker–talker pipeline to see what sets the time to first audio, and builds a time-aligned token sequence. No model is trained or downloaded.

A real-time voice assistant has to do a lot, fast:

  1. Hear the user. Real-time speech tokenization, voice activity detection (VAD) to know when they're done speaking.
  2. Optionally see. Camera input at 2-4 FPS, streamed into the Thinker alongside audio.
  3. Think. Compose a response conditioned on the conversation history.
  4. Speak. Synthesize audio tokens, decode to waveform, stream to the user's speakers.

Each step adds latency. Conversational-feel requires total round-trip < 500ms — below that, the user stops noticing the lag. GPT-4o claims ~250ms. Moshi ~160ms. Qwen2.5-Omni ~350-500ms.

Every component needs to stream. Nothing can be "batch everything then decode."

Idea 1 · Thinker and Talker

Qwen2.5-Omni's decomposition:

  • Thinker: the large text-generating transformer (initialised from Qwen2.5-7B in Qwen2.5-Omni-7B). It consumes interleaved text, image, audio and video tokens through a vision encoder (about 675M parameters) and a Whisper-large-v3-initialised audio encoder, and outputs text tokens.
  • Talker: a dual-track autoregressive transformer that reads the Thinker's hidden states and the text tokens it samples, and outputs discrete speech codec tokens. Feeding it hidden states, not just text, lets it pick up tone and emotion before the words are final.
  • Speech decoder: a flow-matching DiT with sliding-window block attention turns codec tokens into a mel-spectrogram, and a modified BigVGAN vocoder turns that into audio, chunk by chunk, so speech can start before the reply is finished.

The separation matters. Thinker has to be big for good reasoning. Talker can be small because its job is local — convert text to speech tokens. Bigger Talker is not more expressive; it's slower.

Running both in parallel:

  1. Thinker emits text token ti​.
  2. Talker consumes ti​ (via streaming) and emits speech tokens si​,si+1​,…,si+k​.
  3. Speech decoder consumes speech tokens as they come and emits audio samples.
  4. By the time Thinker is at text token ti+3​, Talker has already streamed audio for t0​,…,ti+2​.

Idea 2 · TMRoPE — time-aligned multimodal positions

Thinker needs to integrate image frames, audio frames (one encoder frame per 40 ms) and text from conversation history. A naive sequence order (all images, then all audio, then text) loses temporal alignment.

TMRoPE gives every token a temporal position id in units of 40 ms of real time: audio advances one id per 40 ms, and video frames take the id of their actual timestamp. The input is also cut into 2-second chunks, with each chunk's video tokens first and its audio tokens after, so the frame and the sound of the same moment sit close together and share temporal ids (measured below).

This is the infrastructure for "he waved while saying hello" to work — the model sees the video frame and the audio at the same conceptual moment.

Idea 3 · Streaming speech synthesis

Speech tokens must stream. Mini-Omni (Xie & Wu, 2024) introduced "language models can hear, talk while thinking in streaming": Thinker output tokens and Talker output tokens interleave in the same sequence. Talker fires as soon as Thinker commits the next text token. No batch boundaries.

Moshi (Défossez et al., October 2024) is a leading open full-duplex design: 160 ms theoretical and about 200 ms practical latency on one GPU. Architecture: a 7B language model (Helium) predicts text and speech tokens together, with an "inner monologue" of time-aligned text tokens, and a small depth transformer predicts each frame's residual codebooks from Mimi, a 12.5 Hz codec. This is effectively Thinker + Talker fused into one model with careful training.

The same idea as a formula

Time to first audio. With prefill time Tp​, thinker time per text token tθ​, talker time per speech frame tτ​, f frames per text token and a first playback chunk of c frames:

Tfirst​≈Tp​+tθ​+ctτ​+Tvocoder​.

Keeping up. Each audio frame lasts Δ ms (here 80); speech plays smoothly only if the pipeline produces frames faster than they play:

RTF=audio durationproduction time​<1,in steady state RTF≈max(Δtτ​​,fΔtθ​​).

TMRoPE time ids. A token at real time s seconds gets temporal id ⌊1000s/40⌋; audio advances one id per 40 ms, video frames take the id of their timestamp.

Worked example
Two talkers, one thinker
  • Thinker: 150 ms prefill, 25 ms per text token. Each text token becomes 3 speech frames of 80 ms (240 ms of audio).
  • Small talker at 8 ms per frame: first 4-frame chunk ready at about 150+25+4×8+20≈228 ms; RTF ≈8/80=0.1.
  • 7B-class talker at 90 ms per frame: first audio about 555 ms, and 90/80>1, so it falls behind: every frame after the first few arrives late.
  • A 900 ms prefill (a long video context) pushes first audio near one second even with a fast talker: context size costs latency directly.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "The Talker just reads the Thinker's text aloud."
    ✓ In Qwen2.5-Omni it also reads the Thinker's hidden states, which carry tone and emphasis.
  • ✗ "A bigger Talker sounds better."
    ✓ Past a point it only adds latency; it must produce frames faster than they play.
  • ✗ "Time alignment needs the video and audio tokens side by side."
    ✓ TMRoPE gives co-occurring tokens the same temporal id wherever they sit in the sequence.

A thinker–talker pipeline, simulated

A timeline simulation with assumed component times: the thinker streams text tokens after a prefill, the talker turns each into 3 speech frames of 80 ms, the vocoder adds 20 ms, and playback starts after 4 frames. Four setups report time to first audio, playback stalls and the real-time factor. The times are illustrative, not measurements of a specific model. Instant.

# A thinker-talker pipeline as a timeline simulation: when does the first audio play, and does speech keep up?
# Component times are assumptions for a 7B-class thinker on one GPU, not measurements of a specific model.
def simulate(think_ms_per_token, talk_ms_per_frame, frames_per_text_token, prefill_ms=150, first_chunk_frames=4,
             codec_ms=20, frame_ms=80, n_text=40):
    text_ready = [prefill_ms + think_ms_per_token * (i + 1) for i in range(n_text)]   # thinker streams text tokens
    t, frames_done, audio = 0.0, 0, []
    for i, ready in enumerate(text_ready):                 # talker turns each text token into speech frames
        for _ in range(frames_per_text_token):
            t = max(t, ready) + talk_ms_per_frame
            frames_done += 1
            audio.append(t + codec_ms)                     # frame playable after the vocoder
    first = audio[first_chunk_frames - 1]
    play = [first + k * frame_ms for k in range(len(audio))]   # playback schedule once audio starts
    stalls = sum(a > p for a, p in zip(audio, play))
    rtf = (audio[-1] - audio[0]) / (len(audio) * frame_ms)
    return first, stalls, rtf, len(audio) * frame_ms / 1000

print(f"{'setup':<54}{'first audio':>12}{'stalls':>8}{'real-time factor':>18}")
for label, kw in [("small talker, fast thinker (25 ms/token)", dict(think_ms_per_token=25, talk_ms_per_frame=8, frames_per_text_token=3)),
                  ("small talker, slow 70B-class thinker (60 ms/token)", dict(think_ms_per_token=60, talk_ms_per_frame=8, frames_per_text_token=3)),

Reading this output

A fast pipeline speaks in about a quarter of a second. With a 150 ms prefill, a thinker at 25 ms per text token and a small talker at 8 ms per frame, the first 4-frame chunk is ready after about 228 ms. Then speech is produced ten times faster than it plays (real-time factor 0.10), with no stalls.

A slower thinker costs little. At 60 ms per token (70B-class speed), first audio comes after 298 ms and the factor is 0.25. Each text token yields 240 ms of speech, so even a slow thinker stays far ahead of playback.

A slow talker breaks the conversation. At 90 ms per 80 ms frame, the first audio comes after 555 ms. Worse, 92 of 120 frames arrive after their playback time: the voice stutters and falls further behind (factor 1.12). That is why talkers are small and why residual codebooks are predicted in parallel.

Context costs latency directly. A 900 ms prefill, for example a long video context, delays first audio to 978 ms even with fast components. Keep the live context small: summaries instead of raw history, few frames, cached prefixes (Lesson 11.15).

TMRoPE: one timeline for sound and vision

Four seconds of video with audio arranged as Qwen2.5-Omni does: 2-second chunks, video tokens first, then audio tokens, with temporal ids in 40 ms units. The run prints selected positions and their ids, and the tokens that share one moment. Instant.

# TMRoPE-style ordering, as in Qwen's omni models: one temporal position id = 40 ms; video and audio of the same moment are cut
# into 2-second chunks, and inside each chunk the video tokens come first, then the audio tokens.
CHUNK_S, ID_MS = 2.0, 40
video_fps, video_tokens_per_frame = 2, 4            # tiny numbers so the sequence fits on screen
audio_tok_per_s = 25                                # one audio token per 40 ms
duration = 4.0
seq = []
for c in range(int(duration / CHUNK_S)):
    t0 = c * CHUNK_S
    for f in range(int(CHUNK_S * video_fps)):
        ts = t0 + f / video_fps
        seq += [("video", ts, round(ts * 1000 / ID_MS))] * video_tokens_per_frame
    for a in range(int(CHUNK_S * audio_tok_per_s)):
        ts = t0 + a / audio_tok_per_s
        seq.append(("audio", ts, round(ts * 1000 / ID_MS)))
print(f"{duration:.0f} s of video with audio -> {len(seq)} tokens in {int(duration / CHUNK_S)} chunks of {CHUNK_S:.0f} s")
for i in [0, 4, 15, 16, 17, 65, 66, 82, 131]:
    kind, ts, tid = seq[i]
    print(f"  position {i:>3}: {kind:<5} at {ts:4.2f} s -> temporal id {tid}")
same = [(k, round(ts, 2)) for k, ts, tid in seq if tid == 25]
print(f"\ntokens sharing temporal id 25 (t = 1.00 s): {len(same)} -> {sorted(set(k for k, _ in same))}: "

Reading this output

Chunks keep the sequence close to the timeline. The first 2 seconds hold 16 video tokens (4 frames × 4 tokens) and then 50 audio tokens; the next chunk repeats the pattern. A token's position in the sequence still roughly follows time: nothing from second 3 appears before second 1.

Temporal ids carry the real time. The video frame at 0.50 s gets id 12 and the one at 1.50 s gets id 38; audio advances one id every 40 ms (0, 1, …, 49). At 2.00 s, the video frame (position 66) and the audio (position 82) both get id 50, though they are 16 positions apart.

Co-occurrence is explicit. Five tokens share temporal id 25 (t = 1.00 s): four video tokens of the frame at 1 s and one audio token from the same moment. Rotary positions with the same temporal id make "what was heard while this was seen" easy for attention to find.

Idea 4 · VAD and turn-taking

Voice activity detection runs on the input side. Two patterns:

  • Half-duplex: user speaks, model listens. Model speaks, user listens. Clear handoff via VAD silence detection (~200ms).
  • Full-duplex: both can speak simultaneously. Model can backchannel ("uh-huh") or interrupt. Much harder. Moshi supports this.

Qwen2.5-Omni streams its input and output in chunks but works turn by turn; turn-taking is handled by the application (for example, voice activity detection with a silence threshold). Full-duplex behaviour requires application-layer handling or a model trained for it, like Moshi.

Idea 5 · Qwen3-Omni (November 2025)

The successor (technical report September 2025). Thinker: a mixture-of-experts model, 30B parameters with about 3B active; Talker: also mixture-of-experts (3B, about 0.3B active). The Talker predicts each frame's first codebook, a small multi-token-prediction module (about 80M) predicts the rest, and a lightweight causal ConvNet (Code2Wav, about 200M) replaces the DiT vocoder. A new audio encoder (AuT) is trained from scratch on about 20 million hours. Reported theoretical first-packet latency: 234 ms for audio. It supports 119 text languages, 19 for speech input and 10 for speech output. It reports open-source state of the art on 32 of 36 audio and audio-visual benchmarks, close to or ahead of Gemini 2.5 Pro on several (MMAU 77.5).

Idea 6 · Production latency budget

For a typical streaming interaction:

  • Mic -> audio tokens: 40-80ms.
  • Prefill (prompt + history): 100-200ms at 7B, much more at 70B.
  • First Thinker text token: 40ms.
  • Talker processes first text token: 20ms.
  • First speech tokens commit: 40ms.
  • Residual-VQ decode: 30ms.
  • Speech waveform decode: 50-80ms.

Total TTFAB: 320-490ms at 7B, 600-900ms at 70B. Frontier quality usually means 70B+; hence the frontier latency gap.

Idea 7 · Token-rate math

At 50 speech frames per second, the Talker must produce at least 50 frames per second of output to keep up, every frame, for the whole reply. A small Talker does this easily; a 7B Talker generating every frame token by token would fall behind (simulated below). Lower frame rates (12.5 Hz in Moshi's Mimi codec) and predicting the extra codebooks in parallel make the budget easier.

This is why small dedicated Talker models exist rather than "just use the main model."

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Qwen3-Omni (September 2025): open MoE thinker (30B-A3B) and talker (3B-A0.3B), 234 ms theoretical first-packet latency, 119 text languages, state of the art among open models on most audio benchmarks.
  • Full-duplex speech: Moshi listens and speaks at once (about 200 ms); hosted voice modes (GPT-4o-class realtime APIs, Gemini Live) set the product bar.
  • Codec design drives latency: low frame rates (Mimi at 12.5 Hz) and parallel prediction of residual codebooks keep the talker ahead of real time.
  • Laptop reality: Qwen2.5-Omni-3B and 7B need quantization on an 8 GB GPU, and real-time speech is hard there; the Qwen3-Omni MoE (30B total) needs a large GPU even though only 3B are active.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson

Solutions

SolutionP1 · The slow talker

The slow talker, by far. A thinker 2.4 times slower only moves first audio from 228 to 298 ms and keeps a real-time factor of 0.25, because each text token produces 240 ms of speech. A talker at 90 ms per 80 ms frame raises first audio to 555 ms and falls behind real time (factor 1.12): 92 of 120 frames arrive late, so the voice stutters. Speech must be produced faster than it plays, frame after frame.

SolutionB1 · Every turn re-reads twenty minutes

Twenty minutes of camera frames and audio is tens of thousands of tokens at least: audio alone at 25 tokens per second is 30,000, before any frames. Every turn re-runs prefill over all of it unless the prefix is cached perfectly. So the time to first audio grows from a fraction of a second to seconds, as the 900 ms prefill case in the simulation already shows. Memory for the KV cache grows too, and quality can drop as the relevant moment is buried in a long context (Lesson 11.5). Instead, keep a rolling window of recent audio and frames, plus a running text summary and a memory of key facts. Retrieve older moments only when a question needs them (Lesson 12.18), and cache the stable part of the prompt.
