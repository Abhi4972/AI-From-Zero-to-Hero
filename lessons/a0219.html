In plain words first
The idea in one line

A model is one part of a system. The design decides what the model predicts, who acts on it, what it costs, and what happens when it fails.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it means
ML task "Build a churn model" A prediction target, an entity, a horizon, and the decision that uses the prediction
Offline metric "The model score" A metric computed on held-out past data before launch
Online metric "Live numbers" A metric measured on real traffic while the model runs
Business metric "The goal" The company outcome that the decision must change, measured with an experiment
Guardrail metric "Do no harm" A metric that must not get worse when the new model launches
Baseline "The dumb model" A trivial, heuristic, or current-system predictor that the model must beat by a set margin
Batch inference "Precomputed scores" A scheduled job scores every entity and stores the results for lookup
Online inference "Real-time model" The model scores each request when it arrives
Shadow deployment "Dark launch" The new model scores live traffic, and nobody uses its output
Canary release "Slow rollout" The new model decides for a small, growing share of traffic, with a rollback ready
Feedback loop "The model trains itself" The model's decisions change the data that trains its next version

The problem

In plain words first

Before training anything, write down what the model predicts, who acts on the prediction, how you will know the action got better, which simple rule it has to beat, how fresh and fast the predictions must be, and what happens when something breaks. Those decisions, not the algorithm, decide whether a model gets used. This lesson turns each one into a number or a checklist item you can test.

A subscription company asks for "a churn model". A data scientist trains a gradient-boosted classifier and reports an AUC of 0.91. Three months later, nobody uses it.

The reasons have nothing to do with the algorithm. Nobody decided which action a score starts. The scores arrive once a week, but the retention team calls customers every day. The label came from a cancellations table that also stored the cancellation reason, so one feature contained the answer. Nobody compared the model with the rule the support team already used: call anyone idle for two weeks.

Each of these failures happened before training started. They are design failures, and a one-page design document asks about each of them. This lesson teaches the decisions in that document. You build a toolkit that prices batch and online serving, compares a model with its baselines, and scores a design document against a checklist.

Idea 1 · From a business goal to an ML task

A business goal is not an ML task. You turn it into one with four questions:

  1. What exactly does the model predict, and for which entity (user, payment, store, item)?
  2. Which decision uses the prediction, and who or what makes that decision?
  3. When is the prediction needed, and which data exists at that moment?
  4. How will you know that the decision got better?
Business goal Prediction Decision that uses it ML task
Reduce churn Probability that a user cancels in the next 30 days Send a save offer to the top 5 percent each night Binary classification
Cut fraud losses Probability that a payment is fraud Approve, review, or block within 100 ms Binary classification
Keep shelves stocked Units sold per store per day next week Order quantity for each store Forecasting
Show relevant items Relevance score for a (user, item) pair Order of items in the feed Ranking

The second column needs the most care. "Predict churn" leaves the horizon, the entity, and the action open. "Predict whether this user cancels in the next 30 days, so that tonight's job can pick who gets an offer" fixes all three.

Idea 2 · When not to use ML

Rule #1 in Google's Rules of Machine Learning says not to be afraid to launch a product without machine learning. Machine learning needs data, and a simple heuristic often gets you part of the way. Do not use ML in these cases:

  • A short rule reaches the goal and stays correct. A rule is cheaper to run, test, and explain.
  • You have no labels and no practical way to collect them.
  • Nobody acts on the prediction, so a better prediction changes nothing.
  • The data that exists at prediction time does not contain the signal.
  • One error is unacceptable and no person can check the output.
  • Building and running the system costs more than the better decisions are worth.

When a rule gets long and fragile, ML starts to pay off. Rule #3 of the same guide prefers ML over a complex heuristic, because a learned model is easier to update and maintain.

Idea 3 · Three layers of metrics

You measure an ML system at three layers, plus a set of limits:

Layer When you can measure it Churn example
Offline metric Before launch, on held-out past data F1 at the offer threshold, precision in the top 5 percent
Online metric During launch, on live traffic Offer acceptance rate, share of users scored, score distribution
Business metric Weeks after launch, with an experiment 30-day churn rate in the treated group versus a holdout group
Guardrail metric All the time Offer cost per saved user, unsubscribe rate, p99 latency

The layers often disagree. An offline metric scores past data, and the past did not contain your model. An online metric depends on how people react to the decision. A business metric moves slowly and carries noise from everything else in the company.

Pick the offline metric that matches the decision. If the job contacts the top 5 percent of users, measure precision in the top 5 percent. AUC averages over every threshold, including thresholds that you never use. Rule #2 of the same guide says to design and implement metrics first, before you decide what the ML system will do.

Idea 4 · Baselines

A metric value means nothing alone. An F1 of 0.56 is good if the best alternative reaches 0.30 and poor if a rule reaches 0.60. Compare every model with three kinds of baseline:

  • Trivial baseline. Always predict the majority class, or always predict the training mean. We used this check in the model evaluation lesson.
  • Heuristic baseline. A rule from domain knowledge, or a one-feature threshold that you fit on the training data.
  • Current system. Whatever makes the decision today, even when it is a person with a spreadsheet.

The lift is the model score minus the score of the strongest baseline. Set a minimum lift before you look at the results, and make it large enough to pay for the extra system. The trivial baseline is usually weak. The heuristic baseline is the hard one to beat.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "An AUC of 0.91 means the model is ready."
    ✓ AUC averages over every threshold. If the job contacts the top 5 percent, measure precision in the top 5 percent, and compare it with a rule on the same metric.
  • ✗ "Beating the majority-class baseline proves the model works."
    ✓ On F1 the majority baseline scores 0 because it never predicts churn, so anything beats it. The fitted one-feature rule (0.500 in Step 5) is the real competitor.
  • ✗ "A better offline score means a better business result."
    ✓ Offline data never contained your model's decisions. Only an experiment with a holdout group measures the business metric.

Idea 5 · Data and label requirements

Answer these questions before you train:

  • Label source. Which table or event defines the label, and who owns it?
  • Label delay. How long after the prediction does the true outcome arrive? Fraud chargebacks can arrive weeks after the payment. Churn labels arrive at the end of the 30-day horizon.
  • Label quality. Who assigns the label, and how often is it wrong?
  • Availability at prediction time. Does every feature exist at the moment of the prediction? A feature that the system writes after the outcome leaks the label into training.
  • Volume per class. How many positive examples exist? Two hundred positives limit you to simple models.
  • Time split. Hold out the most recent period. We split data by time in the time-series lesson for the same reason.

Idea 6 · Latency, throughput, and cost budgets

Three numbers bound every serving design:

  • Latency budget. The p99 latency is the time that 99 of 100 requests stay under. A payment check can need 50 ms. A nightly report can take hours.
  • Peak throughput. Size the system for the busiest minute, not for the daily average. Peak QPS is often several times the average.
  • Cost budget. Express it as dollars per day or dollars per 1000 predictions, so that you can compare designs.

Little's law connects the first two. The number of requests in progress equals throughput times latency. At 400 requests per second and 50 ms each, 20 requests are in progress at any moment.

Worked example
Pricing the feed ranking by hand

The feed has 5,000,000 items to score, 2,000,000 requests a day, a peak of 120 requests per second and a freshness limit of 2 hours. With this lesson's prices:

  • Batch. A run takes 1 hour, so runs must start every 2 − 1 = 1 hour: 24 runs a day, 5,000,000 × 24 = 120 million predictions. At $0.40 per million that is $48.00, plus 2 million lookups at $0.25 per million, $0.50: $48.50 a day.
  • Online. 120 ÷ 200 requests per replica rounds up to 1, but the minimum is 2 replicas for availability: 2 × $0.50 × 24 hours = $24.00 a day.

Online wins, because the short freshness limit forces 24 full re-scorings a day. Little's law gives the load on those replicas: 120 requests per second × 0.035 s = about 4 requests in progress at the peak.

Idea 7 · Batch inference versus online inference

There are two basic ways to serve predictions:

  • Batch inference. A scheduled job scores every entity and writes the scores to a table or a key-value store. The application reads the stored score at request time.
  • Online inference. The application sends the features to a model service at request time and waits for the score.
Property Batch Online
Freshness As old as the last run, plus the run time Uses the data of the request
Request latency One key-value read, a few ms Feature fetch plus model time
Cost driver Number of entities times runs per day Replicas sized for peak QPS
New entities No score until the next run Scored on the first request
When it breaks Old scores stay readable Requests fail or time out

The toolkit in this lesson prices both. Batch cost grows with the number of entities and with the number of runs per day. A shorter staleness limit means more runs. Online cost grows with peak traffic, because you pay for idle replicas between peaks. A batch job that takes one hour cannot meet a staleness limit under one hour at any price.

A hybrid design is common. A nightly batch job scores known users, and an online path scores new users and requests that need fresh data.

Idea 8 · Deployment patterns

A new model reaches users in stages:

  1. Shadow. The new model receives a copy of live traffic. The system logs its predictions and never uses them. You compare latency, error rate, and prediction distribution with the current model.
  2. Canary. The new model makes real decisions for a small share of traffic, often 1 to 5 percent. You widen the share in steps and roll back when a guardrail breaks.
  3. A/B test. A random split of users sees each model. You measure the business metric with a statistical test, and you need enough traffic and enough time to see the difference.

Every stage needs a rollback that takes minutes. Keep the old model version deployed and switch traffic with configuration. The LLM serving phase covers the same three patterns for language models in the shadow and canary lesson and the A/B testing lesson.

Idea 9 · Feedback loops

A feedback loop exists when the model's decisions change the data that trains its next version. Sculley et al. list direct and hidden feedback loops among the main sources of technical debt in ML systems. Three common cases:

  • Interventions hide the label. A churn model sends offers, and the offers keep some users. Next month, high-risk users look loyal, and the retrained model learns that their features do not predict churn.
  • Blocked actions have no outcome. A fraud model blocks a payment, so that payment never gets a chargeback label. Retrained models see fewer fraud examples from the blocked region.
  • Exposure limits feedback. A ranking model shows its top items, so only those items collect clicks.

The fixes share one idea: keep some data that the model did not influence. Hold out a small random group that never gets the intervention. Give a small share of traffic a random decision. Log the score and the decision with each example, so that later training can tell influenced data from clean data.

Worked example
Why the holdout matters

Suppose 1,000 users a month would churn without help and the save offer keeps 30 percent of those who get it. If every high-risk user gets the offer, next month's data shows only 700 churners among them, and the high-risk features look less predictive than they are. Retrain on that data and the model gets worse at finding the very users the offer saved. A 5 percent random holdout that never gets offers keeps a clean sample: there the true churn rate of high-risk users stays visible, and comparing holdout with treated users also measures the offer's real effect.

Idea 10 · Failure modes and safe defaults

Every part of the system fails sooner or later. Decide the default action for each failure in the design, not during the incident:

Failure Result without a plan Safe default
Model service times out The request fails Use the heuristic baseline
Nightly batch job fails Scores go missing or get old Serve the last good scores for a fixed time, then the rule
Feature is missing or out of range The model scores bad input Use the documented default value and log the event
New entity with no history No score exists Use the rule or the population average
Score distribution moves suddenly Wrong decisions at scale Pause automatic actions and alert a person

The heuristic baseline from your metric section is also the default for most failures, so build it first.

Idea 11 · The design document

The design document records every decision above in one place, before training starts. The checker in this lesson scores a JSON version of it. Six items block a review, because each one makes the system unsafe or useless:

Section Question it answers Blocking
Problem What is predicted, which decision uses it, and why rules are not enough? The decision
Metrics Which offline, online, business, and guardrail metrics apply? The business metric
Baselines Which trivial, heuristic, and current-system baselines apply? A heuristic or current baseline
Data Where do labels come from, how late, and does each feature exist at prediction time? No leakage
Budgets and serving Which latency, peak QPS, cost, and staleness limits apply, and which serving mode fits them? No
Rollout Which shadow, canary, and A/B stages apply, and how do you roll back? Shadow or canary, plus rollback
Feedback loops How can the model change its own future data, and what keeps clean data? No
Failure modes What is the default action for each failure? A default for every failure
Monitoring What do you watch after launch? No

The same idea as a formula

Little's law. In a steady system, the average number of requests in progress equals throughput times latency:

L=λWe.g. 400 requests/s×0.05 s=20 in progress

Sizing for the peak. A replica that handles μ requests per second should run at a utilisation u below 1 (queues explode as u→1), so the peak rate λpeak​ needs

replicas=⌈uμλpeak​​⌉,cost per 1,000 predictions=3600×uμ1000×price per replica-hour​

You pay for the peak all day; batch inference instead pays per run, and its cost grows with the number of entities times runs per day.

Value of a model. For a decision with a benefit B per true positive and a cost C per false positive, a model is worth B⋅TP−C⋅FP per period minus its running cost; a baseline (a rule, or "do nothing") has the same formula, and the model must beat the baseline's value, not just its accuracy.

Tail latency. The p99 is the latency 99% of requests stay under. If a page waits for k independent backend calls, it is slower than its p99 with probability 1−0.99k: 26% for k=30.

Step 1 · Describe the workload and the prices

The code in code/main.py uses only the standard library. It has three tools: a serving cost model, a baseline comparator, and a design-document checker.

A workload holds the traffic numbers and the budgets. A cost model holds the prices. The prices are round numbers for the exercise, not vendor quotes.

@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Workload:
    name: str
    entities: int
    requests_per_day: int
    peak_qps: float
    max_staleness_hours: float
    latency_budget_ms: float


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class CostModel:
    batch_dollars_per_million: float = 0.40
    batch_job_hours: float = 1.0
    lookup_dollars_per_million: float = 0.25
    lookup_latency_ms: float = 4.0
    replica_dollars_per_hour: float = 0.50
    replica_qps: float = 200.0
    min_replicas: int = 2
    online_latency_ms: float = 35.0

Step 2 · Price batch and online serving

A batch job must finish inside the staleness limit. The time between runs is the limit minus the job time. Each run scores every entity, whether or not anyone asks for that score.

def batch_plan(workload: Workload, costs: CostModel) -> ServingPlan:
    refresh_hours = workload.max_staleness_hours - costs.batch_job_hours
    if refresh_hours <= 0:
        return ServingPlan(
            "batch", False, math.inf, costs.lookup_latency_ms, math.inf, 0.0,
            "staleness limit is shorter than one batch job",
        )
    runs_per_day = 24.0 / refresh_hours
    predictions = workload.entities * runs_per_day
    dollars = (
        predictions / 1e6 * costs.batch_dollars_per_million
        + workload.requests_per_day / 1e6 * costs.lookup_dollars_per_million
    )
    feasible = costs.lookup_latency_ms <= workload.latency_budget_ms
    reason = f"{runs_per_day:.1f} runs per day over {workload.entities:,} entities"
    if not feasible:
        reason = "key-value lookup is slower than the latency budget"
    return ServingPlan(
        "batch", feasible, dollars, costs.lookup_latency_ms,
        workload.max_staleness_hours, predictions, reason,
    )

Online serving pays for enough replicas to handle the peak, with a minimum of two for availability:

def online_plan(workload: Workload, costs: CostModel) -> ServingPlan:
    replicas = max(costs.min_replicas, math.ceil(workload.peak_qps / costs.replica_qps))
    dollars = replicas * costs.replica_dollars_per_hour * 24.0
    feasible = costs.online_latency_ms <= workload.latency_budget_ms
    reason = f"{replicas} replicas sized for {workload.peak_qps:g} peak QPS"
    if not feasible:
        reason = "model latency is slower than the latency budget"
    return ServingPlan(
        "online", feasible, dollars, costs.online_latency_ms, 0.0,
        float(workload.requests_per_day), reason,
    )

choose_serving keeps the plans that meet the latency and staleness limits and returns the cheapest one. It returns None when no plan fits, which tells you to change the budgets.

Step 3 · Compare a model with its baselines

The comparator scores the model and every baseline with the same metric. It measures the lift against the strongest baseline, not the weakest.

def compare_to_baselines(
    y_true: list,
    model_pred: list,
    baselines: dict[str, list],
    metric,
    metric_name: str,
    higher_is_better: bool = True,
    min_lift: float = 0.02,
) -> BaselineReport:
    if not baselines:
        raise ValueError("a model needs at least one baseline")
    scores = {name: metric(y_true, pred) for name, pred in baselines.items()}
    pick = max if higher_is_better else min
    best_name = pick(scores, key=scores.get)
    model_score = metric(y_true, model_pred)
    lift = model_score - scores[best_name] if higher_is_better else scores[best_name] - model_score
    return BaselineReport(metric_name, {"model": model_score, **scores}, best_name, lift, min_lift)

The heuristic baseline is a one-feature rule. fit_rule tries each value of the feature as a threshold on the training data and keeps the threshold with the best F1. The demo trains a small logistic regression on synthetic churn data and compares it with three baselines.

Step 4 · Score a design document

Each checklist item has a key, a weight, a blocking flag, a message, and a check function. A blocking item has weight 2.

CheckItem("decision", 2, True, "name the action that uses each prediction", lambda d: _text(d, "problem.decision")),
CheckItem("baselines", 2, True, "add a heuristic or current-system baseline", check_baselines),
CheckItem("no_leakage", 2, True, "confirm every feature exists at prediction time",
          lambda d: get_path(d, "data.features_available_at_prediction_time") is True),
CheckItem("serving_fit", 1, False, "pick a serving mode that meets the staleness, latency, and cost budgets",
          check_serving_fit),

check_serving_fit reuses the cost model. It reads the workload numbers from the document and prices the chosen mode. The check fails when that mode misses the staleness limit, the latency budget, or the daily cost budget. A document that says "batch" with a three-minute staleness limit fails this check. The cost model has no hybrid plan, so a document that says "hybrid" fails this check until you add that plan in Exercise 1.

review_design_doc adds the weights of the passed items and divides by the total. The verdict is "ready for review" only when the score reaches 0.85 and no blocking item fails.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

Each part of the toolkit maps to tools that teams use in production.

Baselines. scikit-learn provides the trivial baselines as estimators, so they fit into the same pipeline as the model:

from sklearn.dummy import DummyClassifier, DummyRegressor
from sklearn.metrics import f1_score

train_rows, train_y = make_churn_data(2000, seed=1)       # the lesson's synthetic churn data
test_rows, test_y = make_churn_data(1000, seed=2)
X_train = [[r[k] for k in FEATURES] for r in train_rows]
X_test = [[r[k] for k in FEATURES] for r in test_rows]
y_train, y_test = train_y, test_y
y_reg_train = [r["tenure_months"] for r in train_rows]    # any numeric target works for the mean baseline

majority = DummyClassifier(strategy="most_frequent").fit(X_train, y_train)
base_rate = DummyClassifier(strategy="stratified", random_state=0).fit(X_train, y_train)
mean_value = DummyRegressor(strategy="mean").fit(X_train, y_reg_train)

print(f1_score(y_test, majority.predict(X_test)))
print(round(f1_score(y_test, base_rate.predict(X_test)), 3))
print(round(mean_value.predict(X_test[:1])[0], 2))

The majority baseline's F1 is 0.0, as in the lesson's own comparator. The stratified baseline guesses churn at the training base rate and lands near the from-scratch "random at base rate" score (the exact value depends on the random draws). The mean baseline predicts the training mean, about 29.6 months of tenure, for every row.

most_frequent matches majority_baseline, and stratified matches base_rate_baseline. scikit-learn has no heuristic baseline, because the heuristic comes from your domain.

Batch serving. A workflow scheduler such as Apache Airflow or Dagster runs the scoring job. The job writes scores to a warehouse table or to a key-value store such as Redis or DynamoDB.

Online serving. Model servers such as KServe, BentoML, NVIDIA Triton, and TorchServe wrap a model behind an HTTP or gRPC endpoint. A plain web service with a loaded model also works for small models.

Rollouts. Kubernetes tools such as Argo Rollouts split traffic for canary releases. Experiment platforms such as GrowthBook and Statsig assign users to A/B groups and compute the statistics.

Design review. Breck et al. published the ML Test Score, a rubric of 28 tests and monitoring needs for production ML systems. Our checker is smaller. It asks the design questions that come before those tests.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Turn a goal into a task: entity, prediction, horizon, and the decision that uses it.
  • Measure offline, online and business metrics, plus guardrails; pick the offline metric that matches the decision.
  • Beat the strongest baseline, usually a rule, by a margin set in advance.
  • Price batch and online serving from entities, freshness, peak traffic and latency.
  • Roll out through shadow, canary and A/B stages with a fast rollback, and keep a holdout against feedback loops.
  • Decide a safe default for every failure before launch.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Freshness sets the bill

With a 4-hour limit a run starts every 3 hours: 8 runs a day, 40 million predictions, $16.00 plus $0.50 of lookups, so choose_serving picks batch at $16.50 a day against $24.00 online. The two meet where $48 ÷ (S − 1) + $0.50 = $24, that is S − 1 ≈ 2.04, a freshness limit of about 3.04 hours: at 3 hours batch still costs $24.50, at 3.1 hours $23.36.

SolutionM1 · A stricter bar

The lift is still +0.064, but 0.064 < 0.10, so the verdict becomes "model does not beat the strongest baseline (days_idle > 12.8688)". Set the minimum lift before you look at the results; a bar chosen after seeing them proves nothing.

SolutionB1 · A non-blocking hole

Only serving_fit fails. Its weight is 1 of 24, so the score is 23 ÷ 24 = 0.96 and the verdict is still "ready for review", because serving_fit is not a blocking item. A design that cannot meet its own freshness limit passes the checklist. If your systems depend on it, make the item blocking (set its flag to True and its weight to 2), and add a test with this exact document.

# ML Design Review

Use this skill when someone shares a design document, a proposal, or a plan for a model that will make real decisions. Review the document in the order below. Report the blocking gaps first, then the other gaps, then the questions.

## Step 1: Check the six blocking items

Stop the review and ask for a fix when any of these items is missing:

1. **Decision.** The document names the action that uses each prediction and who or what takes it.
2. **Business metric.** The document names the company outcome that the decision must change and how an experiment measures it.
3. **Strong baseline.** The document compares the model with a heuristic rule or with the current system. A majority-class baseline alone does not count.
4. **No leakage.** The document confirms that every feature exists at the moment of the prediction.
5. **Rollout and rollback.** The plan has a shadow or canary stage and a rollback that takes minutes.
6. **Failure defaults.** Every failure mode has a default action, such as the heuristic rule or the last good score.

## Step 2: Check the problem framing

- The prediction names an entity, a target, and a horizon, such as "user cancels in the next 30 days".
- The document says why a rule or a lookup table is not enough.
- The ML task type matches the decision: classification, regression, ranking, forecasting, or anomaly detection.

## Step 3: Check the metrics

| Layer | Ask |
|---|---|
| Offline | Does the metric match the decision threshold? Use precision in the top 5 percent if the job acts on the top 5 percent. |
| Online | Which live signals show that the model works: acceptance rate, share of requests scored, score distribution? |
| Business | Which experiment measures the outcome, and how long until the result? |
| Guardrails | Which metrics must not get worse: cost, latency, complaints, unsubscribes? |

## Step 4: Check the data and labels

- Where the labels come from and who owns that table
- The label delay in days, and what the team monitors until labels arrive
- The number of positive examples
- A time-based holdout set, with the most recent period held out

## Step 5: Check the serving choice with arithmetic

Ask for five numbers: entities, requests per day, peak QPS, maximum staleness in hours, and the p99 latency budget. Then price both modes:

```text
batch runs per day   = 24 / (max_staleness_hours - batch_job_hours)
batch cost per day   = entities * runs per day * price per prediction + lookups * price per lookup
online replicas      = max(2, ceil(peak_qps / qps per replica))
online cost per day  = replicas * price per replica hour * 24
```

- Batch cannot work when the staleness limit is shorter than one batch job.
- Online cannot work when model latency is above the p99 budget.
- When both work, prefer the cheaper one, and check how many batch scores nobody reads.
- New entities with no history need an online path or a rule.

## Step 6: Check feedback loops

Ask how the model's decisions change its future training data:

- Does an intervention, such as an offer or a block, hide the true outcome?
- Do only the items that the model shows collect feedback?
- Is there a random holdout group or an exploration share that keeps clean data?
- Does the system log the score and the decision with each example?

## Step 7: Write the review

Use this format:

```text
Verdict: ready for review | revise
Blocking: <list, or "none">
Missing: <list of non-blocking gaps>
Serving check: <batch $/day, online $/day, chosen mode, pass or fail>
Questions: <at most five questions for the author>
```

To score a JSON version of the document, run `python3 code/main.py path/to/design_doc.json` from the lesson folder.

Defend a designA new version of a chat prompt looks better in a notebook. Design how it reaches users: the stages, how users are assigned, what you measure at each stage, and when it rolls back automatically.

Level 2 of 4 · about 12 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Run it in shadow first, on all traffic, logging its answers without showing them, and compare quality metrics with the current prompt. Then route a small share of users to it, 10% in the lesson, and widen in steps to 25%, 50% and 100% while the metrics hold. Assign by a deterministic hash of the user id so each user sees one version, and roll back automatically when the new version's error rate reaches twice the old one over a window of requests.

Shadow mode.

The new prompt runs on 100% of traffic but only its logs are kept. Nobody sees its output. This gives a full comparison of latency, cost per request, guardrail block rate and quality scores against the current version, and users never see a shadow answer. Shadow calls can share the provider quota with live calls, so give them a separate quota or key; otherwise a quota limit can slow or fail live requests. It is the same first stage that the ML design lesson calls shadow deployment for a model.

Percentage rollout.

Route 10% of traffic to the new version. If quality holds, raise it to 25%, then 50%, then 100%. If quality drops, switch back at once. Both versions stay deployed and the switch is configuration, so a rollback takes minutes, which is the lesson's rule for every stage.

Deterministic assignment.

Hash the user id combined with the name of the experiment and take it modulo 100. Buckets below the traffic share get the variant, the rest get the control. A user then sees the same version across requests inside one experiment, and the assignment can be recomputed later from the log. Random assignment per request mixes versions inside one conversation and makes the comparison noisy.

What to measure.

Per prompt version: latency percentiles, user ratings where they exist, error rate, guardrail block rate and cost per request. The eval logger records the template name and version with every request, so the metrics split by version without extra plumbing.

Automatic rollback.

The lesson's exercise sets the rule: when a new version shows 2x the error rate of the previous version over 100 requests, revert automatically. The window matters; a handful of requests is noise, and waiting for thousands lets a bad prompt run too long.

A note on the business metric.

Shadow and canary protect the user. Whether the new prompt improves the outcome that matters, such as task completion, needs a randomized split with enough traffic and time, which is the A/B stage from the ML design lesson.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Ship to everyone and watch the dashboard." A regression then hits every user before the dashboard moves.
  • "Assign per request at random for a cleaner statistic." Users see both versions in one conversation and the logged metrics mix them.
  • "Shadow mode is enough; skip the canary." Shadow mode never shows the output to a user, so it cannot measure user reaction.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why hash the user id and not pick randomly per request? Expected: a user then gets one consistent version across a session and the assignment is reproducible from the log.
  • (level 2) What does shadow mode not tell you? Expected: how users react to the new answers; it only compares logged outputs and system metrics.
  • (level 3) The new prompt costs more per request but scores higher. Who decides? Expected: a guardrail on cost per request decides whether it can ship at all; the quality gain against the cost is then a product decision with both numbers on the table.
  • (level 3) How do you keep the control and the variant from sharing a cache entry? Expected: include the prompt version in the cache key, otherwise a hit returns the other version's answer and the comparison is corrupted.

Where it is taught. The production LLM application lesson describes shadow mode, percentage rollout and hashed assignment for prompts, and the ML system design lesson gives the same three stages with the rollback rule for models.

Weigh a trade-offA feed ranking score must be no more than two hours old for 5,000,000 items, and the serving peak is 120 requests per second. Price batch and online serving with the lesson's cost model, pick one, and say what would flip the choice.

Level 2 of 4 · about 12 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. With a one-hour batch job and a two-hour staleness limit, the job must run every hour, which the lesson's cost model prices at 24.0 runs per day over 5,000,000 items for $48.50 a day. Two online replicas sized for 120 peak QPS cost $24.00 a day and serve the freshest data. Online wins here; the choice flips when the freshness limit relaxes enough to cut the runs, or when peak traffic grows enough to need many replicas.

How each plan is priced.

Property Batch Online
Freshness As old as the last run plus the run time The data of the request
Request latency One key-value read, a few milliseconds Feature fetch plus model time, 35 ms in the model
Cost driver Entities times runs per day Replicas sized for peak QPS, minimum of two
New entities No score until the next run Scored on the first request
When it breaks Old scores stay readable Requests fail or time out

Batch must finish inside the staleness limit, so the time between runs is the limit minus the job time. Each run scores every entity whether or not anyone asks. Online pays for idle replicas between peaks, with a floor of two for availability.

The arithmetic for this workload.

The staleness limit of two hours minus a one-hour job leaves one hour between runs, so 24.0 runs per day. Every run scores all 5,000,000 items. That is why the batch plan costs $48.50 a day while the online plan sits at its two-replica floor of $24.00, since 120 peak QPS fits inside the capacity of two replicas.

What flips it.

  • A longer staleness limit cuts runs and batch cost in proportion; the nightly churn job in the same lesson costs under a dollar.
  • A staleness limit shorter than one batch job makes batch infeasible at any price.
  • A much higher peak needs more replicas and raises online cost linearly with peak traffic.
  • A latency budget below the online path's model time rules online out, and a hybrid serves known items from a store and new items online.

What the decision is not about.

Freshness and cost decide; model quality does not change between the two plans. The failure modes differ: a broken batch job leaves old but readable scores, while a broken online service fails requests, so the online design needs a default such as the last stored score or the heuristic.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Batch is always cheaper." Batch cost scales with runs per day times entities; a tight freshness limit makes it the expensive plan.
  • "Online is always fresher, so always better." Fresher costs replicas, and a nightly process cannot use freshness it does not read.
  • "Pick by model accuracy." Accuracy is the same in both plans; the choice is about freshness, latency and cost.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why is the online plan at its minimum here? Expected: two replicas is the availability floor, and 120 peak QPS fits inside what two replicas handle, so traffic does not drive the count.
  • (level 2) The job takes longer than the staleness limit. What does the planner return? Expected: infeasible; no schedule satisfies the limit, so the budgets or the job must change.
  • (level 3) Design the hybrid. Expected: batch scores known items on the relaxed schedule, online scores new items and requests that need fresh data; price it as the sum of a cheaper batch plan and a small online tier.
  • (level 3) Which design document check does this feed? Expected: the serving-fit check, which prices the chosen mode against the staleness, latency and cost budgets and fails a mode that misses any of them.

Where it is taught. The ML system design lesson builds the batch and online cost model and runs it on the feed ranking workload.

Defend a designA subscription company with 2,000,000 users wants to reduce churn. The retention team can send a save offer to a small share of users each night. Design the system end to end: the prediction, the decision, the serving mode, the baselines, the rollout, and the failure defaults. State the load and the budgets you assume.

Level 2 of 4 · about 20 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. The task is binary classification: the probability that a user cancels in the next 30 days, so that the nightly job can pick the top 5 percent for an offer. Nightly scores for every user fit batch inference; the lesson's cost model prices one run a day over the user base at under one dollar, while an online service sized for 40 peak QPS costs $24.00 a day for a freshness nobody needs. The model must beat the idle-days rule by a set lift, ship through shadow and canary stages, and fall back to that rule when anything fails.

From the goal to a task.

Question Answer
What is predicted, for which entity Cancellation in the next 30 days, per user
Which decision uses it The nightly job sends an offer to the top 5 percent
When is it needed, with which data Each night, from data up to that night
How do we know the decision got better 30-day churn in the treated group versus a random holdout

Load and budgets.

Entities: 2,000,000 users. Requests: one score read per user visit. Freshness: a day is fine, because the job runs once a night. Latency budget: one key-value read at request time, a few milliseconds. Cost: the batch plan prices at $0.91 per day for one run over the user base, against $24.00 per day for the minimum two replicas of an online service. A hybrid can score new users online on their first visit, since batch has no score for them until the next run.

Metrics and baselines.

Offline: precision in the top 5 percent. Online: offer acceptance rate and share of users scored. Business: churn in treated versus holdout. Guardrails: offer cost per saved user, unsubscribe rate. Three baselines: majority class, which scores zero on F1 because it never predicts churn; a one-feature idle-days rule fitted on training data; and the current process. In the lesson's demo the model reaches an F1 of 0.564 against the fitted rule at 0.500, a lift of 0.064 above the minimum of 0.02, so the model earns its place. With weaker features the rule would ship instead.

Data.

Labels come from the cancellations table, with a 30-day delay. Every feature must exist at the moment of scoring; a cancellation reason written after the fact leaks the label. Hold out the most recent period for the test split.

Rollout.

Shadow first: score live users, log, never act. Then canary: act for a small share, often 1 to 5 percent, and widen in steps with a rollback switch. Then an A/B test against the holdout for the business metric. Keep the old model deployed and switch traffic by configuration.

Feedback loop and failures.

Offers keep some users, so next month high-risk users look loyal and the retrained model learns that their features do not predict churn. Keep a random holdout that never gets the offer and log the score and the decision with each row. Failure defaults: a failed nightly job serves the last good scores for a fixed time, then the rule; a missing feature takes its documented default and logs; a sudden move in the score distribution pauses automatic offers and alerts a person.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Serve it online so scores are always fresh." The decision runs once a night; online replicas cost more for freshness the process cannot use.
  • "Use AUC as the offline metric." The decision uses one threshold; precision in the contacted share is the metric that matches it.
  • "Retrain monthly on all users." Treated users carry the intervention in their labels; without a holdout the model degrades with each retrain.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why does the majority baseline score zero? Expected: F1 counts positive predictions, and the majority class is "stays", so there are none.
  • (level 2) The retention team wants hourly freshness. What changes? Expected: more runs per day raise batch cost in proportion, and the online path becomes cheaper at some point; price both with the cost model.
  • (level 3) How do you keep the model from learning its own interventions? Expected: a random holdout that never receives offers, plus logging of score and decision so training can separate influenced from clean rows.
  • (level 3) What is the safe default when the model service is down during the nightly run? Expected: serve the last good scores for a fixed window, then fall back to the idle-days rule.

Walk me through your review: the sections you expect, which gaps block, how you score it, and what you tell the team.

Level 4 of 4 · about 20 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. The document needs nine sections, and six items block a review because each one makes the system unsafe or useless: the decision that uses the prediction, a business metric, a heuristic or current-system baseline, confirmation that every feature exists at prediction time, a shadow or canary stage with a rollback, and a default action for every failure. This document fails at least three of them: the only baseline is trivial, there is no rollout and no rollback, and the serving choice fails the fit check because batch cannot meet a limit of three minutes when one job takes an hour. I send it back with the blocking list first, and I ask for precision at the decision threshold instead of AUC.

Scoring.

Each checklist item has a weight, a blocking flag and a check. Blocking items weigh 2. The score is the sum of passed weights over the total, and the verdict is ready for review only when the score reaches 0.85 and no blocking item fails. A document can score high on the non-blocking items and still be sent back on one blocker; that is the point of the flag.

The serving fit check.

This check reuses the cost model. It reads the workload numbers from the document, prices the chosen mode, and fails when that mode misses the staleness limit, the latency budget or the daily cost budget. A batch job that takes an hour cannot meet a limit of three minutes at any price, so a document that says batch with that limit fails the check. For a payment decision made at request time the fit is online, and the document must state the peak QPS and the p99 budget that size it.

What I tell the team.

  1. Blockers first: name the action that each score starts; add the rule the fraud team uses today as the heuristic baseline and set the minimum lift; add shadow and canary stages with a configuration rollback; write a default for a timeout, a missing feature, a new card and a sudden score shift.
  2. Then the serving section: switch to online, give the budgets, price it.
  3. Then the metric: AUC averages thresholds nobody uses; report precision and recall at the approve, review and block thresholds, and name the business metric and the guardrails.
  4. Last the feedback loop and the monitoring, which are not blocking but will decide the second quarter.

Why the review happens before training.

Every failure in the churn story at the start of the design lesson happened before training: no decision, stale cadence, a leaked feature, no comparison with the existing rule. A one-page document asks about each, and the review costs a day where the failures cost a quarter.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "A strong offline metric compensates for a missing rollout." A model with no shadow stage and no rollback reaches users with no way back; that is why rollout blocks.
  • "Score the document and pass it at a high enough total." One failed blocker fails the review regardless of the total.
  • "Serving mode is an implementation detail for later." The fit check shows batch cannot meet the limit at all, which changes the architecture and the cost.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) The team says the majority baseline is enough since it scores zero on F1. Why is that the wrong baseline? Expected: it is the trivial one and usually weak; the heuristic or current system is the real competitor and the one the lift is measured against.
  • (level 4) Why does the serving fit check belong in a document review rather than in load testing? Expected: it is arithmetic on stated budgets and prices, so it catches an infeasible design before a line of serving code exists.
  • (level 4) The document proposes a hybrid serving mode. What does the checker do and what do you do? Expected: the lesson's cost model has no hybrid plan, so the check fails until a hybrid plan is added; ask the team to price the batch and the online parts and add the plan.
  • (level 4) What is the one non-blocking section you would still refuse to skip for a fraud system? Expected: feedback loops, because blocked payments never get a label and the model drifts toward its own decisions without a clean sample.

Defend a designThree systems retrain monthly on their own production data: a churn model that sends save offers, a fraud model that blocks payments, and a ranking model that orders a feed. Each one gets a little worse after every retrain. Explain the shared cause, design the data layer that prevents it, and say what each training row must carry for the fix to work.

Level 4 of 4 · about 15 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Each model's decisions change the data that trains its next version, which is a feedback loop. Offers keep users who would have churned, so high-risk users look loyal; blocked payments never get a chargeback, so fraud disappears from the blocked region; the feed shows only its top items, so only those collect clicks. The fix is to keep data the model did not influence: a random holdout that never receives the intervention, a small share of random decisions, and a log that stores the score and the decision with every row, joined to features as of the decision time so training can separate influenced from clean data.

Three shapes of the same loop.

System How the decision hides the label What the retrain learns
Churn offers Offers retain some at-risk users Their features do not predict churn
Fraud blocks A blocked payment has no outcome The blocked region looks safe
Feed ranking Only shown items can be clicked The current top items are the only good ones

The loop is listed among the main sources of technical debt in production ML systems because it is silent: every retrain looks fine on its own held-out data, which is also influenced.

The data layer.

  1. A random holdout group that never receives the intervention. Its outcomes are the clean estimate of the base rate and the clean labels for training.
  2. A small share of random decisions on live traffic, for example payments that are not blocked or items shown out of order, so the blocked and unshown regions keep producing labels. The share is sized as the smallest that keeps enough labels from those regions.
  3. Logging of the score, the decision and the assignment with every row at decision time.
  4. Features joined as of the decision time, with the point-in-time rule from the feature store, so that a row's inputs are what the model saw and not a later snapshot that already reflects the outcome.

What a training row carries.

The entity and the decision time, the features as of that time, the model score, the decision taken, the group it belonged to, the label and the label time. With those fields training can select clean rows, weight influenced rows, or model the decision as a feature. Without the decision and the group, influenced and clean rows are indistinguishable and the loop is invisible.

Why the feature store is part of it.

A naive join on the entity returns the newest feature snapshot for every label, which copies the outcome into the input. The same mistake inside a feedback loop is worse: the newest snapshot also reflects the intervention. The as-of join with a TTL makes each row describe the moment of the decision, and the created timestamp check keeps a backfill from reading data that arrived later.

Measuring the fix.

The holdout gives the business metric: the outcome in the treated group against the holdout group. It also gives the retrain test: evaluate the new model on holdout rows only, so the score is not inflated by the model's own decisions. A model that improves on influenced data and not on the holdout has learned the loop.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "More training data fixes a drifting model." The new data is influenced; more of it entrenches the loop.
  • "A random train and test split shows the real performance." Both halves are influenced; only a holdout that never saw the intervention is clean.
  • "Log the decision later from the outcome table." The decision and the assignment must be recorded at decision time, with the features as of that time, or the join leaks the outcome.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) Why is a time split alone not enough here? Expected: the recent period is also influenced by the deployed model; time ordering prevents future leakage but not intervention bias.
  • (level 4) The random non-blocking share costs real fraud. How do you argue for it? Expected: as the price of clean labels against the cost of a model that drifts blind; size it to the minimum that keeps enough labeled fraud from the blocked region.
  • (level 4) How does the exposure loop in ranking differ from the other two? Expected: the label is never collected rather than hidden; random exposure of lower-ranked items is the only way to learn their click rate.
  • (level 4) A retrain evaluated on the holdout is worse than on the full set. Which number do you trust? Expected: the holdout; the full set rewards the model for agreeing with its own past decisions.

Where it is taught. The ML system design lesson names the three feedback loops and the shared fix, and the feature store lesson gives the as-of join that keeps each training row at the decision time.

Defend a designA payment service must approve, review or block each payment within 100 ms, at 400 requests per second at peak. Fraud labels arrive as chargebacks weeks later. Design the system: the task, the serving mode and its sizing, the data and label plan, the rollout, the failure defaults, and the two things that will quietly break it after launch.

Level 3 of 4 · about 20 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. The task is binary classification of fraud probability with two thresholds that split the score into approve, review and block, served online because the decision needs the data of the request. At 400 requests a second and 50 ms per request about 20 requests are in flight at any moment, so the service is small, and the budget leaves room for a feature fetch plus the model. Chargeback labels come weeks after the payment, so training uses a time split and features that exist at payment time, and the rollout goes shadow, then canary on a small share, with the heuristic rule as the default on timeout. The two quiet failures are the feedback loop, where blocked payments never get a label, and concept drift, where the same basket changes its fraud risk and only outcome monitors see it.

Task and decision.

Question Answer
Prediction, per entity Probability that this payment is fraud
Decision Approve, send to review, or block, inside 100 ms
When, with what data At the request, with the payment, the card history and the device
Success Fraud losses down with the approval rate as a guardrail

Serving and sizing.

Batch cannot serve this: the staleness limit is zero because the payment did not exist a minute ago. Online inference fetches features and runs the model inside the budget. Little's law sizes it: throughput times latency is the number in progress, so 400 requests a second at 50 ms each is 20 requests in flight, far below what two replicas handle. The p99, not the mean, must stay under the budget; a p99 of 100 ms means 99 of 100 requests finish in time.

Data and labels.

Chargebacks arrive weeks after the payment, so the outcome of recent traffic is unknown. Train on a time split with the most recent labeled period held out. Every feature must exist at payment time; anything written after the outcome, such as a dispute flag, leaks the label.

Rollout.

Shadow: score live payments and log, never act; compare latency, error rate and score distribution with the current rule. Canary: act on a small share, often 1 to 5 percent, widen in steps, roll back by configuration when a guardrail moves. Then measure losses against a holdout.

Failure defaults.

  • Model times out: apply the heuristic baseline the team already uses.
  • A feature is missing or out of range: use its documented default and log it.
  • A new card with no history: the rule or the population average.
  • The score distribution moves suddenly: pause automatic blocks and page a person.

The two quiet failures.

  1. The feedback loop. A blocked payment never gets a chargeback, so the retrained model sees fewer fraud examples from the region it blocks and slowly learns that region is safe. Give a small random share of payments a non-blocking decision and log the score and the action with every row, so later training can tell influenced data from clean data.
  2. Concept drift. Fraudsters change tactics, and payments that were safe start to charge back. Inputs look the same, so input monitors stay quiet; only the outcome monitor sees it, and only after the label delay. Prior drift, such as the fraud rate doubling during an attack, shows up earlier in the prediction monitor as a moving positive rate.

Monitoring.

System health at once, inputs and predictions at once, outcomes joined as chargebacks arrive and computed on the labeled part only. Alert policy with effect sizes, a corrected alpha and persistence, so the on-call person reads the alerts.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Precompute fraud scores nightly for every card." The payment did not exist last night; the decision needs request-time data.
  • "Retrain weekly on all blocked and approved payments." Blocked payments have no outcome, so the training set drifts toward the model's own decisions.
  • "Watch feature drift and you will see the fraudsters change." Concept drift leaves inputs unchanged; only outcomes show it.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) Why does a time split matter more here than a random split? Expected: a random split leaks future tactics into training and overstates performance on the next month's fraud.
  • (level 3) The review queue grows after launch. Which guardrail caught it and what do you adjust? Expected: the review rate guardrail; move the review threshold, not the model.
  • (level 4) The random non-blocking share costs real fraud losses. How do you size it? Expected: as the smallest share that keeps enough labeled fraud examples from the blocked region to retrain; price it as the cost of clean data against the cost of a model that drifts blind.
  • (level 4) Retraining on the last quarter's labels after a tactic change makes the model worse. Why? Expected: concept drift; most of the labels still describe the old relation, so new labels must be collected before retraining helps.

Explain the mechanismA data scientist reports a churn classifier with an AUC of 0.91. Three months after launch, nobody on the retention team uses the scores. The algorithm was fine. Name the design decisions that were missing, and say which metric should have been reported instead.

Level 1 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. The failures happened before training: nobody named the action a score starts, the scores arrived weekly while the team called customers daily, a feature leaked the label through the cancellation reason, and nobody compared the model with the rule the team already used. AUC averages over every threshold including ones never used; the decision was to contact the top 5 percent each night, so the right offline number was precision in that top 5 percent, paired with online, business and guardrail metrics.

Four layers of metrics.

Layer When measured Churn example
Offline Before launch, on held-out past data Precision in the top 5 percent at the offer threshold
Online During launch, on live traffic Offer acceptance rate, share of users scored
Business Weeks later, with an experiment 30-day churn in the treated group versus a holdout
Guardrail All the time Offer cost per saved user, unsubscribe rate, p99 latency

The layers disagree often. The offline score comes from a past that did not contain the model. The online metric depends on how people react to the decision. The business metric moves slowly and carries noise from everything else in the company. The guardrails must not get worse.

Pick the offline metric from the decision.

The job contacts a fixed share of users, so the metric is the precision of that share. AUC ranks every pair of users, most of which never reach the decision. The rules guide quoted in the lesson puts it first: design the metrics before deciding what the system does.

Baselines.

An F1 of 0.56 is good against a best alternative of 0.30 and poor against a rule that reaches 0.60. Three baselines are needed: the trivial one, a heuristic such as the idle-days rule the support team used, and the current system. The lift is measured against the strongest one, and a minimum lift is set before the results are seen.

The other three failures.

  • Freshness: weekly scores for a daily process means the team acted on stale numbers.
  • Leakage: a feature written after the outcome contained the answer, so the offline score was inflated.
  • Decision: a score with no action attached cannot change a business metric.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "A higher AUC would have fixed adoption." Adoption failed on freshness, action and trust, not on ranking quality.
  • "Business metrics are enough; skip the offline one." The business metric arrives weeks later and is noisy; the offline metric gates the launch.
  • "Guardrails are a product concern, not a model concern." A model that moves a guardrail the wrong way is rolled back no matter its offline score.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 1) Why is a 30-day churn label slow to evaluate? Expected: the label arrives at the end of the horizon, so the business metric lags the launch by at least that long.
  • (level 2) Which guardrail would catch a model that saves users at any price? Expected: offer cost per saved user.
  • (level 2) The heuristic baseline beats the model by a small margin. What ships? Expected: the rule, because it is cheaper to run and explain, unless the model clears the minimum lift.
