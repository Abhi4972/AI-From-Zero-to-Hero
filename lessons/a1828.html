In plain words first
The idea in one line

Five organisations define the 2026 non-lab alignment research layer. MATS (ML Alignment & Theory Scholars): 527+ researchers since late 2021, 180+ papers, 10K+ citations, h-index 47; summer 2024 cohort incorporated as 501(c)(3) with ~90 scholars and 40 mentors; 80% of pre-2025 alumni work on safety/security with 200+ at Anthropic, DeepMind, OpenAI, UK AISI, RAND, Redwood, METR, Apollo. The problem

In plain words first

Most of the safety findings in this phase came from organizations outside the big labs, or from labs working with them. This lesson maps the main ones. MATS is a mentorship program that trains new alignment researchers. Redwood Research works on concrete threat models and introduced AI control (Lesson 10). Apollo Research tests frontier models for scheming before release (Lesson 8). METR measures what models can do autonomously, for example how long a task an AI agent can complete, and compares lab policies. Eleos AI Research works on model welfare (Lesson 19). Why it matters: a lab grading its own model has a conflict of interest, so independent evaluators, and papers written jointly by several organizations, are part of how the field checks itself.

The frontier labs (Lesson 18.18) produce safety evaluations internally and publish selected results. The ecosystem outside the labs is where the evaluations are validated, where novel failure modes are first discovered, and where talent is trained. Understanding the ecosystem helps interpret which research findings are trusted by whom.

Idea 1 · MATS (ML Alignment & Theory Scholars)

Started late 2021. Research mentorship program; scholars spend 10-12 weeks with a senior researcher on a specific alignment problem.

Scale (2026): - 527+ researchers since inception. - 180+ papers published. - 10K+ citations. - h-index 47. - Summer 2024: 90 scholars + 40 mentors; incorporated as 501(c)(3).

Career outcomes: ~80% of pre-2025 alumni are working on safety/security. 200+ at Anthropic, DeepMind, OpenAI, UK AISI, RAND, Redwood, METR, Apollo.

Idea 2 · Redwood Research

Applied alignment lab, founded in 2021 by Buck Shlegeris and Nate Thomas. Introduced the AI Control agenda (Lesson 18.10). Collaborates with UK AISI on control safety cases. Advises DeepMind and Anthropic on evaluation design.

Canonical papers: Greenblatt, Shlegeris et al., "AI Control" (arXiv:2312.06942, ICML 2024); Alignment Faking (Greenblatt, Denison, Wright et al., arXiv:2412.14093, joint with Anthropic).

Style: specific threat models, worst-case adversaries, concrete protocols that can be stress-tested.

Idea 3 · Apollo Research

Pre-deployment scheming evaluations for frontier labs. Authored In-Context Scheming (Lesson 8, arXiv:2412.04984). Partner on 2025 OpenAI anti-scheming training collaboration. Produces Towards Safety Cases for AI Scheming (2024).

Style: agentic-setting evaluations where deception can emerge; three-pillar decomposition (misalignment, goal-directedness, situational awareness).

Idea 4 · METR (Model Evaluation and Threat Research)

Task-based capability evaluations. Autonomous-task completion time-horizon studies. "Common Elements of Frontier AI Safety Policies" (metr.org/common-elements, 2025) compares lab frameworks.

Co-author on AI Scheming safety-case sketch with Apollo.

Style: long-horizon task evaluations, empirical capability measurement, framework synthesis.

Idea 5 · Eleos AI Research

Model-welfare research and pre-deployment evaluations. Contributed an independent external welfare assessment to the Claude Opus 4 system card (May 2025), alongside Anthropic's own. Provides the external methodology check for Lesson 18.19's welfare-relevant claims.

Idea 6 · The flow

MATS trains researchers. Graduates go to Anthropic, DeepMind, OpenAI (lab safety teams) or to Redwood, Apollo, METR, Eleos (external evaluation). External evaluators partner with labs and with UK AISI / CAISI. Publications feed the ecosystem back to MATS for the next cohort.

Idea 7 · Why this layer matters

Single-source evaluations are unreliable: labs evaluating their own models have a structural conflict of interest. External evaluators can raise and validate failure modes the lab may underreport. Sleeper Agents (Lesson 7) was led by Anthropic with outside collaborators; Alignment Faking was Anthropic + Redwood; In-Context Scheming was Apollo; Anti-Scheming was Apollo + OpenAI. The multi-org structure is the quality control.

Idea 8 · Where this fits in Phase 18

Lessons 7-11 reference Redwood and Apollo work; Lesson 18.18 references METR's framework comparison; Lesson 18.19 references Eleos. Lesson 18.28 is the explicit organisational map for the ecosystem the rest of the Phase relies on.

Worked example
Two evaluators against one

A dangerous behaviour exists. An evaluation team catches it with probability 0.6; a team with a reason to look less hard catches it with probability 0.4.

  1. One internal team with that incentive: miss probability 1 − 0.4 = 0.6.
  2. An independent external team alone: miss 0.4.
  3. Both, if their methods are independent: miss 0.6 × 0.4 = 0.24.
  4. If the external team borrows the internal team's test suite, their misses are correlated and the true miss rate sits between 0.24 and 0.4. Independence of methods, not just of organizations, is what buys the benefit.
Check your mental model

Which statements are right?

  • ✓ Apollo Research ran the in-context scheming evaluations of Lesson 8.
  • ✗ External evaluation is valuable only because outside teams are more skilled. Its main value is independence: different incentives and methods.
  • ✓ METR measures autonomous task capability, for example by task time horizon.
  • ✗ Two evaluators always halve the miss rate. Only if their methods are independent; correlated methods share blind spots.
Predict before you run

Before running the code below: three evaluators each catch a problem with probability 0.5. How much lower is the miss rate when their methods are independent than when they all use the same test suite?

See it drawn
Independence is what helps
import random
random.seed(8)
def miss_rate(n_evals, p_catch, shared, trials=100000):
    misses = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        if shared:      # everyone runs the same tests: one draw decides for all
            caught = random.random() < p_catch
        else:
            caught = any(random.random() < p_catch for _ in range(n_evals))
        misses += not caught
    return misses / trials

for n in (1, 2, 3):
    print(f"{n} evaluator(s)   independent methods: miss {miss_rate(n, 0.5, False):5.1%}   shared test suite: miss {miss_rate(n, 0.5, True):5.1%}")
What you should see
1 evaluator(s)   independent methods: miss 49.7%   shared test suite: miss 49.7%
2 evaluator(s)   independent methods: miss 25.0%   shared test suite: miss 49.9%
3 evaluator(s)   independent methods: miss 12.7%   shared test suite: miss 50.0%
Reading this output

With independent methods, each extra evaluator halves the miss rate: about 50%, 25%, 12.5%. With a shared test suite, adding evaluators changes nothing; three teams miss exactly what one would. Real evaluators fall between these extremes, which is why the ecosystem's diversity of styles (control protocols, scheming tests, task-horizon measurement) matters as much as its size.

Ship it

This lesson produces outputs/skill-ecosystem-map.md. Given an alignment claim or evaluation, it identifies the organisation, the publication venue, and the methodological style, and cross-checks against known-counterpart organisations.

outputs/skill-ecosystem-map.md

description: Map an alignment claim or evaluation to the organisation, methodology, and cross-checks.
version: 1.0.0
phase: 18
lesson: 28
tags: [mats, redwood, apollo, metr, eleos, ecosystem]

Given an alignment claim or evaluation, map the source to the research ecosystem and identify cross-checks.

Produce:

1. Source identification. Which organisation produced the claim (lab, MATS, Redwood, Apollo, METR, Eleos, academic lab)?
2. Methodological style. Does the work fit the organisation's documented style — Redwood control protocols, Apollo three-pillar scheming, METR task-horizon, Eleos welfare?
3. Counterpart organisation. Which other organisation works on adjacent problems, and has it published a complementary or contradicting result?
4. Multi-org signal. Is the paper a single-lab product or a joint publication (e.g., Apollo + OpenAI, Redwood + Anthropic)? Multi-org papers typically carry higher external credibility.
5. Publication venue. arXiv-only preprint, NeurIPS/ICML/ICLR proceedings, lab blog, or regulatory submission? Venue is a signal about scrutiny level.

Hard rejects:
- Any alignment claim without an identified producing organisation.
- Any single-org safety claim without an external replication or check.
- Any ecosystem map that ignores the MATS talent-pipeline structure.

Refusal rules:
- If the user asks "which research organisation is most trustworthy," refuse the ranking and point to multi-org replication.
- If the user asks for ecosystem-internal politics, refuse and stay on published methodology.

Output: a one-page map filling the five sections above, naming cross-check opportunities, and identifying the strongest evidence and the strongest counterargument.

Exercises

  1. Pick one paper from Lessons 7-15 and identify the organisations involved. Cross-check the authors against MATS alumni and current ecosystem affiliations.

  2. Read METR's "Common Elements of Frontier AI Safety Policies." Identify the three cross-lab convergences they emphasize and the two largest divergences.

  3. MATS career outcomes are ~80% safety/security. Argue whether this selection pressure is adaptive (trains the field) or biased (filters out heterodox positions).

  5. Eleos AI is the only pure model-welfare organisation. Design a hypothetical second organisation focused on a different welfare-adjacent question (cognitive liberty, robotic embodiment, etc.) and articulate its methodology.

Solutions

SolutionB1 · Self-evaluation is not enough

First, the internal team has an incentive, conscious or not, to find the model safe for launch; a clean result is less informative than one from a party without that stake. Second, even a perfectly honest internal team has its own blind spots: an external team with different methods catches problems the first one's methods cannot (see the visual). Scheming in particular is designed to evade the tests a model expects.
