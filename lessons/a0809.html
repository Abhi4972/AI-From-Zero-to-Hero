In plain words first
The idea in one line

Text-to-image makes new things. Inpainting fixes old ones. In production, much of the paid image work is editing: swap a background, remove a logo, extend the canvas, regenerate a hand. Inpainting is where diffusion earns its keep.

The problem

In plain words first

Inpainting regenerates only part of an image, the region inside a mask, and leaves everything else as it was: remove an object, replace a sky, fix a hand. Outpainting does the same beyond the image's edges. Image editing changes the whole picture but keeps its layout. Diffusion models handle all three because generation is gradual: at every denoising step, the known pixels can be fed back in so the generated part has to fit them. The simplest way to do this needs no retraining but can produce fills that do not match their surroundings; dedicated inpainting models see the whole known image and the mask as extra inputs. This lesson measures that mismatch exactly on the lesson's five-number example, shows how RePaint's "resampling" reduces it, and runs SDEdit, editing by partial noising, on a real photo with a small Stable Diffusion. Nothing is trained.

A client sends a perfect product photo with a distracting sign in the background. You want to erase the sign and leave everything else pixel-identical. You cannot run text-to-image from scratch — the result will have a different color, different lighting, different product angle. You want to regenerate only the masked region, and you want the regeneration to respect the surrounding context.

That is inpainting. Variants:

  • Inpainting. Regenerate inside a mask, keep outside pixels.
  • Outpainting. Regenerate outside a mask (or beyond the canvas), keep inside.
  • Image editing. Regenerate the whole image but keep semantic or structural fidelity to the original (SDEdit, InstructPix2Pix).

Most image-generation stacks ship an inpainting mode: FLUX.1 Fill, Stable Diffusion Inpaint, SDXL Inpaint, and mask-based editing in hosted APIs such as OpenAI's GPT Image models. They work on the same principle.

The idea

Idea 1 · The naive approach (and why it's wrong)

Run standard text-to-image with a mask. At each sampling step, replace the unmasked region of the noisy latent with the forward-diffused clean image. It works... badly. Boundary artifacts bleed through because the model has no information about what is in the masked region.

Idea 2 · The proper inpainting model

Train a modified U-Net that takes 9 input channels instead of 4:

input = concat([ noisy_latent (4ch), encoded_image (4ch), mask (1ch) ], dim=channel)

The extra channels are a copy of the VAE-encoded source image plus a single-channel mask. At training time, you randomly mask regions of the image and train the model to denoise only the masked region while the unmasked region is given as a clean conditioning signal. At inference, the model can "see" what surrounds the masked region and produces coherent completions.

SD-Inpaint, SDXL-Inpaint, Flux-Fill all use this 9-channel (or analog) input. Diffusers StableDiffusionInpaintPipeline, FluxFillPipeline.

Idea 3 · SDEdit (Meng et al., 2022) — free editing

Add noise to the source image up to some intermediate t, then run the reverse chain from t down to 0 with a new prompt. No retraining. The choice of starting t trades fidelity for creative freedom:

  • t/T = 0.3 → nearly identical to source, small stylistic changes
  • t/T = 0.6 → moderate edits, preserves coarse structure
  • t/T = 0.9 → generated from near-noise, minimal source preservation

Idea 4 · InstructPix2Pix (Brooks et al., 2023)

Fine-tune a diffusion model on (input_image, instruction, output_image) triples. At inference, condition on both the input image and a text instruction ("make it sunset", "add a dragon"). Two CFG scales: image scale and text scale.

Idea 5 · RePaint (Lugmayr et al., 2022)

Keep a standard unconditional diffusion model. At each reverse step, resample — jump back to a noisier state occasionally and regenerate. Avoids boundary artifacts. Used when you don't have a trained inpainting model.

The same idea as a formula

The goal. Split the image into known pixels xk and missing ones xm (mask M, 1 where missing). Inpainting should sample p(xm∣xk).

Replacement (Step 3). At each reverse step, set the known part to a freshly noised copy of the source and denoise everything:

xtk​=αˉt​​x0k​+1−αˉt​​ε,xt−1​=reverse step(M⊙xt​+(1−M)⊙xtk​)

This is not exact: the missing part is updated using the noisy known part, which says little about the source at high noise, and choices made early are never revisited.

RePaint's resampling. After a reverse step, jump back with the forward step, xt​=1−βt​​xt−1​+βt​​ε, and repeat the step J times. Each round lets the missing part re-adjust to the known part at that noise level.

9-channel inpainting models learn εθ​([xt​,E(x⊙(1−M)),M],t,c): the clean known image and the mask are inputs at every step, so the network sees the context directly.

SDEdit. For strength s∈(0,1], start from xt0​​=αˉt0​​​x0​+1−αˉt0​​​ε with t0​=sT, and run only the last sN of N sampling steps with the new prompt. The source's information surviving in xt0​​ is set by αˉt0​​.

Worked example
SDEdit strength by hand

Stable Diffusion's schedule (scaled linear, β from 0.00085 to 0.012, T=1,000):

strength t0​ αˉt0​​ signal scale αˉ​ noise scale 1−αˉ​
0.3 300 0.59 0.77 0.64
0.6 600 0.16 0.40 0.92
0.9 900 0.014 0.12 0.99

At 0.3 the photo still dominates; at 0.6 noise dominates but the coarse layout survives (low frequencies carry most of an image's energy); at 0.9 little more than the overall colour scheme remains. With 10 sampling steps, the three strengths run 3, 6 and 9 of them, so cost scales with strength too.

A 9-channel input for a 512×512 image: noisy latent 4×64×64, encoded masked image 4×64×64, mask downsampled to 1×64×64: 9×64×64=36,864 numbers into the first convolution.

  • ✗ "Pinning the known pixels at every step guarantees a coherent fill."
    ✓ It guarantees the known pixels are right, not that the fill agrees with them; with weak context the plain method fills the wrong cluster 28% of the time in the run after Step 3.
  • ✗ "A small mask makes inpainting cheap."
    ✓ The network still processes the whole image at every step; only cropping around the mask saves compute.
  • ✗ "SDEdit strength is a percentage of change."
    ✓ It sets the starting noise level; how much changes depends on the schedule's αˉ at that point and on how strongly the prompt disagrees with the image.

Step 1 · 5-D DDPM data

code/main.py implements a toy 1-D inpainting scheme on 5-dimensional data. We train a DDPM on 5-D mixture data where each sample is 5 floats from one of two clusters. At inference, we "mask" 2 of the 5 dimensions, inject the noisy-forward version of the unmasked three at each step, and regenerate only the masked dimensions.

def sample_data(rng):
    cluster = rng.choice([0, 1])
    center = [-1.0] * 5 if cluster == 0 else [1.0] * 5
    return [c + rng.gauss(0, 0.2) for c in center], cluster

Step 2 · train denoiser over all 5 dims

Standard DDPM. Net outputs 5-D noise prediction for 5-D noisy input.

Step 3 · at inference, mask-aware reverse

def inpaint_step(x_t, mask, clean_image, alpha_bars, t, rng):
    # replace unmasked dims with a freshly noised version of the clean source
    a_bar = alpha_bars[t]
    for i in range(len(x_t)):
        if not mask[i]:
            x_t[i] = math.sqrt(a_bar) * clean_image[i] + math.sqrt(1 - a_bar) * rng.gauss(0, 1)
    # ...then run the normal reverse step on x_t

This is the naive approach and it works on toy 1-D data. Real image inpainting uses the 9-channel input because texture coherence matters more.

Replacement and resampling, with an exact denoiser

main.py's schedule at T=1,000 and its two-cluster, five-number data (its main() trains a network, which this page does not run). Because the data is a known Gaussian mixture, the best possible noise predictor is computed exactly, so any failure belongs to the inpainting rule, not to a model. Step 3's replacement rule fills the missing numbers for 1,000 samples, alone and with RePaint's resampling.

import math, runpy, numpy as np
m = runpy.run_path("main.py")       # the lesson's schedule; main() is not run, because it trains a network
T = 1000
alphas, abar = m["make_schedule"](T)
alphas, abar = np.array(alphas), np.array(abar)
D, SD = 5, 0.2                       # main.py's data: 5 numbers near all -1 or all +1, spread 0.2
print(f"schedule: T = {T}, alpha_bar_T = {abar[-1]:.6f}")

def eps_exact(x, t):                 # the best possible noise predictor for this data (Bayes' rule, no training)
    ab = abar[t]; v = ab * SD ** 2 + 1 - ab
    logits = np.stack([-((x - math.sqrt(ab) * c) ** 2).sum(1) / (2 * v) for c in (-1.0, 1.0)], 1)
    r = np.exp(logits - logits.max(1, keepdims=True)); r /= r.sum(1, keepdims=True)
    e0 = sum(r[:, [k]] * (c + math.sqrt(ab) * SD ** 2 / v * (x - math.sqrt(ab) * c)) for k, c in enumerate((-1.0, 1.0)))
    return (x - math.sqrt(ab) * e0) / math.sqrt(1 - ab)

def inpaint(clean, known, n=1000, jumps=1, seed=0):
    """Step 3's rule: re-noise the known numbers from the clean source every step, denoise everything.
    jumps > 1 adds RePaint's resampling: after a step, noise back up one step and redo it."""
    g = np.random.default_rng(seed); x = g.normal(size=(n, D))
    for t in range(T - 1, -1, -1):
        for j in range(jumps):
            x[:, known] = math.sqrt(abar[t]) * clean[known] + math.sqrt(1 - abar[t]) * g.normal(size=(n, known.sum()))
            b = 1 - alphas[t]
            x = (x - b / math.sqrt(1 - abar[t]) * eps_exact(x, t)) / math.sqrt(alphas[t])
            if t > 0:
                x = x + math.sqrt(b) * g.normal(size=x.shape)
                if j < jumps - 1:                                    # RePaint: jump back to step t
                    x = math.sqrt(alphas[t]) * x + math.sqrt(b) * g.normal(size=x.shape)
    x[:, known] = clean[known]
    return x

clean = np.array([1.1, 0.9, 1.05, 0.95, 1.0])                     # a sample from the +1 cluster
odds = math.exp(((clean[0] + 1) ** 2 - (clean[0] - 1) ** 2) / (2 * SD ** 2))
print(f"exact answer: knowing only the first number ({clean[0]}), the odds of the +1 cluster are e^{math.log(odds):.0f} to 1, "
      f"so the filled numbers should all be near +1")
for k, jump_list in ((3, (1,)), (1, (1, 5, 10))):
    known = np.arange(D) < k
    for jumps in jump_list:
        x = inpaint(clean, known, jumps=jumps)
        filled = x[:, ~known]
        wrong = np.mean(filled.mean(1) < 0)
        print(f"{k} known, {D - k} filled, {'plain replacement' if jumps == 1 else f'resampling x{jumps}':<17}: "
              f"filled mean {filled.mean():+.2f}, spread {filled.std():.2f}, samples in the wrong cluster {wrong:.1%}")
What you should see
schedule: T = 1000, alpha_bar_T = 0.000040
exact answer: knowing only the first number (1.1), the odds of the +1 cluster are e^55 to 1, so the filled numbers should all be near +1
3 known, 2 filled, plain replacement: filled mean +0.97, spread 0.21, samples in the wrong cluster 0.0%
1 known, 4 filled, plain replacement: filled mean +0.42, spread 0.92, samples in the wrong cluster 28.4%
1 known, 4 filled, resampling x5    : filled mean +0.72, spread 0.72, samples in the wrong cluster 14.0%
1 known, 4 filled, resampling x10   : filled mean +0.87, spread 0.54, samples in the wrong cluster 6.9%
Reading this output

With three known numbers, plain replacement works perfectly: every fill lands near +1 with the data's spread of 0.2. With only one known number it fails badly, although the right answer is just as certain (odds of e55 to 1): 28% of the fills land in the −1 cluster, and the fills' spread is 0.92 instead of 0.2. The cause is the formula box's caveat. At high noise, the re-noised known number is almost pure noise, so the sampler commits the four missing numbers to a cluster almost at random; by the time the known number is clear enough to say "+1", the fills have already settled and each step can move them only a little. RePaint's resampling gives them more chances to move: 5 rounds per step halve the error to 14%, 10 rounds cut it to 7%, at 5 and 10 times the cost. Image inpainting fails the same way when the context is a thin border around a large hole: colours or objects that do not match the surroundings.

Step 4 · outpainting

Outpainting is inpainting with the mask inverted: mask the new (previously non-existent) canvas, fill the rest with the original. Identical training objective.

Pitfalls

  • Seams. The naive approach leaves visible boundaries because gradient info doesn't flow across the mask. Fix: dilate the mask by 8-16 pixels, or use a proper inpainting model.
  • Mask leakage. If the conditioning image's unmasked region is low-quality or noisy, it pollutes the generation inside the mask. Denoise or blur slightly.
  • CFG interacts with mask size. High CFG on a small mask = saturated patch. Reduce CFG for small edits.
  • SDEdit fidelity cliff. Going from t/T = 0.5 to t/T = 0.6 can lose the subject's identity. Sweep and checkpoint.
  • Prompt mismatch. The prompt should describe the whole image, not just the new content. "A cat sitting on a chair" not "a cat".

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

Break it on purpose

In Step 3's inpaint_step, replace the known numbers with the clean source values instead of a freshly noised version: x_t[i] = clean_image[i]. The final known numbers are still exactly right. What goes wrong with the filled ones? (Solution B1.)

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Dedicated fill models. FLUX.1 Fill and SDXL/SD inpainting checkpoints take the masked image and the mask as inputs; ComfyUI's crop-and-stitch nodes and diffusers' inpaint pipelines (with padding_mask_crop) inpaint a cropped region at full resolution.
  • Instruction-based editing. FLUX.2, FLUX.1 Kontext, Qwen-Image-Edit, OpenAI's GPT Image models and Google's Nano Banana models edit from an instruction alone, often replacing mask-based workflows for everyday edits.
  • Masks from a click. SAM and SAM 2 produce masks from clicks or boxes, and Grounded-SAM from text; matting models such as BiRefNet handle hair-level background removal.

Sources: FLUX.1 Fill, diffusers inpainting, FLUX.1 Kontext, Qwen-Image-Edit, SAM 2, BiRefNet.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Inpainting samples the missing part given the known part; outpainting is the same with the mask inverted.
  • Replacement re-noises the known part each step; cheap and training-free, but weak context can give incoherent fills.
  • RePaint's resampling lets the fill re-adjust; 9-channel models see the clean context directly.
  • SDEdit: noise to t0​=sT, denoise with the new prompt; αˉt0​​ decides what survives.
  • Cost does not shrink with the mask unless you crop.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · More than 20%

More than 20%: 28% of the fills land in the wrong cluster, even with the best possible denoiser and a known number that makes the answer certain. Replacement never conditions on the clean known value; it conditions on a noisy copy of it, and at the high-noise steps, where each sample's cluster is decided, that copy carries almost no information. Later steps, when the known value is clear, can only make small corrections. With three known numbers the combined evidence is strong enough even when noisy, so the problem disappears. Resampling (RePaint) or a model trained to see the clean context (9-channel inpainting) fixes it.

SolutionB1 · Noise levels that do not match

The network was trained on inputs where every number has the noise level of step t. With clean known numbers next to noisy unknown ones, the input is unlike anything it saw in training: at high t the known numbers look like an implausibly strong signal, and the noise predictor's estimate for them, ε=(xt​−αˉt​​x0​)/1−αˉt​​, is badly off because xtk​=x0k​ is not αˉt​​x0k​. Its errors feed into the estimate for the missing numbers, so fills are biased and boundaries in images show seams and colour shifts. The known values are restored at the end, so the bug hides in the output's "correct" pixels. Re-noising to the current level, as Step 3 does, keeps the input on the training distribution.

Production note: edit pipelines are latency-sensitive

Users editing an image expect sub-5-second round trips. A 30-step SDXL-Inpaint at 1024² is 3-4 s on an L4, plus SAM mask generation (~200 ms) and VAE encode/decode (~500 ms combined). In production framing, this is TTFT-bound rather than throughput-bound — batch 1, low concurrency, minimize every stage:

  • SAM-H is the slow one. SAM-H at 1024² is ~200 ms; SAM-ViT-B is ~40 ms with minor quality loss. SAM 2 (video) adds temporal overhead; do not use it for single-image edits.
  • Skip the encode when possible. Every pipeline call VAE-encodes the source image. If you have the latents from the previous generation (typical in iterative-edit UIs), pass them directly via latents=... to skip one VAE encode.
  • Mask size does not change the cost. Both the naive and the 9-channel pipelines run the full U-Net over the whole latent at every step, so a small fix costs as much as a large one, unless you crop around the mask, inpaint the crop at full resolution and paste it back (ComfyUI's crop-and-stitch nodes do exactly this).
  • Instruction editors collapse the stages. FLUX.2, FLUX.1 Kontext and Qwen-Image-Edit take (source_image, instruction) directly: no separate mask, no SDEdit strength sweep. They still run a multi-step sampler, but one model replaces masking, inpainting and prompt engineering.
