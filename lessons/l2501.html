Why this came about

Computers were built for numbers, but most of what people know is written in words: emails, contracts, manuals, conversations. Natural language processing (NLP) is the field that tries to get computers to work with that text. It started in the 1950s with machine translation projects and hand-written grammar rules, which proved far harder than expected. Each later wave, from counting word statistics, to neural networks, to transformerslater · 7.19, solved more of the problem by learning from more text and relying less on rules written by people. Large language models are the latest step in that line, and they still inherit its vocabulary and its hard problems.

In plain words first
Words are easy, meaning is hard

Splitting a sentence into words is easy for a computer. Knowing what the sentence means is not. "The parcel didn't arrive, great service" is a complaint, though it contains the word "great". "Can you cancel it?" depends entirely on what "it" refers to. People resolve these using context, common sense and knowledge of the world, without noticing. An NLP system has to get all of that from somewhere: from rules a person wrote, or from patterns learned from huge amounts of text.

Step 1 · Why language is hard

Difficulty Example What a system needs
Words with several meanings "bank" (river or money), "charge" (a fee, or a battery) the surrounding words
Structure that can be read two ways "I saw the courier with the binoculars" (who had them?) world knowledge about what is likely
References "The order and the refund were both delayed. Has it been sent?" tracking what it points to
Saying one thing, meaning another "Oh great, late again." (sarcasm) tone and context
Endless variation "where's my stuff", "order not received", "parcel missing??" recognising the same intent in different words
Many languages and mixtures "Mera order kab aayega?" (Hindi in Latin letters) data in that language

Every approach in this block is a different answer to the same question: where does the system get the knowledge to resolve these?

Step 2 · The shapes of language tasks

Most real NLP problems are one of these four shapes. A support desk might use all of them: classify incoming tickets by intent, pull out order numbers and names, translate replies, and draft answers. Before 2020 each needed its own model trained on its own labelled data. A large language model can do all of them when asked in plain words (Chapter 2), which is the main reason it replaced so many separate systems.

Step 3 · Text as data: cleaning, splitting and counting

The most basic NLP is plain Python. Here are eight support tickets:

import re
from collections import Counter

tickets = [
    "Where is my order? It was due on Monday!",
    "My order arrived damaged, I want a refund.",
    "Refund still not received after 10 days",
    "How do I change my delivery address?",
    "Order SK-48213 is late. Where is it?",
    "The delivery person was very polite, thanks!",
    "I was charged twice for order SK-50017",
    "Can I return a damaged item for a refund?",
]

def words(text):
    """Lowercase, then keep runs of letters, digits and hyphens as words."""
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9-]+", text.lower())

print(words(tickets[4]))

counts = Counter(w for t in tickets for w in words(t))
print(counts.most_common(8))
What you should see
['order', 'sk-48213', 'is', 'late', 'where', 'is', 'it']
[('order', 4), ('i', 4), ('is', 3), ('my', 3), ('was', 3), ('a', 3), ('refund', 3), ('where', 2)]
Reading this output
  • re.findall(r"[a-z0-9-]+", ...) is a regular expression (Lesson 0.17): "one or more of the characters a to z, 0 to 9 or hyphen". Everything else, such as spaces and punctuation, separates words. This is a simple tokeniserlater · 5.31, a function that cuts text into units.
  • Lowercasing first makes "Order" and "order" the same word. That is usually helpful, though it also merges "US" (the country) with "us".
  • The most common words are mostly short glue words: "is", "my", "a". These are called stop words; classic systems often removed them. The useful words ("order", "refund", "delivery", "damaged") come next.

Removing stop words leaves the words that carry the topic:

STOP = {"is", "my", "a", "i", "it", "the", "was", "for", "on", "do", "how", "can", "to", "after", "where"}
content = Counter(w for t in tickets for w in words(t) if w not in STOP)
print(content.most_common(6))
What you should see
[('order', 4), ('refund', 3), ('damaged', 2), ('delivery', 2), ('due', 1), ('monday', 1)]

Counting words already tells you something: this desk's tickets are about orders, refunds, damage and delivery. Lesson 5.32 turns exactly these counts into features for a classifier.

Step 4 · Rules: where they work and where they break

Hand-written rules work well when the pattern is exact. Order numbers, amounts and dates follow fixed formats, and a regular expression finds them reliably:

text = "Hi, order SK-48213 cost Rs 1,299 and arrived 12/03/2026; order SK-50017 never came."

print("order ids:", re.findall(r"SK-\d{5}", text))                 # SK- followed by exactly 5 digits
print("amounts:  ", re.findall(r"Rs\s?[\d,]+", text))               # Rs, optional space, digits and commas
print("dates:    ", re.findall(r"\d{2}/\d{2}/\d{4}", text))        # dd/mm/yyyy
What you should see
order ids: ['SK-48213', 'SK-50017']
amounts:   ['Rs 1,299']
dates:     ['12/03/2026']

They break when the pattern is about meaning. Here is a rule-based intent detector of the kind built in the 1990s:

RULES = {
    "refund":   r"\brefund|money back\b",
    "where":    r"\bwhere is\b|\bnot (arrived|received)\b",
    "damaged":  r"\bdamaged|broken\b",
}

def intent(text):
    found = [name for name, pattern in RULES.items() if re.search(pattern, text.lower())]
    return found or ["unknown"]

for t in ["Where is my order?",
          "I'd like my money back please",
          "The box came smashed and the glass is in pieces",
          "Still waiting... it's been two weeks",
          "No need for a refund, the replacement was fine"]:
    print(f"{intent(t)!s:<22} {t}")
What you should see
['where']              Where is my order?
['refund']             I'd like my money back please
['unknown']            The box came smashed and the glass is in pieces
['unknown']            Still waiting... it's been two weeks
['refund']             No need for a refund, the replacement was fine
Reading this output
  • \b means a word boundary, so \brefund matches "refund" but not "prerefund". | means "or".
  • The first two are right. "Smashed" and "in pieces" mean damaged, but the rule only knows the words "damaged" and "broken". "Still waiting... two weeks" is clearly a missing order but matches nothing. And "No need for a refund" is tagged as a refund request: the rule sees the word and misses the "no need".
  • You could add more patterns, and teams did, for years. Rule sets grew to thousands of lines and still missed new phrasings. That is the problem learning from examples solves (Lesson 2.22), and the rest of this block shows how.
Tip
Rules still have a place

In real systems built with language models, rules and regular expressions are still the right tool for exact formats: validating an order ID before calling a tool, masking card numbers before text is logged, or checking that a model's output is valid JSON. Use learned models for meaning and rules for format.

Step 5 · Seventy years in one picture

  • Rules (1950s to 1980s). ELIZA (1966) mimicked a therapist with pattern-and-response rules, and some users believed it understood them. Grammars and rule systems worked in narrow domains and broke outside them.
  • Statistics (1990s to 2000s). Count how often words and word sequences appear in large amounts of text, and use probabilities: n-gram language models (Lesson 5.34), naive Bayes spam filters, statistical translation. Much more robust, but blind to meaning beyond a few words.
  • Neural networks (2003 to 2016). Learn a vector for every word (Lesson 5.33), and use recurrent networks that read text word by word (Lesson 5.35). Encoder-decoder models and attention (Lesson 5.36) made neural translation work.
  • Transformers (2017 onwards). A design built entirely on attention, which trains efficiently on GPUs (Chapter F6). Pretrained models such as BERT and GPT-2 (2018 to 2019) learned general language skills from huge text collections, then were adapted to tasks.
  • Large language models (2020 onwards). Scale those models up, train them to follow instructions (Chapter F7), and a single model handles most language tasks when asked in words.

The pattern across the eras is that each one needed less knowledge written by people and more data and compute. Lesson 3.23 tells the same story for AI as a whole.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "If a computer can split text into words, it understands the text."
    ✓ Splitting and counting are easy. Meaning depends on context, references and world knowledge, which is the hard part.
  • ✗ "Rules and regular expressions are obsolete."
    ✓ They are still the best tool for exact formats such as IDs, dates and amounts, and for checking outputs. They fail at meaning.
  • ✗ "Language models replaced NLP."
    ✓ They are the current method of NLP. The tasks, the difficulties and many of the tools (tokenisers, embeddings, evaluation) carried over.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · A messy string

['don', 't', 'e-mail', 'me', 'at', 'a', 'b', 'x', 'com']. The apostrophe, the dots and the @ are not in the character class, so they split words: "don't" becomes two pieces and the email address falls apart. Real tokenisers make deliberate choices about such cases; Lesson 5.31 shows the approach language models use.

SolutionM1 · Extending the rules

For example, add "damaged": r"\bdamaged|broken|smashed|in pieces\b" and "where": r"\bwhere is\b|\bnot (arrived|received)\b|\bstill waiting\b". New failures are easy to write: "the vase is cracked" (another damage word), "it's been ages and nothing has turned up" (a missing order with none of the phrases), "is it possible to send it somewhere else?" (contains no rule word, yet is an address change). Each fix invites a new miss.

SolutionX1 · Task shapes

(a) token labelling (named-entity recognition); (b) text classification (sentiment); (c) text to text (summarisation); (d) generation. A language model can do all four from instructions, while older systems needed a separately trained model for each.

Recap
  • Language is hard for computers because meaning depends on context, references, tone and world knowledge, and the same idea can be said in endless ways.
  • Most tasks are one of four shapes: classify a text, label each token, turn text into other text, or generate text.
  • Basic text handling (lowercasing, splitting with regular expressions, counting) is plain Python and already reveals what a collection of text is about.
  • Rules are reliable for exact formats and brittle for meaning.
  • The field moved from rules, to statistics, to neural networks, to transformers and large language models, each needing less hand-crafting and more data.
