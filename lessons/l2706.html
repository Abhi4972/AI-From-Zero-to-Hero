Why this came about

For years, images, sound and text each had their own kind of network: convolutional networks for images, recurrent ones for speech. In 2020 the Vision Transformer (ViT) showed that a plain transformer works on images if you cut them into patches and treat each patch as a token. In 2021 OpenAI's CLIP trained an image encoder and a text encoder together on 400 million image-caption pairs, so pictures and words landed in one shared embedding space. Image generation took a different path: diffusion models (2020) learned to turn random noise into images step by step, leading to DALL·E 2, Stable Diffusion and Midjourney in 2022. Whisper (2022) applied the encoder-decoder transformer to speech. By 2024, frontier modelslater · 10.43 read images, audio and documents natively, and the same transformer ideas from the Transformers, step by step section sit underneath all of it.

In plain words first
Everything becomes a sequence of vectors

A transformer does not care where its vectors come from. Cut a photo into small squares and turn each into a vector: now it is a "sentence" of patches. Cut a sound recording into short slices and describe each slice's pitch content: another sequence. Once everything is a sequence of vectors, the same attention machinery can relate image patches, sound slices and words to each other. Generating images works differently: start from pure static and remove a little noise at a time, guided by a network that has learned what images look like.

Step 1 · Images as tokens

An image is a grid of numbers: brightness per pixel (or three numbers, red, green and blue, for colour). A Vision Transformer cuts it into fixed-size patches, flattens each patch into a vector, and projects it to the model's width, exactly like a token embedding (Lesson 3.19):

import numpy as np
import torch
from torch import nn
from sklearn.datasets import load_digits

image = torch.tensor(load_digits().images[0], dtype=torch.float32)   # an 8 x 8 picture of a zero
print("image shape:", tuple(image.shape))

patches = image.unfold(0, 4, 4).unfold(1, 4, 4)       # cut into 4 x 4 squares: (2, 2, 4, 4)
patches = patches.reshape(4, 16)                       # 4 patches, 16 pixel values each
print("patches as a sequence:", tuple(patches.shape))

torch.manual_seed(0)
patch_embed = nn.Linear(16, 64)                        # learned projection, like a token embedding
tokens = patch_embed(patches)
print("patch tokens for a transformer:", tuple(tokens.shape))
What you should see
image shape: (8, 8)
patches as a sequence: (4, 16)
patch tokens for a transformer: (4, 64)

Real models use larger images and patches: a 224 × 224 colour image cut into 16 × 16 patches gives 196 patches of 768 numbers (16 × 16 × 3), a "sentence" of 196 tokens. A position embedding tells the model where each patch was (Lesson 7.19), and from there it is an ordinary transformer encoder.

Step 2 · Linking pictures and words: contrastive learning

CLIP trains an image encoder and a text encoder so that a picture and its caption get similar vectors, and mismatched pairs get dissimilar ones. In a batch of n image-caption pairs, every image is compared with every caption; the training target is the diagonal: image i should match caption i. With small made-up embeddings:

import torch.nn.functional as F

captions = ["a cracked phone screen", "a parcel on a doorstep", "a dented kettle"]
image_vecs = F.normalize(torch.tensor([[0.9, 0.1, 0.2], [0.1, 0.95, 0.1], [0.3, 0.1, 0.9]]), dim=-1)   # from an image encoder
text_vecs = F.normalize(torch.tensor([[1.0, 0.0, 0.3], [0.0, 1.0, 0.2], [0.2, 0.2, 1.0]]), dim=-1)     # from a text encoder

sims = image_vecs @ text_vecs.T                       # every image against every caption
print(sims.round(decimals=2))
probs = F.softmax(sims / 0.07, dim=-1)                # CLIP sharpens similarities with a temperature of about 0.07
print("best caption for each image:", [captions[i] for i in probs.argmax(dim=-1)])
loss = F.cross_entropy(sims / 0.07, torch.arange(3))  # target: image i matches caption i
print(f"contrastive loss: {loss:.3f}")
What you should see
tensor([[0.9900, 0.1500, 0.4200],
        [0.1300, 0.9900, 0.3100],
        [0.5700, 0.2900, 0.9900]])
best caption for each image: ['a cracked phone screen', 'a parcel on a doorstep', 'a dented kettle']
contrastive loss: 0.001

Training on hundreds of millions of pairs gives encoders that place "a cracked phone screen" near photos of cracked phones, without anyone labelling categories. Such models power image search ("find photos of damaged parcels"), zero-shot image classification (compare an image with the captions "a photo of a dog", "a photo of a cat"), and the image side of many multimodal systems (Lesson 4.31).

Step 3 · How a multimodal language model reads an image

Most multimodal LLMs connect a vision encoder (often CLIP-like) to a language model through a small projection layer, then fine-tune on image-text conversations. The image becomes a few hundred "image tokens" placed in the context alongside the text, and attention does the rest. That is why images count towards a model's context and cost (Lesson 4.30), and why models can misread small text in an image: detail is limited by the patch resolution.

Step 4 · Generating images: diffusion

A diffusion model learns to reverse a gradual noising process:

  • Forward process (fixed, no learning): take a real example and add a little random noise, step after step, until nothing but noise remains.
  • Reverse process (learned): train a network to look at a noisy example and the step number, and predict the noise that was added. Start from pure noise, repeatedly subtract the predicted noise, and an example emerges.

Here is a complete diffusion model on two-dimensional points (two interlocking moons), small enough to train in seconds:

import time
from sklearn.datasets import make_moons

X, _ = make_moons(n_samples=2000, noise=0.05, random_state=0)
data = torch.tensor((X - X.mean(0)) / X.std(0), dtype=torch.float32)      # 2,000 points, standardised

T = 50                                                    # number of noise steps
betas = torch.linspace(1e-4, 0.2, T)                      # how much noise each step adds
alphas = 1 - betas
abar = torch.cumprod(alphas, 0)                           # how much of the original survives after t steps
print("signal remaining after steps 1, 10, 25, 50:", abar[[0, 9, 24, 49]].numpy().round(3))

class Denoiser(nn.Module):
    """Given a noisy point and the step number, predict the noise that was added."""
    def __init__(self, h=128):
        super().__init__()
        self.net = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(3, h), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(h, h), nn.SiLU(),
                                 nn.Linear(h, h), nn.SiLU(), nn.Linear(h, 2))
    def forward(self, x, t):
        return self.net(torch.cat([x, (t.float() / T).unsqueeze(1)], dim=1))

torch.manual_seed(0)
model = Denoiser()
opt = torch.optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=2e-3)
start = time.time()
for step in range(1, 3001):
    idx = torch.randint(0, len(data), (256,))
    x0 = data[idx]
    t = torch.randint(0, T, (256,))                                      # a random noise level for each point
    eps = torch.randn_like(x0)
    xt = abar[t].sqrt().unsqueeze(1) * x0 + (1 - abar[t]).sqrt().unsqueeze(1) * eps   # jump straight to step t
    loss = F.mse_loss(model(xt, t), eps)                                 # learn to predict the noise
    opt.zero_grad()
    loss.backward()
    opt.step()
print("trained in under a minute:", time.time() - start < 60)
What you should see
signal remaining after steps 1, 10, 25, 50: [1.    0.829 0.281 0.005]
trained in under a minute: True
Reading this output
Reading this code
  • After 50 steps almost none of the original point survives (0.5%): the forward process really does end in pure noise.
  • Training never runs the 50 steps one by one: a formula jumps straight to any step t, so each training example is a random point at a random noise level. The loss is just mean squared error between the true noise and the predicted noise (Lesson 2.23).
  • The network sees the step number (scaled to 0 to 1) so it knows how noisy its input is. Image models condition on the step in a similar way, and on the text prompt too.

Now generate: start from pure noise and run the reverse steps.

@torch.no_grad()
def generate(n=500, seed=1):
    g = torch.Generator().manual_seed(seed)
    x = torch.randn(n, 2, generator=g)                                    # pure noise
    for t in reversed(range(T)):
        eps = model(x, torch.full((n,), t))
        x = (x - betas[t] / (1 - abar[t]).sqrt() * eps) / alphas[t].sqrt()   # remove the predicted noise
        if t > 0:
            x = x + betas[t].sqrt() * torch.randn(n, 2, generator=g)       # a little fresh noise keeps samples varied
    return x

samples = generate()
noise = torch.randn(500, 2, generator=torch.Generator().manual_seed(1))

def share_near_data(points):
    return (torch.cdist(points, data).min(dim=1).values < 0.15).float().mean().item()

print(f"points close to the real moons: pure noise {share_near_data(noise):.0%}, generated {share_near_data(samples):.0%}")
What you should see
points close to the real moons: pure noise 41%, generated 88%

Most generated points land on the moons, though the model never memorised any training point: it learned the shape of the data. Image generators work the same way, with three differences in scale and design: the "points" are images (often compressed first into a smaller latent space, as in Stable Diffusion), the denoiser is a large network (a U-Net or a transformer), and it is conditioned on a text embedding, so the noise is removed in the direction of "a parcel on a doorstep at sunset". Lesson 4.32 builds each of those pieces in miniature, and Lesson 4.33 covers using such models and the provenance and rights questions they raise.

Step 5 · Audio

Sound is a sequence of air-pressure measurements, typically 16,000 per second for speech. Models rarely use the raw samples; they use a spectrogram: cut the sound into short frames and measure how much of each frequency each frame contains.

rate = 16_000
t = np.arange(0, 1.0, 1 / rate)
signal = np.where(t < 0.5, np.sin(2 * np.pi * 440 * t), np.sin(2 * np.pi * 880 * t))   # half a second at 440 Hz, then 880 Hz
print("raw samples:", signal.shape)

frame = 400                                                  # 25 ms frames
frames = signal[: len(signal) // frame * frame].reshape(-1, frame)
spectrum = np.abs(np.fft.rfft(frames, axis=1))               # strength of each frequency in each frame
freqs = np.fft.rfftfreq(frame, 1 / rate)
print("spectrogram shape (frames, frequencies):", spectrum.shape)
for i in [0, 10, 30, 39]:
    print(f"frame {i:>2} ({i * 25:>3} ms): strongest frequency {freqs[spectrum[i].argmax()]:.0f} Hz")
What you should see
raw samples: (16000,)
spectrogram shape (frames, frequencies): (40, 201)
frame  0 (  0 ms): strongest frequency 440 Hz
frame 10 (250 ms): strongest frequency 440 Hz
frame 30 (750 ms): strongest frequency 880 Hz
frame 39 (975 ms): strongest frequency 880 Hz

The spectrogram shows the switch from 440 Hz to 880 Hz halfway through. Whisper feeds such spectrograms (on a perceptual "mel" frequency scale) into a transformer encoder, and a decoder writes the transcript token by token: the encoder-decoder design of Lesson 7.22, with sound in and text out. Text-to-speech runs in the other direction, and speech-to-speech modelslater · 6.18 used in voice agents (Lesson 6.18) handle both.

The same idea as a formula
  • ViT: image → n patches of p × p × channels → linear projection → n tokens (+ positions)
  • CLIP loss: cross-entropy over the similarity matrix S/τ in both directions, with targets on the diagonal
  • diffusion forward process: x_t = √ᾱ_t x₀ + √(1 − ᾱ_t) ε, with ε ~ N(0, I) and ᾱ_t = Π (1 − β_s)
  • training: minimise ‖ε − ε_θ(x_t, t)‖²; sampling: x_{t−1} = (x_t − β_t / √(1 − ᾱ_t) · ε_θ) / √(1 − β_t) + √β_t z
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Image models and language models are completely different technologies."
    ✓ Both use transformers on sequences of vectors; images are cut into patch tokens.
  • ✗ "A diffusion model stores and retrieves training images."
    ✓ It learns to remove noise towards the shape of its training data. (Large models can still memorise some heavily repeated images, a known concern for copyright and privacy.)
  • ✗ "A model that reads images sees every pixel in detail."
    ✓ Images are reduced to a limited number of patch tokens; small text and fine detail can be lost.

Practice
Apply it

A colour image of 448 × 448 pixels is cut into 14 × 14 patches. How many patch tokens does it produce, and how many numbers does each patch hold before projection? (Solution X1.)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Stopping halfway

The points are still noisy: they have moved towards the moons but are spread in a blurry cloud around them, because the remaining noise (about 28% of the signal survives at step 25, so most of the variation is still noise) has not been removed. Image generators show the same thing: intermediate steps look like blurry blobs that sharpen in the last steps.

SolutionM1 · Two rings

Yes: with the same model and training, most samples land on one of the two rings. The denoiser learns whatever shape the data has; nothing in the code is specific to moons. Thinner or more complex shapes need more training, a bigger network or more steps.

SolutionX1 · Counting patches

448 ÷ 14 = 32 patches along each side, so 32 × 32 = 1,024 patches. Each holds 14 × 14 × 3 = 588 numbers. 1,024 image tokens is a large share of a context windowlater · 10.42, which is why many models resize images or compress image tokens.

Recap
  • Vision transformers cut images into patches and treat each patch as a token; CLIP-style training links images and captions in one embedding space.
  • Multimodal language models feed image tokens from a vision encoder, through a projection, into the language model's context.
  • Diffusion models learn to predict the noise added to data; generation starts from pure noise and removes it step by step, guided by text in image generators.
  • Audio is usually turned into spectrograms; Whisper is an encoder-decoder from spectrogram to text.
  • The same transformer and embedding ideas from the sections from Neural networks and deep learning to Transformers, step by step underlie all of these.
