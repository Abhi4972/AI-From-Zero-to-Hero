The problem

In plain words first

Natural language inference (NLI) asks one question about two texts: given the first (the premise), is the second (the hypothesis) definitely true, definitely false, or undecided? These three answers are called entailment, contradiction and neutral. The task sounds academic, but it is the workhorse behind three practical jobs: checking whether a summary or chatbot answer is supported by its source, classifying text into labels the model was never trained on ("this text is about sports": true or not?), and filtering contradictory claims. This lesson defines the task precisely, shows how a cross-encoder computes the three probabilities and how zero-shot classification turns them into label scores, measures the famous dataset shortcut where the hypothesis alone gives the answer away, and runs a real NLI model on standard and adversarial test sets and as a faithfulness checker.

You built a summarizer. It produced a summary. How do you know the summary does not contain a hallucination?

You built a chatbot. It answered "yes." How do you know the answer is supported by the retrieved passage?

You need to classify 10,000 news articles by topic. You have no training labels. Can you reuse a model?

All three problems reduce to Natural Language Inference. NLI asks: given a premise t and a hypothesis h, is h entailed by t, contradicted, or neutral (unrelated)?

  • Hallucination check: t = source document, h = summary claim. Not entailment = hallucination.
  • Grounded QA: t = retrieved passage, h = generated answer. Not entailment = fabrication.
  • Zero-shot classification: t = document, h = verbalized label ("This is about sports"). Entailment = predicted label.

One task, three production uses. This is why NLI models, or LLMs prompted to do the same three-way judgement, sit underneath most hallucination and faithfulness checks in RAG evaluation.

The idea

The three labels.

  • Entailment. t → h. "The cat is on the mat" entails "There is a cat."
  • Contradiction. t → ¬h. "The cat is on the mat" contradicts "There is no cat."
  • Neutral. No inference either way. "The cat is on the mat" is neutral to "The cat is hungry."

Not logical entailment. NLI is natural language inference — what a typical human reader would infer, not strict logic. "John walked his dog" entails "John has a dog" in NLI, but strict first-order logic would only admit it if you axiomatize possession.

Datasets.

  • SNLI (2015). 570k human-annotated pairs, image captions as premises. Narrow domain.
  • MultiNLI (2017). 433k pairs across 10 genres. The standard training corpus in 2026.
  • ANLI (2019). Adversarial NLI. Humans wrote examples specifically designed to break existing models. Harder.
  • DocNLI, ConTRoL (2020–21). Document-length premises. Tests multi-hop and long-range inference.

The architecture. A transformer encoder (BERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa) reads [CLS] premise [SEP] hypothesis [SEP]. The [CLS] representation feeds a 3-way softmax. Train on MNLI, evaluate on held-out benchmarks, get 90%+ accuracy on in-distribution pairs.

Zero-shot via NLI. Given a document and candidate labels, turn each label into a hypothesis ("This text is about sports"). Compute entailment probability for each. Pick the max. This is the mechanism behind Hugging Face's zero-shot-classification pipeline.

The same idea as a formula

The model. A cross-encoder reads both texts together, [CLS] premise [SEP] hypothesis [SEP], so every hypothesis token can attend to every premise token. The final [CLS] vector h goes through a linear layer to three logits z=Wh+b, and

P(y∣t,h)=softmax(z)y​,y∈{entailment,neutral,contradiction}

trained with cross-entropy on labelled pairs (MultiNLI, ANLI, ...).

Zero-shot classification. For a text x and labels ℓ1​,…,ℓK​, build hypotheses hk​ = "This example is about ℓk​." Two ways to score: - Single-label (one label is true): softmax across labels of the entailment logits, P(ℓk​∣x)=∑j​expzent​(x,hj​)expzent​(x,hk​)​. Scores sum to 1. - Multi-label (any number can be true): for each label separately, softmax over just entailment and contradiction, P(ℓk​)=ezent​+ezcon​ezent​​. Scores do not sum to 1.

Faithfulness. Split an answer a into atomic claims c1​,…,cm​ and score faithfulness(a,context)=m1​∑i​1[P(entail∣context,ci​)>τ].

Dataset artefacts. If the label is predictable from the hypothesis alone, P(y∣h)=P(y), then a model can score well without reading the premise; a test of how much a dataset leaks is how far maxy​P(y∣some feature of h) rises above the base rate.

Worked example
From logits to labels by hand

An NLI model gives these entailment and contradiction logits for "The stock market rallied after the central bank cut rates" against three hypotheses:

hypothesis zent​ zcon​
This example is about finance. 3.0 -2.0
This example is about politics. 0.5 0.0
This example is about sports. -2.5 2.0
  • Single-label: e3.0=20.1, e0.5=1.65, e−2.5=0.082; sum 21.8. P(finance)=0.92, P(politics)=0.076, P(sports)=0.004.
  • Multi-label: finance =e3/(e3+e−2)=0.993; politics =e0.5/(e0.5+e0)=0.62; sports =e−2.5/(e−2.5+e2)=0.011.

Single-label forces the labels to compete, so "politics" looks unlikely; multi-label judges each on its own, and "politics" is more likely than not, a fair reading of a story about a central bank. Pick the mode that matches whether your labels exclude each other.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Entailment means logical implication."
    ✓ It means what a typical reader would conclude. "John walked his dog" entails "John has a dog" for NLI.
  • ✗ "A high accuracy on MultiNLI means the model understands inference."
    ✓ Part of that accuracy can come from shortcuts in the hypotheses; adversarial sets such as ANLI and HANS expose how much.
  • ✗ "Neutral means the texts are unrelated."
    ✓ Neutral means the premise neither guarantees nor rules out the hypothesis; the texts can be closely related ("The cat is on the mat" / "The cat is hungry").

Step 1 · run a pretrained NLI model

import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForSequenceClassification

nli_id = "MoritzLaurer/mDeBERTa-v3-base-xnli-multilingual-nli-2mil7"   # stands in for the 1.6 GB bart-large-mnli
nli_tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(nli_id)
nli_model = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(nli_id).eval()


def nli(pair):
    """Same output shape as the old text-classification pipeline with top_k=None."""
    enc = nli_tok(pair["text"], pair["text_pair"], return_tensors="pt", truncation=True)
    with torch.no_grad():
        probs = torch.softmax(nli_model(**enc).logits[0], -1)
    scores = [{"label": nli_model.config.id2label[i], "score": round(p.item(), 4)} for i, p in enumerate(probs)]
    return [sorted(scores, key=lambda s: -s["score"])]


premise = "The cat is sleeping on the couch."
hypothesis = "There is a cat in the room."
result = nli({"text": premise, "text_pair": hypothesis})[0]
print(result)
What you should see
[{'label': 'entailment', 'score': 0.9814}, {'label': 'neutral', 'score': 0.0185}, {'label': 'contradiction', 'score': 0.0001}]

For production NLI, facebook/bart-large-mnli and MoritzLaurer/DeBERTa-v3-large-mnli-fever-anli-ling-wanli are the open defaults; DeBERTa-v3 models are among the strongest encoders on NLI benchmarks. Both are over 1.2 GB, so this page runs a smaller multilingual DeBERTa-v3 NLI model through the same interface (rewritten above, since transformers 5 removed the zero-shot-classification pipeline).

Step 2 · zero-shot classification

ENTAIL = nli_model.config.label2id["entailment"]

def zs(text, candidate_labels, hypothesis_template="This example is about {}."):
    """Single-label zero-shot: softmax over the entailment logits of all candidate hypotheses."""
    logits = []
    for label in candidate_labels:
        enc = nli_tok(text, hypothesis_template.format(label), return_tensors="pt", truncation=True)
        with torch.no_grad():
            logits.append(nli_model(**enc).logits[0, ENTAIL])
    probs = torch.softmax(torch.stack(logits), 0)
    order = probs.argsort(descending=True)
    return {"labels": [candidate_labels[i] for i in order], "scores": [round(probs[i].item(), 3) for i in order]}

text = "The stock market rallied after the central bank cut interest rates."
labels = ["finance", "sports", "politics", "technology"]
result = zs(text, candidate_labels=labels)
print(result)
What you should see
{'labels': ['finance', 'politics', 'technology', 'sports'], 'scores': [0.986, 0.014, 0.0, 0.0]}

The template is "This example is about {label}." by default. Customize with hypothesis_template. No training data required. No fine-tuning. Works out of the box.

Step 3 · faithfulness check for RAG

def is_faithful(answer, context, threshold=0.5):
    result = nli({"text": context, "text_pair": answer})[0]
    entail = next(s for s in result if s["label"] == "entailment")
    return entail["score"] > threshold

This is the core idea of faithfulness metrics such as RAGAS's (which uses an LLM as the judge, where this uses an NLI model). Split the generated answer into atomic claims. Check each claim against the retrieved context. Report the fraction that entail.

Break it on purpose

Use the lesson's is_faithful with threshold=0.5 on the context "The meeting is on Tuesday at 10 am in room 4" and the answer "The meeting is on Tuesday at 10 am in room 4, and lunch will be provided." What does it return, and what is wrong with checking a whole answer as one hypothesis? (Solution B1.)

Pitfalls

  • Hypothesis-only shortcuts. Classifiers that never see the premise predict the label from the hypothesis alone about 67% of the time on SNLI and 53% on MultiNLI, against about 34% by chance (Gururangan et al., 2018), because annotators wrote contradictions with "not", "nobody", "never" and entailments as vaguer paraphrases. Strong baseline for detecting label leakage.
  • Lexical overlap heuristic. The subsequence heuristic ("every subsequence is entailed") passes SNLI but fails HANS/ANLI. Use adversarial benchmarks.
  • Document-length degradation. Models trained on sentence pairs lose much of their accuracy on document-length premises, where the evidence may be spread over several sentences. Use DocNLI-trained models for long context.
  • Zero-shot template sensitivity. "This example is about {label}" vs "{label}" vs "The topic is {label}" can swing accuracy by many points. Tune the template on a labelled sample.
  • Domain mismatch. MNLI trains on general English. Legal, medical, and scientific text need domain-specific NLI models (e.g., SciNLI, MedNLI).

import numpy as np
import pyarrow.parquet as pq
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

Overall the three labels are balanced (35%, 32%, 33%). Group the pairs by nothing but the hypothesis, and the balance breaks: hypotheses containing a negation word are contradictions 60% of the time and entailments only 22%; hedges like "some" or "a few" are contradictions only 10% of the time; universal words like "all", "every" and "only" lean toward contradiction (46%). Annotators, asked to write a false statement quickly, reached for "not" or an absolute claim; asked for a true one, they wrote something vaguer. A model can learn these cues without reading the premise, which is why adversarial datasets were built and why hypothesis-only baselines are a standard check.

A real NLI model: standard, adversarial, and lexical-overlap tests

The NLI model above on 300 pairs from MultiNLI's validation set and 300 from ANLI's first adversarial round (written by people trying to fool a strong model), then on a few HANS-style pairs where every hypothesis word appears in the premise.

import time
anli = pq.read_table(hf_hub_download("facebook/anli", "plain_text/test_r1-00000-of-00001.parquet", repo_type="dataset")).to_pylist()
def predict(p, h):
    return max(nli({"text": p, "text_pair": h})[0], key=lambda s: s["score"])["label"]
start = time.time()
for name, data in (("MultiNLI matched", mnli[:300]), ("ANLI round 1", anli[:300])):
    acc = np.mean([predict(r["premise"], r["hypothesis"]) == NAMES[r["label"]] for r in data])
    print(f"{name:18s} accuracy {acc:.1%}")
print(f"({time.time() - start:.0f} s)\n")
hans = [("The doctor saw the lawyer.", "The lawyer saw the doctor.", "neutral"),
        ("The senator near the actor danced.", "The actor danced.", "neutral"),
        ("The judge who the athlete admired slept.", "The athlete slept.", "neutral"),
        ("The president thanked the manager and the banker.", "The president thanked the banker.", "entailment")]
for p, h, gold in hans:
    print(f"{p:50s} => {h:34s} predicted {predict(p, h):13s} (correct: {gold})")

On ordinary MultiNLI pairs the model is right 82% of the time; on ANLI's adversarial first round, 67%, although these pairs are no harder for people. This model was trained on ANLI's training data among others, so the gap is smaller than for a model trained on MultiNLI alone, and still 15 points. The HANS-style pairs probe the overlap shortcut. The model handles the swapped roles ("the lawyer saw the doctor": neutral) and the conjunction. It fails the relative clause: "The judge who the athlete admired slept" does not say the athlete slept, yet every word of "the athlete slept" is in the premise and the model calls it entailment, exactly the heuristic HANS was designed to catch. And it calls "The actor danced" a contradiction of "The senator near the actor danced", over-reading the sentence. A base-size model on CPU, in a few minutes, reproduces the main findings of a decade of NLI research.

Faithfulness, claim by claim

A short context and an answer of three claims, one of which is not in the context. The lesson's is_faithful checks the whole answer at once and then each claim separately.

context = ("The museum opens at 9 am on weekdays and 10 am on weekends. Entry is free for children under 12. "
           "The cafe on the second floor closes at 5 pm.")
claims = ["The museum opens at 10 am on Saturdays.", "Children under 12 get in free.", "The cafe serves vegan lunches."]
answer = " ".join(claims)
print(f"whole answer faithful? {is_faithful(answer, context)}   "
      f"(entailment {next(s['score'] for s in nli({'text': context, 'text_pair': answer})[0] if s['label'] == 'entailment'):.2f})")
ok = []
for c in claims:
    scores = {s["label"]: s["score"] for s in nli({"text": context, "text_pair": c})[0]}
    ok.append(scores["entailment"] > 0.5)
    print(f"   {c:42s} entail {scores['entailment']:.2f}  neutral {scores['neutral']:.2f}  contra {scores['contradiction']:.2f}")
print(f"claim-level faithfulness: {sum(ok)}/{len(ok)}")

Reading this output

Checked as one hypothesis, the whole answer gets an entailment probability of 0.00, so is_faithful returns False: correct, but useless for fixing anything, since it does not say which part failed, and two of the three statements are fine. Checked claim by claim, the picture is clear: the Saturday opening time (inferred from "10 am on weekends", a real inference, not a word match) and the free entry are entailed, while the vegan lunches are neutral, not supported by the context. Two of three claims are faithful. That decomposition is exactly what faithfulness metrics do, and the third claim is the hallucination they exist to catch.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • NLI-style checks are production guardrails. Amazon Bedrock Guardrails' contextual grounding check, Azure AI Content Safety's groundedness detection and Vectara's open HHEM-2.1 model score whether an answer is supported by its source text, the task of this lesson applied to RAG outputs.
  • LLM judges and small verifiers side by side. RAG evaluation frameworks ask an LLM to verify each claim, while small trained verifiers (HHEM, Bespoke-MiniCheck) do the same job far more cheaply at high volume.
  • NLI as a routing and classification tool. Zero-shot NLI classification remains a cheap baseline before collecting labels, and GLiNER2 (Lesson 5.6) covers classification with the same "labels at inference time" idea.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • NLI: premise and hypothesis in, entailment / neutral / contradiction out; "entailment" means what a reader would conclude.
  • A cross-encoder reads both texts together and softmaxes three logits.
  • Zero-shot classification scores label hypotheses: softmax across labels for single-label, entailment vs contradiction per label for multi-label.
  • Datasets leak labels through hypothesis wording; test on adversarial sets (ANLI, HANS).
  • Check faithfulness claim by claim, not answer by answer.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · About 60%

In the run, 60% of the 1,456 hypotheses with a negation word are contradictions, and only 22% entailments, against a base rate of about a third each. Annotators writing a contradiction under time pressure most easily negated the premise. That single cue is enough for a hypothesis-only classifier to beat chance by a wide margin, the artefact Gururangan et al. reported in 2018.

SolutionB1 · One verdict for a mixed answer

is_faithful returns one verdict for the whole sentence. Depending on the model, the unsupported clause "lunch will be provided" either drags the entailment score down, so the answer is rejected although its main content is correct (as in the museum example under "Use it"), or is swamped by the long copied part, so the answer passes with a hallucination inside it. Either way, the verdict cannot say what is wrong. Split the answer into atomic claims ("the meeting is on Tuesday at 10 am in room 4"; "lunch will be provided") and check each: the first is entailed, the second neutral.
