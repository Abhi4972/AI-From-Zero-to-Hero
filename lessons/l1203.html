Senior AI engineering, architect and forward deployed roles now share one expectation: given a business problem, you can lay out the whole system, not just the prompt or the retriever. This lesson pulls the rest of the course together into that skill. Everything here applies equally to a team whiteboard session, a client workshop and an architecture review board.

Why this came about

As AI systems became core products, teams and design reviews needed a method for designing them. A repeatable approach, requirements, estimates, design, risks and evaluation, emerged from system design practice and was adapted to models' costs and uncertainties.

In plain words first
Ask, estimate, design, then defend

AI system design follows a repeatable method: clarify requirements and what success means; estimate volumes, tokens, cost and latency to find what actually constrains you; design the flow with code where rules are known and models where they are not; then go through risks (failure, security, privacy) and how you will evaluate and roll it out. The estimates often decide the design.

What is different about AI system design

Classic system design is about data, traffic and failure. AI system design keeps all of that and adds five things that change the architecture:

Difference Consequence for the design
Outputs are probabilistic Quality is a requirement with a number, measured by an eval suite; the design includes how it is measured and gated
Cost scales with tokens, not requests Cost per outcome is a first-class requirement; context size, caching and model routing are architecture decisions
Latency is dominated by model calls The number of sequential model calls is the main latency lever; streaming is a requirement, not a nicety
The system acts on untrusted text Security boundaries follow data flow (untrusted content, private data, external actions), not just network zones
Models change underneath you Version pinning, gateways, evals on upgrade and fallbacks are part of the design, not operations afterthoughts

The method: nine steps, said out loud

Use the same sequence every time. In a 45-minute design discussion, spend roughly the minutes shown; in a client workshop, the same steps take a day.

# Step Minutes Questions to answer
1 Clarify the problem 5 Who are the users, what tasks, which actions are allowed, what is out of scope, what does a wrong answer cost?
2 Define success and constraints 4 Quality metric and target, latency (first token and total), cost per outcome, availability, data residency, compliance
3 Estimate 4 Volume, peak rate, tokens, cost, quotas, storage (Step 1 below)
4 Choose the shape 4 Workflow, single agent, router plus agents, or multi-agent; the least autonomy that works (Lesson 14.62)
5 Design knowledge and tools 7 Retrieval type, structured data, tools with risk levels, permissions, memory
6 Draw the architecture 6 The reference architecture below, adapted; the request path step by step
7 Safety and governance 4 Trifecta check, approvals, guardrails, identity, residency, audit
8 Operate and evolve 4 Evals and release gates, tracing, dashboards, cost controls, rollout and rollback, model upgrades
9 Trade-offs, failure modes and what next 7 What you chose and against what, what breaks first at 10 times the load, what you would build next

Say each step's decision with its reason and trade-off ("a router plus one agent, because 70% of traffic is three fixed intents; the trade-off is maintaining the router, which I would evaluate separately"). Reviewers judge the reasoning more than the boxes.

Step 1 · Estimate before you design

Numbers turn opinions into decisions: whether one deployment's quota is enough, whether caching is optional or essential, whether self-hosting is worth discussing. A back-of-envelope estimate does not need precision; what matters is knowing which numbers matter and to get the order of magnitude right.

import math

def estimate(conversations_per_day: int, turns_per_conversation: float, model_calls_per_turn: float,
             input_tokens_per_call: int, output_tokens_per_call: int, peak_to_average: float,
             price_in: float, price_out: float, cached_share: float, cached_discount: float = 0.9) -> dict:
    """Back-of-envelope numbers for an LLM system. Prices in USD per million tokens."""
    calls_per_day = conversations_per_day * turns_per_conversation * model_calls_per_turn
    avg_calls_per_s = calls_per_day / 86_400
    peak_calls_per_s = avg_calls_per_s * peak_to_average
    in_tok_day = calls_per_day * input_tokens_per_call
    out_tok_day = calls_per_day * output_tokens_per_call
    effective_in = in_tok_day * (1 - cached_share * cached_discount)
    cost_day = (effective_in * price_in + out_tok_day * price_out) / 1e6
    peak_tpm = peak_calls_per_s * 60 * (input_tokens_per_call + output_tokens_per_call)
    return {"calls/day": round(calls_per_day), "peak calls/s": round(peak_calls_per_s, 1),
            "peak tokens/min": round(peak_tpm), "cost/day USD": round(cost_day),
            "cost per conversation USD": round(cost_day / conversations_per_day, 4)}

support = estimate(conversations_per_day=50_000, turns_per_conversation=4, model_calls_per_turn=2.5,
                   input_tokens_per_call=6_000, output_tokens_per_call=250, peak_to_average=3,
                   price_in=3.0, price_out=15.0, cached_share=0.7)
for k, v in support.items():
    print(f"{k:26} {v:,}")
QUOTA_TPM = 2_000_000                                   # per deployment, from your provider's quota page
print(f"deployments needed at peak (quota {QUOTA_TPM:,} TPM): {math.ceil(support['peak tokens/min'] / QUOTA_TPM)}")

chunks = 3_000_000                                      # 600k documents x 5 chunks
dims, bytes_per = 1024, 4
print(f"vector index: {chunks * dims * bytes_per / 1e9:.1f} GB float32, "
      f"{chunks * dims * 1 / 1e9:.1f} GB int8, plus HNSW graph overhead of roughly 10-30%")
What you should see
calls/day                  500,000
peak calls/s               17.4
peak tokens/min            6,510,417
cost/day USD               5,205
cost per conversation USD  0.1041
deployments needed at peak (quota 2,000,000 TPM): 4

Three conclusions fall out immediately. Peak traffic needs about four deployments' worth of token quota, so the design needs a gateway that load-balances across deployments or Regions. Input tokens dominate cost even with 70% cached, so prompt caching and compact tool results are requirements, not optimizations. And the vector index fits on one node even in float32, so a dedicated vector database is not justified by size. The prices and quota here are illustrative; in a real estimate, state your assumptions and move on.

Step 2 · Budget latency along the critical path

Users feel the time to the first visible token. Write the request path as hops with their p95 times, mark which run in parallel, and add up the critical path:

HOPS = [   # (step, p95 milliseconds, runs in parallel with the previous step?)
    ("edge + auth", 30, False),
    ("input guardrail", 120, False),
    ("router (small model)", 350, False),
    ("retrieval: hybrid search", 180, False),
    ("order lookup tool", 220, True),          # runs alongside retrieval
    ("rerank", 150, False),
    ("main model: time to first token", 900, False),
    ("output check (streamed, sentence by sentence)", 60, False),
]

def critical_path(hops) -> int:
    total, group = 0, []
    for name, ms, parallel in hops:
        if parallel and group:
            group.append(ms)
        else:
            total += max(group) if group else 0
            group = [ms]
    return total + (max(group) if group else 0)

budget = 2_000                                          # ms to first visible token for a chat turn
t = critical_path(HOPS)
print(f"time to first token on the critical path: {t} ms (budget {budget} ms)")
print("biggest hops:", sorted(((ms, n) for n, ms, _ in HOPS), reverse=True)[:3])
print("after moving the router to rules and caching the prompt prefix:",
      critical_path([(n, {"router (small model)": 5, "main model: time to first token": 550}.get(n, ms), p)
                     for n, ms, p in HOPS]), "ms")
What you should see
time to first token on the critical path: 1830 ms (budget 2000 ms)
biggest hops: [(900, 'main model: time to first token'), (350, 'router (small model)'), (220, 'order lookup tool')]
after moving the router to rules and caching the prompt prefix: 1135 ms

The design fits the budget with little margin, and the two cheapest wins are architectural: route the common intents with rules instead of a model call, and keep the main model's prompt prefix stable so caching shortens time to first token. Running the order lookup alongside retrieval already removed one hop. For voice the budget is under a second to first audio (Lesson 14.129), which usually forces a different shape: fewer hops and a faster model.

Step 3 · Compose availability

Every component in series multiplies its availability; alternatives in parallel multiply their failure probabilities. Model providers are often the least available component, so the design question is what happens when one is down:

def chain(*availabilities: float) -> float:
    """Components in series: all must be up."""
    a = 1.0
    for x in availabilities:
        a *= x
    return a

def redundant(*availabilities: float) -> float:
    """Independent alternatives in parallel: at least one must be up."""
    down = 1.0
    for x in availabilities:
        down *= (1 - x)
    return 1 - down

minutes_per_month = 30 * 24 * 60
designs = {
    "single provider": chain(0.9995, 0.999, 0.995, 0.999),        # edge, orchestrator, model API, vector store
    "model failover to a second provider": chain(0.9995, 0.999, redundant(0.995, 0.995), 0.999),
    "failover + degraded mode when search is down": chain(0.9995, 0.999, redundant(0.995, 0.995),
                                                          redundant(0.999, 0.99)),   # fall back to FAQ answers
}
for name, a in designs.items():
    print(f"{name:46} {a:.4%}  ~{(1 - a) * minutes_per_month:5.0f} min/month down")
What you should see
single provider                                99.2514%  ~  323 min/month down
model failover to a second provider            99.7477%  ~  109 min/month down
failover + degraded mode when search is down   99.8466%  ~   66 min/month down

A second provider behind the gateway removes most of the downtime; a degraded mode (answer common questions from a cached FAQ when search is down, switch to read-only when tools fail) removes more. The formula assumes independent failures, which is optimistic: two providers can share a cloud Region or an upstream dependency, so check where your fallbacks actually run. The fallback model must also pass your evals (Lesson 14.93): availability bought with a much worse model is a quality incident.

Exercises

Predict before you run

10,000 conversations a day, 4 model calls each, 3,000 input and 300 output tokens per call, at illustrative prices of $3 and $15 per million tokens. What is the daily model cost? (Solution P1.)

Break it on purpose

Design the system without a latency budget per step. The agent takes 14 seconds. Where do you cut? (Solution B1.)

Debugging exercise

A design proposal jumps to "use a vector DB and GPT" in the first minute. What step of the method was skipped? (Solution D1.)

Make it your own

Estimate peak requests per second, tokens per second and model concurrency for ShopKart's assistant on a sale day with 5 times normal traffic. (Solution M1.)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Estimate before building

Calls: 40,000. Input: 120M tokens × $3/M = $360. Output: 12M × $15/M = $180. About $540 a day before caching, which could cut most of the input cost (Lesson 11.23).

SolutionB1 · Budgets per component

Without per-step budgets, there is no target to cut against. Allocate a budget to each step (retrieval, model, tools), measure, and cut the largest overruns first.

SolutionD1 · Requirements first

Clarifying requirements and estimates: users, traffic, data, latency, quality and risk. The method is requirements, estimates, design, deep dives, trade-offs.

SolutionM1 · Peak estimates

Daily calls times 5, divided over the busiest hours, gives peak calls per second; multiply by tokens per call for tokens per second, and by call duration for concurrency. Compare with provider quotas.

Check your understanding

Explain the mechanismHow do you approach designing an AI system from a blank page?

Worked answer. Clarify the task, users and constraints; estimate traffic, tokens, cost and latency; sketch the architecture (interfaces, models, retrieval, tools, state); deep-dive on the hardest parts (evaluation, safety, scaling); discuss trade-offs and failure modes; and propose how to measure success.

A plausible wrong answer. "Name the best model and a vector database." That skips the reasoning a design needs.

Follow-ups. Which estimate matters most for an agent?

Debug a failureYour cost estimate was 3 times too low after launch. What did you likely miss?

Worked answer. Calls per task (retries, tool loops), context growth over a conversation, output length, and traffic peaks. Measure tokens per task in production and update the model.

A plausible wrong answer. "Prices went up." Usage usually did.

Follow-ups. How would you set guardrails on cost?

Defend a designOptimize cost or quality first?

Worked answer. Quality to the required bar first, then cost, while estimating cost early so the design is viable. Most cost wins (caching, routing, smaller models for easy steps) come after quality is measured.

A plausible wrong answer. "Cheapest model first, then improve." You may never reach the bar.
