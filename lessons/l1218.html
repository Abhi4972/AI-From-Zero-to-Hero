Why this came about

Design methods are easiest to learn by seeing them applied. These worked designs come from the kinds of systems teams built in 2024 to 2026.

In plain words first
Seeing the method applied

These worked designs apply the system-design method to complete problems, start to finish: the questions asked, the assumptions written down, the estimates, the design, the risks with their controls, and the evaluation and rollout plan. Read each one for the reasoning behind the choices, not just the final diagram.

Worked design 1 · A support assistant at 50,000 conversations a day

  • Clarify: order status, cancellations of unshipped orders, refunds (automatic up to INR 2,000, approval above), policy questions; English, Hindi and Hinglish; chat and voice later.
  • Success: 60% resolved without a person; under 1% wrong resolutions; p95 first token under 2 seconds; under INR 10 per resolved conversation; 99.7% availability.
  • Estimate: Step 1's numbers: about 17 model calls per second at peak, four deployments of quota, prompt caching essential.
  • Shape: an intent router sends the frequent, fixed intents (order status, FAQ) to deterministic flows, policy questions to cited RAG, and the rest to one agent with a step budget. A multi-agent design adds cost and handoff errors without a measured need.
  • Tools: read tools through an MCP gateway with the customer ID taken from the session; cancel and refund as durable workflows with idempotency keys; refunds above the limit pause for approval.
  • Safety: customer messages and reviews are untrusted; the agent holds private data and can act, so outbound actions are limited to the customer's own orders, links in answers are allowlisted, and every write is checked in code.
  • Operate: eval set by intent with pass^3 gates in CI; online judges on 5% of traffic; dashboards for resolution rate, handoff rate, p95 and cost per resolved conversation; canary releases.
  • Trade-offs: the router is a component to maintain and evaluate, but it removes a model call from 70% of traffic; durable workflows add infrastructure, but a lost refund is worse.
  • At 10 times the load: token quota and cost first, then the human desk's capacity for handoffs; the answer is more caching, smaller models for more intents, and moving automation per intent as quality allows.

Worked design 2 · A multi-tenant enterprise knowledge assistant

  • Clarify: 200 client companies, each with its own documents in SharePoint, Google Drive and wikis; users must only see what they could open in the source system.
  • Success: answer accuracy over 85% on each tenant's labeled set, zero cross-tenant or cross-permission leaks, answers with citations or an explicit "not found", fresh within an hour of a document change.
  • Key decisions: permissions enforced inside retrieval with the user's groups from the identity provider, synced hourly; separate indexes for the largest tenants and a mandatory tenant_id filter for small ones; every cache key includes tenant and user; versioned indexes behind aliases so re-chunking is a side-by-side switch.
  • Failure modes to design for: stale ACLs after someone changes teams, derived artifacts (summaries, FAQs) losing the source's permissions, selective filters returning nothing from post-filtered vector search (Lesson 14.145), and documents that fail to parse silently.
  • Trade-offs: per-tenant indexes isolate well but cost more to operate; shared indexes with filters are cheaper but make every filter bug a potential leak, which is why the permission test runs in CI.

Worked design 3 · A text-to-SQL analytics agent

  • Shape: a semantic layer per business domain (metrics, synonyms, sample values, verified queries) is the core component, not the model; the agent selects the domain, generates SQL against it, validates and runs it read-only under the user's role, and returns the SQL, the assumptions and a chart.
  • Numbers that matter: warehouse cost per query and a row limit, not tokens; a query timeout; caching of frequent questions.
  • Evaluation: verified question and result pairs per domain, compared by result set (Lesson 14.137).
  • Platform fit: Genie on Databricks, data agents in BigQuery, or your own agent over dbt or Cube metrics elsewhere.
  • Failure modes: wrong tables in large schemas (narrow by domain), wrong metric definitions (one definition, owned by the data team), ambiguous questions answered confidently (ask, or state assumptions).

Worked design 4 · A document-processing pipeline

  • Shape: asynchronous and batch, not chat. Documents arrive on a queue; each passes classify, parse, extract to a schema, validate against business rules and route: straight through when every field is confident and valid, to a reviewer otherwise.
  • Numbers: documents per day and pages per document set throughput and cost; batch inferencelater · 14.138 APIs cut model cost; reviewer capacity sets how low the straight-through rate can be before the backlog grows.
  • Key decisions: prebuilt extractors for standard formats, a multimodal model for the long tail; every field carries its source page and confidence; idempotent processing so a retried document is not booked twice.
  • Evaluation: field-level accuracy against reviewer corrections, monitored per document source because a vendor's template change breaks one source at a time.

Exercises

Predict before you run

For a design question "an AI assistant for an e-commerce help center", what are the first three clarifying questions you ask? (Solution P1.)

Break it on purpose

Design the assistant as one agent with every tool and no retrieval evaluation. What would a design reviewer probe? (Solution B1.)

Debugging exercise

Your design's latency estimate is 2 seconds, but the components you listed add up to 6. How do you fix the design? (Solution D1.)

Make it your own

Sketch a design for an internal "ask the policy documents" assistant for 5,000 employees, with estimates, components, evaluation and risks. (Solution M1.)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Clarify first

Who the users are and what tasks matter most; traffic and latency expectations; what actions the assistant may take and what data it may see. The answers shape every later choice.

SolutionB1 · Gaps a reviewer finds

How you would evaluate it, handle wrong answers, protect data and actions, scale, and control cost. A design without evaluation and safety is incomplete.

SolutionD1 · Consistent estimates

Parallelize independent steps, stream, cache, and remove or shrink steps; then recompute the budget so the numbers agree.

SolutionM1 · A worked design

Hybrid RAG over documents with permissions, a gateway, caching, estimates of queries and tokens, an eval set from real questions, and risks: stale documents, access leaks, hallucination.

Check your understanding

Explain the mechanismWalk through a worked AI system design.

Worked answer. Clarify requirements; estimate load, tokens, cost and latency; propose components (interface, gateway, orchestration, retrieval, tools, memory, guardrails, observability); deep-dive into the hardest part; cover evaluation, failure modes and security; and close with trade-offs and next steps.

A plausible wrong answer. "Start with the model choice." The model is one decision among many.

Follow-ups. Which part do reviewers probe most?

Debug a failureA reviewer says your design will not scale. How do you respond?

Worked answer. Ask which part, then identify the bottleneck with numbers (quotas, database load, fan-out), propose a change (caching, queues, sharding, smaller models), and state the trade-off.

A plausible wrong answer. "It will scale with the cloud." Show the numbers.

Follow-ups. How would you estimate quota needs?

Defend a designAgent or workflow: how do you decide?

Worked answer. The simplest structure that meets requirements, with reasons: workflows for fixed processes, agents where paths vary, and explicit control points for risky actions. Reviewers reward justified choices.

A plausible wrong answer. "Agents, because they are modern." Justify, do not follow fashion.

Follow-ups. What would change your choice?

Check your understanding

Q1What comes first in a system design walkthrough?

Requirements and numbers come before boxes: load, tokens, cost and latency decide what the architecture has to be.

Q2A reviewer says your design will not scale. What is the best response?

Name the bottleneck (quota, database load, fan-out), then the fix (caching, queues, smaller models) and what it costs.
