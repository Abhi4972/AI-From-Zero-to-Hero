In plain words first
The idea in one line

1992: TD-Gammon beat human champions at backgammon with pure TD. 2016: AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol. 2017: AlphaZero dominated chess, shogi, and Go from scratch. 2025: DeepSeek-R1 showed that RL against an automatic answer checker, with GRPO in place of PPO, can teach a language model to reason. Games are the benchmark that drives every breakthrough in this phase.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
MCTS "Tree search with learned net" Monte Carlo Tree Search; UCB1/PUCT selection with learned $(p, v)$ priors.
AlphaZero "Self-play + MCTS" Policy-value net trained to match MCTS visits and game outcome.
MuZero "Learned-model AlphaZero" Same loop but in latent space via learned dynamics.
GRPO "Critic-free PPO" Group Relative Policy Optimization; REINFORCE with group-mean baseline + KL.
PUCT "AlphaZero's UCB" $Q + c \cdot p \cdot \sqrt{N} / (1 + N_a)$ — balances value estimate with prior.
Self-play "Agent vs past self" Standard for zero-sum; symmetric training signal.
League play "Population-based self-play" Past + current + exploiters sampled as opponents.
Verifier reward "Verifiable RL" Reward comes from a deterministic checker (tests pass, answer matches).
Process reward "PRM" Scores each reasoning step, not just the final answer.

The problem

In plain words first

Games are where reinforcement learning made its name, because a game gives a clear win or loss and can be replayed endlessly. Game programs like AlphaZero combine three things: a network that guesses good moves and who is winning, a search that looks ahead to improve those guesses, and self-play to generate endless practice. Reasoning models for maths and code use a simpler cousin of this loop: the model writes several answers to the same problem, a checker marks each one right or wrong, and the model is nudged towards the answers that did better than their group's average. That method is GRPO. This lesson runs the lesson's GRPO in miniature, computes exactly when a group carries a learning signal, runs tree search with OpenSpiel on tic-tac-toe, and collects one real GRPO step with a small language model. No model is trained or fine-tuned.

Games have everything RL wants. Clean reward (win/loss). Infinite episodes (self-play resets). Perfect simulation (the game is the simulator). Discrete or small continuous action spaces. Multi-agent structure that forces adversarial robustness.

And games are how every major RL breakthrough was tested. TD-Gammon (backgammon, 1992). Atari-DQN (2013). AlphaGo (2016). AlphaZero (2017). OpenAI Five (Dota 2, 2019). AlphaStar (StarCraft II, 2019). MuZero (learned model, 2019). AlphaTensor (matrix multiplication, 2022). AlphaDev (sorting algorithms, 2023). DeepSeek-R1 (math reasoning, 2025) — the latest demonstration that game-RL techniques work on text.

This capstone surveys the three landmark architectures — AlphaZero, MuZero, and GRPO — through a single unifying lens: self-play + search + policy improvement. Each generalizes the previous; GRPO keeps AlphaZero's loop of attempting, scoring and improving but drops the search and the self-play: tokens are the actions and an answer checker supplies the win signal.

The idea

The unifying loop.

while True:
    trajectory = self_play(current_policy, search)     # play game against self
    policy_target = search.improved_policy(trajectory) # search improves raw policy
    policy_net.update(policy_target, value_target)     # supervised on search output

AlphaZero (2017). Silver et al. Given a game (chess, shogi, Go) with known rules:

  • Policy-value network: one tower fθ​(s)→(p,v). p is a prior over legal moves. v is the expected game outcome.
  • Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS): at each move, expand a tree of possible continuations. Use (p,v) as the prior + bootstrap. Select nodes by UCB (PUCT): a∗=argmaxa​Q(s,a)+c⋅p(a∣s)⋅N(s)​/(1+N(s,a)).
  • Self-play: play games agent-vs-agent. At move t, the MCTS visit distribution πt​ becomes the policy training target.
  • Loss: L=(v−z)2−π⋅logp+c⋅∥θ∥2. z is the game outcome (+1 / 0 / -1).

Zero human knowledge. Zero handcrafted heuristics. A single recipe that mastered chess, shogi, and Go after a few tens of millions of self-play games each.

MuZero (2019). Schrittwieser et al. Removes the requirement that the rules are known.

  • Instead of a fixed environment, learn a latent dynamics model (h,g,f):
  • h(s): encode observation to latent state.
  • g(slatent​,a): predict next latent state + reward.
  • f(slatent​): predict policy prior + value.
  • MCTS runs in the learned latent space. Same search, same training loop.
  • Works on Go, chess, shogi and Atari — one algorithm, no rule knowledge.

Stochastic MuZero (2022). Adds stochastic dynamics and chance nodes; extends to backgammon-class games.

Muesli (2021), Gumbel MuZero (2022). Better policy improvement with fewer simulations; Gumbel MuZero still learns well with as few as two simulations per move.

GRPO (2024-2025). DeepSeek-R1 recipe. Same AlphaZero-shaped loop, applied to language-model reasoning:

  • "Game": answer a math / coding / reasoning problem. "Win" = verifier (test case passes, numerical answer matches) returns 1.
  • Policy: the LLM. Actions: tokens. State: prompt + response-so-far.
  • No critic (PPO-style Vϕ​). Instead, for each prompt, sample G completions from the policy. Compute reward for each. Use the group-relative advantage Ai​=(ri​−meanr​)/stdr​ as the signal for REINFORCE-style update.
  • KL penalty to reference policy to prevent drift (like RLHF).
  • Full loss:

LGRPO​(θ)=−Eq,{oi​}​[G1​∑i​Ai​⋅logπθ​(oi​∣q)]+β⋅KL(πθ​∥πref​)

No learned reward model, no critic, no MCTS. A rule-based verifier supplies the reward, the group-relative baseline replaces the critic, and the search step is dropped. In DeepSeekMath it beat the instruction-tuned starting point on maths benchmarks while using less memory than PPO, since no value network is trained.

The R1 recipe in full. DeepSeek-R1 (DeepSeek 2025) is two models in one paper:

  • R1-Zero. Start from the DeepSeek-V3 base model. No SFT. Apply GRPO directly with two reward components: accuracy reward (rule-based — did the final answer parse to the correct number / did the code pass unit tests) and format reward (did the completion wrap its chain-of-thought in <think>…</think> tags). Over thousands of steps, average response length grows from ~100 to ~10,000 tokens and math benchmark scores climb to near-o1-preview levels. The model learns to reason from scratch. The downside: its chains of thought are often unreadable, mix languages, and lack stylistic polish.
  • R1. Fix R1-Zero's readability problems with a four-stage pipeline: 1. Cold-start SFT. Collect a few thousand long-CoT demonstrations with clean formatting. Supervised-finetune the base model on them. This gives a readable starting point. 2. Reasoning-oriented GRPO. Apply GRPO with the accuracy+format rewards plus a language-consistency reward to prevent code-switching. 3. Rejection sampling + SFT round 2. Sample ~600K reasoning trajectories from the RL checkpoint, keep only those with correct final answers and readable CoT, and combine with ~200K non-reasoning SFT examples (writing, QA, self-cognition). Fine-tune the base again. 4. Full-spectrum GRPO. One more RL round covering both reasoning (rule-based rewards) and general alignment (helpfulness/harmlessness preference-based rewards).

The result matches o1 on AIME and MATH-500 at open weights, and is small enough to distill. The same paper also releases six distilled dense models (Qwen-1.5B through Llama-70B) by SFT'ing on R1's reasoning traces — no RL at the student. In the paper, distilling R1 into a 32B model beat running large-scale RL on that 32B model directly.

Why GRPO instead of PPO for reasoning. Three reasons in the DeepSeekMath paper (Feb 2024): (1) no value network to train, halving memory; (2) the group baseline naturally handles the sparse end-of-trajectory reward that reasoning tasks produce; (3) per-prompt normalization makes advantages comparable across problems of wildly different difficulty, which PPO's single critic cannot.

Search-free vs search-based. Games have branched:

  • Perfect-information games with long horizons (Go, chess): still search-based. AlphaZero / MuZero dominate.
  • LLM reasoning: mostly search-free training (the R1 paper reports that its MCTS attempts did not pay off); GRPO-style RL on full rollouts, with parallel sampling and best-of-N for inference compute. Process reward models (PRMs) are one route to step-level search.

The same idea as a formula

AlphaZero. A network fθ​(s)=(p,v) gives move probabilities and a value. MCTS picks moves inside the tree by PUCT,

a∗=argamax​Q(s,a)+cp(a∣s)1+N(s,a)N(s)​​

and after the search the visit counts π(a)∝N(s,a) are a better policy than p. Training makes the network imitate the search and predict the result z: L=(z−v)2−π⊤logp+c∥θ∥2.

GRPO. For a prompt q, sample a group o1​,…,oG​, score them r1​,…,rG​, and use

Ai​=std(r)ri​−mean(r)​

for every token of oi​, inside PPO's clipped ratio objective (Lesson 9.8), optionally with a KL term to a reference model. The advantages of a group always sum to zero.

When a group carries no signal. With a 0/1 checker and success probability p, a group of G is all-correct or all-wrong with probability

pG+(1−p)G

and then every Ai​=0. Dividing by std(r)=p^​(1−p^​)​ also gives prompts the model almost always solves (or almost never solves) more weight than their true gradient p(1−p), the point behind Dr. GRPO, which removes this scaling.

Worked example
A group by hand

Eight answers to one problem, three verified correct: r=(1,0,0,1,0,1,0,0).

  • Mean =3/8=0.375; standard deviation =0.375×0.625​=0.484.
  • Correct answers: A=(1−0.375)/0.484=+1.29. Wrong answers: A=(0−0.375)/0.484=−0.77.
  • Check: 3×1.29−5×0.77=3.87−3.85≈0; the group's advantages cancel, so the group mean acts as the baseline a critic would otherwise provide.

A group with no signal. A problem the model solves 5% of the time, G=8: 0.958+0.058=0.663, so two groups in three are all wrong and teach nothing. With G=16: 0.9516=0.440.

PUCT by hand. c=1.5, N(s)=100, so N(s)​=10. Move A: Q=0.5, p=0.3, visited 10 times: 0.5+1.5×0.3×10/11=0.91. Move B: Q=0.4, p=0.2, visited twice: 0.4+1.5×0.2×10/3=1.40. The search explores B next, although its value estimate is lower, because it has been tried so rarely.

Step 1 · a tiny verifier environment

The code in code/main.py implements GRPO in miniature: a bandit with multiple groups of samples. The algorithm is the same as on an LLM; only the policy and environment are simpler. It teaches the loss and the group-relative advantage, which is the 2024 innovation from DeepSeekMath.

QUESTIONS = [
    {"prompt": "q1", "correct": 3},
    {"prompt": "q2", "correct": 1},
]

def verify(prompt_idx, answer_token):
    return 1.0 if answer_token == QUESTIONS[prompt_idx]["correct"] else 0.0

In real GRPO the verifier runs unit tests or checks math equality.

Step 2 · policy: softmax over K answer tokens per prompt

def policy_probs(theta, p_idx):
    return softmax(theta[p_idx])

Equivalent to the final-layer output of an LLM conditioned on a prompt.

Step 3 · group sampling and group-relative advantage

def grpo_step(theta, p_idx, G=8, beta=0.01, lr=0.1, rng=None):
    probs = policy_probs(theta, p_idx)
    samples = [sample(probs, rng) for _ in range(G)]
    rewards = [verify(p_idx, s) for s in samples]
    mean_r = sum(rewards) / G
    std_r = stddev(rewards) + 1e-8
    advs = [(r - mean_r) / std_r for r in rewards]

    for a, A in zip(samples, advs):
        grad = onehot(a) - probs
        for i in range(len(probs)):
            theta[p_idx][i] += lr * A * grad[i]
    # KL penalty: pull theta toward reference
    for i in range(len(probs)):
        theta[p_idx][i] -= beta * (theta[p_idx][i] - reference[p_idx][i])

The group-relative advantage is the 2024 DeepSeek trick. No critic needed. The "baseline" is the group mean, and normalization uses group std.

Step 4 · compare to REINFORCE baseline (value-free)

Same setup, plain REINFORCE with one sample per update and no baseline. GRPO here draws 8 samples per update, so the comparison in main.py is not at equal compute (the run after Step 5).

Step 5 · observe entropy and KL

Same diagnostics as RLHF: mean KL to reference, policy entropy, reward-over-time. Once these stabilize, training is done.

When a group carries a learning signal

Exact sums over the number of correct answers in a group: for success probability p, the share of groups with no signal and GRPO's expected push on the correct answer, with and without dividing by the group's standard deviation, compared with the true gradient p(1−p).

import numpy as np
from math import comb

# One prompt, a verifier that returns 1 or 0, and a policy that answers correctly with probability p.
# A group of G answers contains k correct ones, k ~ Binomial(G, p). Everything below is an exact sum over k.
def group_stats(p, G, scale_std=True):
    no_signal, step = 0.0, 0.0
    for k in range(G + 1):
        w = comb(G, k) * p ** k * (1 - p) ** (G - k)
        if k in (0, G): no_signal += w; continue                # all answers equal: every advantage is 0
        mean = k / G; std = np.sqrt(mean * (1 - mean)) if scale_std else 1.0
        a_pos, a_neg = (1 - mean) / std, -mean / std              # advantage of a correct / a wrong answer
        # GRPO's push on the correct answer's logit: (1/G) sum_i A_i (1[a_i correct] - p)
        step += w * (k * a_pos * (1 - p) + (G - k) * a_neg * (-p)) / G
    return no_signal, step

print("exact GRPO signal for one prompt; true gradient of the success rate is p(1 - p)")
print("   p   | true grad | G = 8: groups with no signal | step, std-scaled | step, no std | G = 16: no signal | std-scaled")
for p in (0.01, 0.05, 0.2, 0.5, 0.8, 0.95, 0.99):
    n8, s8 = group_stats(p, 8); _, u8 = group_stats(p, 8, scale_std=False); n16, s16 = group_stats(p, 16)
    print(f"  {p:<4} | {p * (1 - p):>9.4f} | {n8:>28.0%} | {s8:>16.4f} | {u8:>12.4f} | {n16:>17.0%} | {s16:>10.4f}")
print()

Reading this output

Silent groups. When the model almost always fails a prompt, most groups are all wrong and carry no gradient at all: 92% of groups at p=0.01 and 66% at p=0.05 with G=8. The same happens symmetrically for prompts it almost always solves. Doubling the group to 16 helps (44% at p=0.05) but costs twice the sampling. This is why DAPO filters out such groups and samples more prompts until the batch is full, and why training sets are chosen so the model succeeds sometimes but not always.

Std scaling. Without it, the expected push is exactly (1−1/G)p(1−p) (0.2188 = 0.875 × 0.25), proportional to the true gradient. Dividing by the group's standard deviation multiplies the push by 1.86 at p=0.5 but by 2.47 at p=0.05 or 0.95, so very hard and very easy prompts get about a third more weight than balanced ones. Whether that is good is debated: it helps rare successes count, but Dr. GRPO argues it biases training, and TRL exposes it as an option (scale_rewards).

Pitfalls

  • Reward hacking via verifier gaming. GRPO inherits RLHF's risk: if the verifier is wrong or exploitable, the LLM will find the exploit. Robust verifiers (multiple test cases, formal proofs) matter.
  • Group size too small. Variance of the group-mean baseline goes like 1/G (its standard error like 1/G​). Below G=4, the advantage signal is noisy; standard choice is G=8 to 64.
  • Length bias. LLM completions of different lengths have different log-probabilities. Normalize by token count, or use sequence-level log-prob, or truncate to max length.
  • Pure self-play cycles. AlphaZero-style training can get stuck in dominance loops on general-sum games. Mitigated by diverse opponent pools (league play, Lesson 9.10).
  • Search-policy mismatch. AlphaZero trains the policy to mimic search output. If the policy net is too small to represent the search's distribution, training stalls.
  • Compute floor. MuZero / AlphaZero need massive compute. A single ablation is often hundreds of GPU-hours. Miniature demos exist (e.g., AlphaZero on Connect Four) for learning.
  • Verifier coverage. Unit tests that pass for a buggy solution reinforce the bug. Design verifiers that catch edge cases.

Tree search with OpenSpiel

Tic-tac-toe solved exactly, then OpenSpiel's MCTS bot with random rollouts plays a perfect player at four search budgets, and its visit counts are read at one tactical position. A few seconds.

import numpy as np, pyspiel
from functools import lru_cache
from open_spiel.python.algorithms import mcts

game = pyspiel.load_game("tic_tac_toe")

@lru_cache(maxsize=None)
def solve(hist):                                   # exact game value for the player to move (+1 win, 0 draw, -1 loss)
    s = game.new_initial_state()
    for a in hist: s.apply_action(a)
    if s.is_terminal(): return -abs(s.returns()[0])  # the previous player just won, or it is a draw
    return max(-solve(hist + (a,)) for a in s.legal_actions())
def perfect_moves(s):
    h = tuple(s.history()); best = max(-solve(h + (a,)) for a in s.legal_actions())
    return [a for a in s.legal_actions() if -solve(h + (a,)) == best]

def play(n_sims, mcts_first, seed):
    rng = np.random.RandomState(seed)
    bot = mcts.MCTSBot(game, uct_c=2.0, max_simulations=n_sims,
                       evaluator=mcts.RandomRolloutEvaluator(n_rollouts=1, random_state=rng), random_state=rng)
    s = game.new_initial_state()
    while not s.is_terminal():
        mcts_turn = (s.current_player() == 0) == mcts_first
        s.apply_action(bot.step(s) if mcts_turn else rng.choice(perfect_moves(s)))
    return s.returns()[0 if mcts_first else 1]

v = solve(())
print(f"tic-tac-toe solved exactly by searching all {solve.cache_info().currsize:,} move sequences: value of the empty board "
      f"for the first player = {v:.0f} (a draw with perfect play)")
print("MCTS with random rollouts (OpenSpiel) against a perfect player, 20 games per row (10 as X, 10 as O)")
print("  simulations per move | MCTS loses | draws")
for n in (10, 50, 200, 1000):
    res = [play(n, mcts_first=(g % 2 == 0), seed=g) for g in range(20)]
    print(f"  {n:>20} | {sum(r < 0 for r in res):>10} | {sum(r == 0 for r in res):>5}")

# Search as policy improvement: the visit counts at one position become AlphaZero's training target
s = game.new_initial_state()
for a in (4, 0, 2):                                # X centre, O corner, X opposite corner: O must block at 6
    s.apply_action(a)
print("\nposition: X in the centre (cell 4) and top-right corner (2), O in the top-left (0); O to move. Only bottom-left (6) avoids a loss:",
      perfect_moves(s))
for n in (20, 100, 1000):
    rng = np.random.RandomState(0)
    bot = mcts.MCTSBot(game, uct_c=2.0, max_simulations=n, evaluator=mcts.RandomRolloutEvaluator(1, rng),
                       random_state=rng)
    root = bot.mcts_search(s); total = sum(c.explore_count for c in root.children)
    visits = {c.action: c.explore_count / total for c in root.children}
    print(f"  {n:>5} simulations: share of visits on cell 6 = {visits[6]:.0%}; most visited cell "
          f"{max(visits, key=visits.get)}")

With 10 simulations per move, MCTS loses 12 of 20 games to the perfect player; with 50 it loses 5, with 200 3, and with 1,000 only 1. Search turns a policy that knows nothing (random rollouts, no network) into a strong player, at a cost that grows with the budget.

At the tactical position, the only move that does not lose is cell 6. With 20 simulations it gets 42% of the visits, with 100 and 1,000 it gets 66%, and it is always the most visited. That visit distribution is the "improved policy" AlphaZero trains its network towards: much sharper than the uniform prior the search started from. It does not reach 100% because UCT keeps exploring and random rollouts are poor judges of positions (the random opponent inside a rollout blunders), which is the weakness AlphaZero removes by replacing rollouts with a learned value network.

One GRPO step with a real model

Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, the model in TRL's GRPO quick start, writes 8 answers to each of 5 maths problems; a rule-based checker scores them and the group advantages are computed. No update is made. About two to three minutes on a laptop CPU.

import re, time, torch, transformers
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

# One GRPO data-collection step with a real model, and no update: sample a group per prompt, verify, compute advantages.
torch.manual_seed(0); torch.set_grad_enabled(False); transformers.logging.disable_progress_bar()
name = "Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct"                       # the model in TRL's GRPO quick start
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(name); tok.padding_side = "left"
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(name, dtype=torch.float32).eval()
problems = [("What is 17 + 25?", 42), ("What is 23 * 19?", 437),
            ("Pens cost 3 dollars each. Tom buys 7 pens and pays with a 50 dollar note. How much change does he get?", 29),
            ("If x + 2x + 3x = 48, what is x?", 8),
            ("What is 1234 * 5678?", 7006652)]
def verify(text, answer):                                 # rule-based reward: the last number in the reply must be right
    nums = re.findall(r"-?\d[\d,]*\.?\d*", text.replace(",", ""))
    return 1.0 if nums and abs(float(nums[-1]) - answer) < 1e-6 else 0.0
G, t = 8, time.time(); rows = []
for q, ans in problems:
    msgs = [{"role": "user", "content": q + " Think briefly, then give the final number last."}]
    ids = tok.apply_chat_template(msgs, add_generation_prompt=True, return_tensors="pt", return_dict=True)
    out = model.generate(**ids, do_sample=True, temperature=1.0, max_new_tokens=100, num_return_sequences=G,
                         pad_token_id=tok.eos_token_id)
    texts = tok.batch_decode(out[:, ids["input_ids"].shape[1]:], skip_special_tokens=True)
    lengths = (out[:, ids["input_ids"].shape[1]:] != tok.eos_token_id).sum(1).tolist()
    rows.append((q, [verify(x, ans) for x in texts], lengths))
print(f"{name}: {G} sampled replies per problem, temperature 1.0 ({time.time() - t:.0f} s)\n")
print(f"{'problem':<42} rewards (1 = verified correct) | cut off | mean | std  | advantage of a right / wrong reply")
no_signal = 0
for q, r, L in rows:
    r_t = torch.tensor(r); m, s = r_t.mean().item(), r_t.std(unbiased=False).item()
    adv = "no signal: every reply got the same reward" if s == 0 else f"{(1 - m) / s:+.2f} / {(0 - m) / s:+.2f}"
    no_signal += s == 0
    print(f"{q[:40]:<42} {' '.join(str(int(x)) for x in r):<30} | {sum(l >= 100 for l in L):>7} | {m:.2f} | {s:.2f} | {adv}")
print(f"\nprompts that would contribute no gradient this step: {no_signal} of {len(rows)}")
mean_len = lambda ok: [l for _, r, L in rows for x, l in zip(r, L) if x == ok]
right, wrong = mean_len(1.0), mean_len(0.0)
print(f"reply length in tokens: correct replies {sum(right) / max(len(right), 1):.0f} on average, wrong replies "
      f"{sum(wrong) / max(len(wrong), 1):.0f}; {sum(l >= 100 for l in wrong)} of {len(wrong)} wrong replies hit the "
      f"100-token limit before giving an answer")

Reading this output

Only two of the five problems produce a learning signal. "17 + 25" is too easy: all eight answers are correct, every advantage is 0. "23 × 19" (5 of 8) and the pens problem (3 of 8) are in the useful range: correct answers get +0.77 or +1.29, wrong ones −1.29 or −0.77, and the group's advantages cancel. The two that fail completely fail for different reasons. For "x + 2x + 3x = 48", all eight answers were cut off at the 100-token limit before stating a number, so the checker read an intermediate number: the model may well know the answer, but the length limit turned every attempt into a 0. For "1234 × 5678" four answers were cut off and four gave a wrong product.

Overall, correct answers averaged 35 tokens and wrong ones 91, and 19 of the 24 wrong answers hit the limit. Two practical lessons follow. The completion-length limit is part of the reward: too short, and the reward punishes the long reasoning you want to grow (R1 trained with completions of thousands of tokens; DAPO adds a soft penalty for overlong answers instead of a hard zero). And prompt difficulty decides how much of the batch is wasted: here 3 of 5 prompts would contribute nothing.

from datasets import load_dataset
from trl import GRPOConfig, GRPOTrainer
import re

def correct(completions, solution, **kwargs):            # 1.0 if the last number matches (trl.rewards.accuracy_reward
                                                         # also handles LaTeX answers)
    out = []
    for c, sol in zip(completions, solution):
        nums = re.findall(r"-?\d+\.?\d*", c[0]["content"].replace(",", ""))
        out.append(1.0 if nums and nums[-1] == str(sol).strip() else 0.0)
    return out

trainer = GRPOTrainer(
    model="Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct",
    reward_funcs=correct,
    args=GRPOConfig(output_dir="./grpo", num_generations=8, max_completion_length=1024,
                    beta=0.0,                # no KL term, TRL's default
                    loss_type="dapo"),       # DAPO's token-level loss
    train_dataset=load_dataset("trl-lib/DeepMath-103K", split="train"),
)
trainer.train()

What's current (2024–2026)
  • RL with verifiable rewards. Reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1, Qwen3 and open replications) are trained with GRPO-family algorithms against answer checkers and unit tests; DAPO's changes (decoupled clipping, filtering groups with no signal, token-level loss, overlong handling), Dr. GRPO and GSPO's sequence-level ratios are common refinements.
  • Frameworks. TRL's GRPOTrainer, verl and OpenRLHF run these pipelines, with vLLM for the sampling step.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • AlphaZero: network guesses (p,v), MCTS improves them, the network learns to imitate the search.
  • GRPO: a group of answers per prompt, advantage = reward minus the group mean (over the group's std); no critic.
  • Groups with equal rewards teach nothing: probability pG+(1−p)G for a 0/1 checker.
  • Std scaling up-weights very easy and very hard prompts; length limits silently turn long answers into zero reward.
  • The checker is the whole reward: if it can be fooled, the model will learn to fool it.

medium30 minresearch

GRPO trains a language model with reinforcement learning and no value network. For each prompt it samples a group of G answers and scores each answer. The baseline of an answer is the mean reward of its group. The update uses the clipped surrogate of PPO and a KL penalty toward a frozen reference model.

Task

Write three functions.

  1. group_advantages(rewards, eps=1e-8) returns the group-relative advantages.
  2. kl_k3(logp, ref_logp) returns the k3 estimate of the KL divergence for each token.
  3. grpo_objective(logp, old_logp, ref_logp, advantages, mask, clip_eps=0.2, beta=0.04) returns the GRPO objective as a float. Training maximizes it.

Advantages. Each row of rewards is one group. For reward ri​ in a group with mean μ and population standard deviation σ (divide by G, not by G−1):

Ai​=σ+εri​−μ​

With outcome rewards, every token of answer i gets the same advantage Ai​.

k3 estimator. For one token with log probabilities logπθ​ and logπref​:

D^KL​=πθ​πref​​−logπθ​πref​​−1

Objective. For token t of answer i, let ρi,t​=exp(logπθ​−logπold​). Then

J=N1​i=1∑N​∣oi​∣1​t∈oi​∑​[min(ρi,t​Ai​, clip(ρi,t​,1−ϵ,1+ϵ)Ai​)−βD^KL,i,t​]

Here N is the number of answers, oi​ is the set of valid tokens of answer i (where mask is True), and ∣oi​∣ is their number. Average the tokens of each answer first. Then average the answers.

Input and output

Name Type Shape Meaning
rewards float array (num_groups, G) the reward of each answer, one group in each row
eps float the small number added to the standard deviation
logp float array (N, L) log probability of each sampled token under the current policy
old_logp float array (N, L) the same under the policy that sampled the answers
ref_logp float array (N, L) the same under the reference policy
advantages float array (N,) one advantage for each answer
mask bool array (N, L) True for a valid token, False for padding
clip_eps float ϵ, the clip range
beta float the weight of the KL penalty
  • group_advantages returns a float array of shape (num_groups, G).
  • kl_k3 returns a float array with the shape of its inputs.
  • grpo_objective returns a Python float.

Constraints

  • 1 <= G, N <= 64 and 1 <= L <= 512.
  • Each answer has at least one valid token.
  • Padding tokens can hold any finite value. They must not change the result.
  • Do not change the input arrays.
  • Use numpy only.

Common mistakes the checks catch
  • Uses pi_theta / pi_ref inside k3, so the estimate is no longer unbiased for KL(pi_theta || pi_ref).
  • Adds beta times k3 to the objective instead of subtracting it.
  • Takes the maximum of the unclipped and the clipped surrogate, so the clip never limits the gain.
  • Divides by the standard deviation without eps, so a group with equal rewards gives NaN.
  • Uses the sample standard deviation with ddof=1, not the population standard deviation.
  • Averages over all valid tokens of the batch, not over each answer first.
Hint 1

Use axis=-1 and keepdims=True for the mean and the standard deviation of each group. np.std divides by G by default, which is what the task asks for.

Hint 2

In the log domain the ratio πref​/πθ​ is exp(ref_logp - logp). The log of that ratio is ref_logp - logp.

Hint 3

Give the advantages a token axis with advantages[:, None], so that they broadcast over L. Compute the unclipped and the clipped surrogate and take np.minimum.

Hint 4

Convert the mask to float. Multiply the per token values by it, sum over axis 1, and divide by the number of valid tokens of each answer. Then take the mean over the answers.

Reference solution and notes
import numpy as np


def group_advantages(rewards, eps=1e-8):
    mean_r = np.mean(rewards, axis=-1, keepdims=True)
    std_r = np.std(rewards, axis=-1, keepdims=True)
    return (rewards - mean_r) / (std_r + eps)


def kl_k3(logp, ref_logp):
    log_ratio = ref_logp - logp
    return np.exp(log_ratio) - log_ratio - 1.0


def grpo_objective(logp, old_logp, ref_logp, advantages, mask, clip_eps=0.2, beta=0.04):
    ratio = np.exp(logp - old_logp)
    adv = advantages[:, None]
    unclipped = ratio * adv
    clipped = np.clip(ratio, 1.0 - clip_eps, 1.0 + clip_eps) * adv
    per_token = np.minimum(unclipped, clipped) - beta * kl_k3(logp, ref_logp)
    mask = mask.astype(float)
    per_seq = np.sum(per_token * mask, axis=1) / np.sum(mask, axis=1)
    return float(np.mean(per_seq))

Why a group baseline

PPO subtracts a value estimate to reduce the variance of the policy gradient, and it needs a second network to learn that value. GRPO samples several answers to the same prompt and uses their mean reward as the baseline. Division by the group standard deviation puts easy and hard prompts on the same scale. A group where every answer gets the same reward has zero advantage and gives no gradient.

Why the k3 estimator

The exact KL needs a sum over the whole vocabulary at each position. The k3 estimator needs only the log probabilities of the sampled tokens. It is never negative, because x−logx−1≥0 for x>0. Its mean over tokens sampled from πθ​ is the exact KL divergence, so it is unbiased. The paper puts this penalty in the loss and not in the reward.

What the clip does

When A>0, the gain stops at a ratio of 1+ϵ. When A<0, the loss stops at 1−ϵ. The minimum keeps the pessimistic term, so one batch cannot move the policy far from the policy that sampled it.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · About two-thirds

About two-thirds: the chance that all eight answers are wrong is 0.958=0.663, plus a negligible 0.058 for all eight right. In 66% of groups every advantage is zero and the prompt teaches nothing, even though a correct answer would have been the most useful thing the model could see. With G=16 the share falls to 44%, and with G=64 to about 4% (0.9564=0.037), at four times the sampling cost again. In practice, hard prompts are handled by larger groups, by filtering silent groups and sampling replacements (DAPO), or by a curriculum that keeps success rates in the middle.

SolutionB1 · Training stops completely

Nothing is learned. With one sample, the group mean is that sample's reward, so the advantage is (r−r)/(0+10−8)=0 on every update, right or wrong. Only the KL term acts, and it pulls the policy towards the reference, which is where it already is. After 400 updates all probabilities are still exactly 0.25: the mean sampled reward in the last 50 updates is 0.2 (chance level, with sampling noise), and greedy evaluation scores 29.5%, because with all answers tied it always picks answer 0, which happens to be correct for one of the three questions. A group baseline needs at least two samples; with G=2 the same run already reaches 100% greedy accuracy. REINFORCE gets away with one sample because it uses the raw reward, with no baseline at all.
