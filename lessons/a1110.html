In plain words first
The idea in one line

You would never deploy a web app without tests. You would never ship a database migration without a rollback plan. But right now, most teams ship LLM applications by reading 10 outputs and saying "yeah, looks good." That is not evaluation. That is hope. Hope is not an engineering practice. Every prompt change, every model swap, every temperature tweak changes your output distribution in ways you cannot predict by reading a handful of examples. Evaluation is the only thing standing between your application and silent degradation.

The problem

In plain words first

An LLM application changes every time someone edits a prompt, swaps a model or updates the retrieval, and its outputs are free text, so you cannot check them by eye. Evaluation is a test suite for that: a fixed set of inputs, a way to score each output (exact checks, text-overlap metrics, a judge model with a rubric, or people), and statistics that say whether a change made things better or worse, or whether the test set is too small to tell. This lesson builds the scorer, the metrics, a confidence-interval calculator and a comparison report, then runs two honest experiments: a deliberately weak judge model, to show the biases a judge can have, and the statistics of how many test cases you need to see a 4-point regression. No model is trained.

You build a RAG chatbot for customer support. It works great in your demos. You ship it. Two weeks later, someone changes the system prompt to reduce hallucinations. The change works -- hallucination rate drops. But answer completeness also drops 34% because the model now refuses to answer anything it is not 100% certain about.

Nobody noticed for 11 days. Revenue from the self-service channel fell. Support tickets spiked.

This is the default outcome when you evaluate by vibes. You check a few examples, they look fine, you merge. But LLM outputs are stochastic. A prompt that works on 5 test cases can fail on the 6th. A model that scores 92% on your benchmarks can score 71% on the edge cases your users actually hit.

The fix is not "be more careful." The fix is automated evaluation that runs on every change, scores outputs against rubrics, computes confidence intervals, and blocks deployment when quality regresses.

Evaluation is not a nice-to-have. It is table stakes. Shipping without evals is deploying blind.

Idea 1 · The Eval Taxonomy

There are three categories of LLM evaluation. Each has a role. None is sufficient alone.

Automated metrics compare output text against reference answers using algorithms. BLEU measures n-gram overlap (originally for machine translation). ROUGE measures recall of reference n-grams (originally for summarization). BERTScore uses BERT embeddings to measure semantic similarity. These are fast and cheap -- you can score 10,000 outputs in seconds. But they miss nuance. Two answers can have zero word overlap and both be correct. One answer can have high ROUGE and be completely wrong in context.

LLM-as-judge uses a strong model to grade outputs against a rubric. This captures semantic quality -- relevance, correctness, helpfulness, safety -- that string metrics miss. It costs money (worked out below) and needs calibration: in the MT-Bench study (Zheng et al. 2023), GPT-4 as a judge agreed with human experts over 80% of the time, about as often as the experts agreed with each other, but judges also showed position bias, a preference for longer answers and a preference for their own outputs. Lesson 5.27 has the calibration recipe.

Human evaluation is the gold standard but the slowest and most expensive. Reserve it for calibrating your automated evals, not for running on every commit.

Idea 2 · LLM-as-Judge: The Workhorse

This is the evaluation method you will use 90% of the time. The pattern is simple: give a strong model the input, the output, an optional reference answer, and a rubric. Ask it to score.

Four criteria cover most use cases:

Relevance (1-5): Does the output address what was asked? A score of 1 means completely off-topic. A score of 5 means directly and specifically answers the question.

Correctness (1-5): Is the information factually accurate? A score of 1 means contains major factual errors. A score of 5 means all claims are verifiable and accurate.

Helpfulness (1-5): Would a user find this useful? A score of 1 means the response provides no value. A score of 5 means the user can immediately act on the information.

Safety (1-5): Is the output free from harmful content, bias, or policy violations? A score of 1 means contains harmful or dangerous content. A score of 5 means completely safe and appropriate.

Idea 3 · Rubric Design

Bad rubrics produce noisy scores. Good rubrics anchor each score to specific, observable behaviors.

Bad rubric: "Rate from 1-5 how good the answer is."

Good rubric: - 5: The answer is factually correct, directly addresses the question, includes specific details or examples, and provides actionable information. - 4: The answer is factually correct and addresses the question but lacks specific detail or is slightly verbose. - 3: The answer is mostly correct but contains a minor inaccuracy or partially misses the question's intent. - 2: The answer contains significant factual errors or only tangentially relates to the question. - 1: The answer is factually wrong, off-topic, or harmful.

Anchored descriptions make scores more consistent between runs and between judges; measure the effect on your own cases (Exercise 4).

Pairwise comparison is an alternative: show the judge two outputs and ask which is better. This eliminates scale calibration issues -- the judge does not need to decide if something is a "3" or a "4." It just picks the winner. Useful for comparing two prompt versions head-to-head.

Best-of-N generates N outputs for each input and has the judge pick the best one. This measures the ceiling of your system. If best-of-5 consistently beats best-of-1, you might benefit from sampling multiple responses and selecting.

Idea 4 · The Eval Pipeline

Every evaluation follows the same 6-step pipeline.

Prompt: Define your test cases. Each case has an input (user query + context) and optionally a reference answer.

Run: Execute the prompt against the model. Collect outputs. Run each test case 1-3 times if you want to measure variance.

Collect: Store inputs, outputs, and metadata (model, temperature, timestamp, prompt version).

Score: Apply your evaluation method -- automated metrics, LLM-as-judge, or both.

Compare: Compare scores against a baseline. The baseline is your last known-good version. Compute confidence intervals on the difference.

Decide: If the new version is statistically significantly better (or not worse), ship it. If it regresses, block.

Idea 5 · Eval Datasets: The Foundation

Your eval dataset is only as good as the cases in it. Three types of test cases matter:

Golden test set (50-100 cases): Curated input-output pairs that represent your core use cases. These are your regression tests. Every prompt change must pass these.

Adversarial examples (20-50 cases): Inputs designed to break your system. Prompt injections, edge cases, ambiguous queries, questions about topics outside your domain, requests for harmful content.

Distribution samples (100-200 cases): Random samples from real production traffic. These catch problems that curated tests miss because they reflect what users actually ask.

Idea 6 · Sample Size and Confidence

50 test cases is not enough.

If your eval scores 90% on 50 cases, the 95% Wilson confidence interval is [78.6%, 95.7%]: a 17-point spread. You cannot distinguish a system scoring 80% from one scoring 95%.

At 200 cases with 90% accuracy, the confidence interval tightens to [85.1%, 93.4%]. Now you can make decisions.

Test cases Observed accuracy 95% CI width Chance to detect a true 4.3-point regression (paired, run below)
50 90% 17.0 points 14%
100 90% 11.9 points low
200 90% 8.4 points 60%
500 90% 5.3 points 91%
1000 90% 3.7 points 100%

Use at least 200 test cases for any evaluation where you need to make deployment decisions. Use 500+ if you are comparing two systems that are close in quality.

Idea 7 · Regression Testing

Every prompt change needs a before/after eval. This is non-negotiable.

The workflow: 1. Run your eval suite on the current (baseline) prompt -- store the scores 2. Make the prompt change 3. Run the same eval suite on the new prompt 4. Compare scores with a statistical test (paired t-test or bootstrap) 5. If no statistically significant regression on any criteria -- ship 6. If regression detected -- investigate which test cases degraded and why

Idea 9 · Anti-Patterns

Vibes-based evaluation. "I read 5 outputs and they looked good." You cannot perceive a 5% quality regression by reading examples. Your brain cherry-picks confirming evidence.

Testing on training examples. If your eval cases overlap with examples in your prompt or fine-tuning data, you are measuring memorization, not generalization. Keep eval data separate.

Single-metric obsession. Optimizing only for correctness while ignoring helpfulness produces terse, technically-accurate-but-useless answers. Always score multiple criteria.

Evaluating without baselines. A score of 4.2/5 means nothing in isolation. Is that better or worse than yesterday? Better or worse than the competing prompt? Always compare.

Using a weak judge. A small judge produces noisy, biased scores (the run below shows how badly). Use a capable model, check it against human labels, and swap the order in pairwise comparisons.

def score_with_llm_judge(input_text, model_output, reference_output=None, criteria=None):
    if criteria is None:
        criteria = ["relevance", "correctness", "helpfulness", "safety"]

    scores = []
    for criterion in criteria:
        score_value = simulate_judge_score(input_text, model_output, reference_output, criterion)
        reasoning = generate_judge_reasoning(input_text, model_output, criterion, score_value)
        scores.append(EvalScore(
            criterion=criterion,
            score=score_value,
            reasoning=reasoning,
        ))
    return scores


def simulate_judge_score(input_text, model_output, reference_output, criterion):
    output_len = len(model_output)
    input_len = len(input_text)

    base_score = 3

    if output_len < 10:
        base_score = 1
    elif output_len > input_len * 0.5:
        base_score = 4

    if reference_output:
        ref_words = set(reference_output.lower().split())
        out_words = set(model_output.lower().split())
        overlap = len(ref_words & out_words) / max(len(ref_words), 1)
        if overlap > 0.5:
            base_score = min(5, base_score + 1)
        elif overlap < 0.1:
            base_score = max(1, base_score - 1)

    if criterion == "safety":
        unsafe_patterns = ["hack", "exploit", "steal", "weapon", "illegal"]
        if any(p in model_output.lower() for p in unsafe_patterns):
            return 1
        return min(5, base_score + 1)

    if criterion == "relevance":
        input_keywords = set(input_text.lower().split())
        output_keywords = set(model_output.lower().split())
        keyword_overlap = len(input_keywords & output_keywords) / max(len(input_keywords), 1)
        if keyword_overlap > 0.3:
            base_score = min(5, base_score + 1)

    seed = hash(f"{input_text}{model_output}{criterion}") % 100
    if seed < 15:
        base_score = max(1, base_score - 1)
    elif seed > 85:
        base_score = min(5, base_score + 1)

    return max(1, min(5, base_score))


def generate_judge_reasoning(input_text, model_output, criterion, score):
    rubric = RUBRICS.get(criterion, {})
    description = rubric.get(score, "No rubric description available.")
    return f"[{criterion.upper()}={score}/5] {description}. Output length: {len(model_output)} chars."

Step 3 · Build Automated Metrics

Implement ROUGE-L and a simple semantic similarity score alongside the LLM judge.

def rouge_l_score(reference, hypothesis):
    if not reference or not hypothesis:
        return 0.0
    ref_tokens = reference.lower().split()
    hyp_tokens = hypothesis.lower().split()

    m = len(ref_tokens)
    n = len(hyp_tokens)

    dp = [[0] * (n + 1) for _ in range(m + 1)]
    for i in range(1, m + 1):
        for j in range(1, n + 1):
            if ref_tokens[i - 1] == hyp_tokens[j - 1]:
                dp[i][j] = dp[i - 1][j - 1] + 1
            else:
                dp[i][j] = max(dp[i - 1][j], dp[i][j - 1])

    lcs_length = dp[m][n]
    if lcs_length == 0:
        return 0.0

    precision = lcs_length / n
    recall = lcs_length / m
    f1 = (2 * precision * recall) / (precision + recall)
    return round(f1, 4)


def word_overlap_score(reference, hypothesis):
    if not reference or not hypothesis:
        return 0.0
    ref_words = set(reference.lower().split())
    hyp_words = set(hypothesis.lower().split())
    intersection = ref_words & hyp_words
    union = ref_words | hyp_words
    return round(len(intersection) / len(union), 4) if union else 0.0

Step 4 · Build the Confidence Interval Calculator

Statistical rigor separates real evaluation from vibes.

def wilson_confidence_interval(successes, total, z=1.96):
    if total == 0:
        return (0.0, 0.0)
    p = successes / total
    denominator = 1 + z * z / total
    center = (p + z * z / (2 * total)) / denominator
    spread = z * math.sqrt((p * (1 - p) + z * z / (4 * total)) / total) / denominator
    lower = max(0.0, center - spread)
    upper = min(1.0, center + spread)
    return (round(lower, 4), round(upper, 4))


def bootstrap_confidence_interval(scores, n_bootstrap=1000, confidence=0.95):
    if len(scores) < 2:
        return (0.0, 0.0, 0.0)
    n = len(scores)
    means = []
    seed_base = int(sum(scores) * 1000) % 2**31
    for i in range(n_bootstrap):
        seed = (seed_base + i * 7919) % 2**31
        sample = []
        for j in range(n):
            idx = (seed + j * 31) % n
            sample.append(scores[idx])
            seed = (seed * 1103515245 + 12345) % 2**31
        means.append(sum(sample) / len(sample))
    means.sort()
    alpha = (1 - confidence) / 2
    lower_idx = int(alpha * n_bootstrap)
    upper_idx = int((1 - alpha) * n_bootstrap) - 1
    mean = sum(scores) / len(scores)
    return (round(means[lower_idx], 4), round(mean, 4), round(means[upper_idx], 4))

import math, random

def wilson(k, n, z=1.96):                       # 95% Wilson score interval for a proportion
    p = k / n; d = 1 + z * z / n
    c = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d; h = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return c - h, c + h

print("A. 95% confidence interval for an observed 90% pass rate")
for n in (50, 100, 200, 500, 1000):
    lo, hi = wilson(round(0.9 * n), n)
    print(f"  n = {n:5}: [{lo:6.1%}, {hi:6.1%}]  width {100 * (hi - lo):4.1f} points")

# B. comparing two prompt versions on the SAME test cases: a paired bootstrap
random.seed(0)
def simulate(n):
    # per test case: does the old prompt pass (90%)? The new prompt breaks 7% of passing cases and fixes 20% of failing ones,
    # so it is truly worse by 0.9 * 0.07 - 0.1 * 0.2 = 4.3 points
    base = [random.random() < 0.90 for _ in range(n)]
    new = [(random.random() >= 0.07) if b else (random.random() < 0.20) for b in base]
    return base, new
def paired_bootstrap(base, new, B=4000):
    d = [int(n) - int(b) for b, n in zip(base, new)]; n = len(d)
    means = sorted(sum(d[random.randrange(n)] for _ in range(n)) / n for _ in range(B))
    return sum(d) / n, means[int(0.025 * B)], means[int(0.975 * B)]
print("\nB. old prompt against new prompt on the same cases (the new one is truly worse by 4.3 points)")
def paired_ci(base, new):                      # normal approximation to the paired difference
    d = [int(n) - int(b) for b, n in zip(base, new)]; n = len(d); m = sum(d) / n
    sd = math.sqrt(sum((x - m) ** 2 for x in d) / (n - 1)) if n > 1 else 0
    return m, m - 1.96 * sd / math.sqrt(n), m + 1.96 * sd / math.sqrt(n)
for n in (50, 200, 500, 1000):
    base, new = simulate(n)
    diff, lo, hi = paired_bootstrap(base, new)
    power = sum(paired_ci(*simulate(n))[2] < 0 for _ in range(300)) / 300
    print(f"  n = {n:5}: one run: difference {diff:+.1%}, bootstrap 95% CI [{lo:+.1%}, {hi:+.1%}] | "
          f"regression detected in {power:.0%} of 300 repeated evaluations")

Reading this output

A: small evals are wide. At 50 cases, an observed 90% is consistent with anything from 78.6% to 95.7%. That 17-point band is wider than most of the prompt changes you will test. The width shrinks with the square root of n: four times the cases, half the width. It is 8.4 points at 200 and 3.7 at 1,000.

B: a real regression is usually invisible at 50 cases. The new prompt is truly 4.3 points worse. In the single 50-case run shown, it broke and fixed the same number of cases, so the measured difference is exactly zero and the bootstrap interval collapses to [0, 0]. Across 300 repeated evaluations of that size, the regression was detected only 14% of the time. At 200 cases, detection rises to 60%, a coin flip with a slight edge. At 500 cases it is 91%, and at 1,000 it is 100%.

Pairing helps. Both prompts run on the same cases, so the cases that are easy or hard for both cancel out. Only the cases that change outcome carry signal. Before trusting a "+2 points" result, check its paired interval. If it includes zero, you have not measured an improvement.

def compare_eval_runs(baseline_results, new_results, criteria=None):
    if criteria is None:
        criteria = ["relevance", "correctness", "helpfulness", "safety"]

    report = {"criteria": {}, "overall": {}, "regressions": [], "improvements": []}

    for criterion in criteria:
        baseline_scores = []
        new_scores = []
        for br in baseline_results:
            for s in br.scores:
                if s.criterion == criterion:
                    baseline_scores.append(s.score)
        for nr in new_results:
            for s in nr.scores:
                if s.criterion == criterion:
                    new_scores.append(s.score)

        if not baseline_scores or not new_scores:
            continue

        baseline_mean = statistics.mean(baseline_scores)
        new_mean = statistics.mean(new_scores)
        diff = new_mean - baseline_mean

        baseline_ci = bootstrap_confidence_interval(baseline_scores)
        new_ci = bootstrap_confidence_interval(new_scores)

        threshold_pct = len(baseline_scores)
        passing_baseline = sum(1 for s in baseline_scores if s >= 4)
        passing_new = sum(1 for s in new_scores if s >= 4)
        baseline_pass_rate = wilson_confidence_interval(passing_baseline, len(baseline_scores))
        new_pass_rate = wilson_confidence_interval(passing_new, len(new_scores))

        criterion_report = {
            "baseline_mean": round(baseline_mean, 3),
            "new_mean": round(new_mean, 3),
            "diff": round(diff, 3),
            "baseline_ci": baseline_ci,
            "new_ci": new_ci,
            "baseline_pass_rate": f"{passing_baseline}/{len(baseline_scores)}",
            "new_pass_rate": f"{passing_new}/{len(new_scores)}",
            "baseline_pass_ci": baseline_pass_rate,
            "new_pass_ci": new_pass_rate,
        }

        if diff < -0.3:
            report["regressions"].append(criterion)
            criterion_report["status"] = "REGRESSION"
        elif diff > 0.3:
            report["improvements"].append(criterion)
            criterion_report["status"] = "IMPROVED"
        else:
            criterion_report["status"] = "STABLE"

        report["criteria"][criterion] = criterion_report

    all_baseline = [s.score for r in baseline_results for s in r.scores]
    all_new = [s.score for r in new_results for s in r.scores]

    if all_baseline and all_new:
        report["overall"] = {
            "baseline_mean": round(statistics.mean(all_baseline), 3),
            "new_mean": round(statistics.mean(all_new), 3),
            "diff": round(statistics.mean(all_new) - statistics.mean(all_baseline), 3),
            "n_test_cases": len(baseline_results),
            "ship_decision": "SHIP" if not report["regressions"] else "BLOCK",
        }

    return report


def print_comparison_report(report):
    print("=" * 70)
    print("  EVAL COMPARISON REPORT")
    print("=" * 70)

    overall = report.get("overall", {})
    decision = overall.get("ship_decision", "UNKNOWN")
    print(f"\n  Decision: {decision}")
    print(f"  Test cases: {overall.get('n_test_cases', 0)}")
    print(f"  Overall: {overall.get('baseline_mean', 0):.3f} -> {overall.get('new_mean', 0):.3f} (diff: {overall.get('diff', 0):+.3f})")

    print(f"\n  {'Criterion':<15} {'Baseline':>10} {'New':>10} {'Diff':>8} {'Status':>12}")

Reading this output

12 of 12 looks perfect, until you swap the order. With the correct answer shown first, the judge picked it every time. With the same answer shown second, it picked it zero times. It answered "A" in all 24 judgements, so it was never comparing the answers. It was reporting a position. A judge evaluated in only one order would have looked flawless.

Pointwise scores were no better. Every wrong answer got exactly 3, whatever it said: "eight sides" for a hexagon, "Sydney" for Australia's capital. The correct answers averaged 2.25, lower than the wrong ones. Six of the 12 were scored 1 (the lowest), even though they were short and right. The judge seems to reward length and a confident tone rather than truth. This is verbosity bias, and the wrong answers were written to exploit it.

What this means for your evals. The test cases here are easy: any careful human gets all 12 right. A judge that fails them would quietly corrupt every number built on it. Before trusting a judge, measure it on cases with known answers, in both orders, against human labels. Use a stronger judge model than the system under test, and give it a reference answer or a rubric. For factual answers like these, an exact check in code needs no judge at all.

You are an LLM evaluation designer. I will describe an LLM application. You will produce a complete evaluation framework: criteria, rubrics, test cases, and scoring methodology.

## Design Protocol

### 1. Analyze the Application

Before writing rubrics:

- Identify the core task (Q&A, summarization, code generation, classification, creative writing, multi-turn dialogue)
- Determine the stakeholders (end users, developers, compliance, business)
- Identify the failure modes (hallucination, off-topic, harmful, too verbose, too terse, wrong format)
- Determine if there is a ground truth (factual answers, known-correct code, reference summaries)
- Assess the risk level (low: creative writing; high: medical, legal, financial advice)

### 2. Select Evaluation Criteria

Choose 3-5 criteria from this menu. Not every criterion applies to every application.

| Criterion | Use when | Skip when |
|-----------|----------|-----------|
| Relevance | Always | Never |
| Correctness | Factual tasks, Q&A, code | Creative writing, brainstorming |
| Helpfulness | User-facing applications | Internal pipelines |
| Safety | All user-facing, especially sensitive domains | Internal batch processing |
| Completeness | Summarization, instructions, multi-part questions | Single-fact lookups |
| Conciseness | Chatbots, quick answers | Detailed explanations, tutorials |
| Tone/Style | Brand-sensitive, customer-facing | Technical pipelines |
| Code Quality | Code generation | Non-code tasks |
| Faithfulness | RAG, grounded generation | Open-ended generation |

### 3. Write Anchored Rubrics

For each selected criterion, write a 1-5 scale with specific, observable descriptions.

Rules:
- Each level must describe a concrete behavior, not a vague quality
- Level 5 is not "perfect" -- it is the highest realistic standard
- Level 3 is "acceptable but with notable issues"

Defend a designTwo engineers ran the same judge on the same 100 answers with the instruction "rate from 1 to 5 how good the answer is" and got means almost a full point apart. How do you rewrite the rubric so the scores become reproducible, and what else goes into the judge prompt?

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Anchor every level to behavior a reader can check. A rubric that states what a 5, a 4 and a 3 look like reduces judge variance by 30 to 40 percent compared with an unanchored scale. Score several criteria separately, give the judge the input, the output and the reference, and ask for the reasoning next to the number.

From vague to anchored.

The bad rubric asks how good the answer is. The good rubric describes each level:

  • 5: factually correct, directly addresses the question, includes specific details, gives actionable information.
  • 4: correct and on topic, but short on detail or slightly verbose.
  • 3: mostly correct with a minor inaccuracy, or partially misses the intent.
  • 2: significant factual errors, or only tangentially related.
  • 1: wrong, off topic or harmful.

One criterion per score.

Score relevance, correctness, helpfulness and safety on separate scales. A single overall score collapses the dimensions, and optimizing it produces terse answers that are technically right and useless.

What the judge sees.

The input, the output, the reference answer when one exists, and the anchored rubric for the criterion. Ask for a structured answer with the score and a short reason, so that a reviewer can audit the judge.

Prove it.

Run the judge three times on each case and compute an agreement statistic across the runs. Below 0.7 the rubric is still ambiguous. If the judge keeps wavering between two adjacent levels, switch that criterion to a pairwise comparison, which removes the scale.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Lower the temperature of the judge." A deterministic judge still applies a vague scale differently across cases; the rubric is the fix.
  • "Average more runs." Averaging noise costs money and leaves a scale that two readers interpret differently.
  • "One overall score is simpler." Simpler to read and wrong to optimize; the lesson warns that single metric optimization produces terse useless answers.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why score four criteria instead of one overall score? Expected: one score collapses dimensions; a model can be correct and useless, and the single number hides the trade.
  • (level 2) How do you show the new rubric is reproducible? Expected: run the judge several times per case and compute an agreement statistic; below 0.7 rewrite again.
  • (level 3) The judge still wavers between 3 and 4 on many cases. What design change removes that? Expected: pairwise comparison, which only asks which output is better.
  • (level 3) One criterion has a reference answer and another has none. Does the rubric change? Expected: yes; correctness anchors to the reference, while helpfulness and safety anchor to properties of the output alone.

Defend a designA model you fine tuned jumped eight points on a public benchmark after you added a new web scraped corpus to the training mix. Leadership wants to announce it. Design the audit that tells you whether the jump is capability or leakage, and say what you would publish instead if it is leakage.

Level 4 of 4 · about 12 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Audit for contamination before anyone announces anything. Search the new corpus for the benchmark questions and their close paraphrases and report the share found, then rerun the model on a versioned custom eval that the corpus cannot contain and diff the individual test cases against the previous model. If the overlap is material and the custom eval did not move, the jump is memorization, and you publish the custom eval result with the contamination share.

Why the jump is suspect.

Training corpora scrape the web and the benchmark questions live on the web. Nobody has to intend it. A new scraped corpus is the most likely path for the test to enter training, and a saturated benchmark rewards memorization with large jumps. The same lesson lists teaching to the test and saturation as the other two mechanisms that move a score without moving capability.

The audit.

  1. Contamination search. Check what share of the eval questions, or close paraphrases of them, appear in the new corpus. Report exact matches and near matches separately with the method.
  2. Model diff. Compare the two model versions case by case: which items improved, which regressed, which stayed. If the improvements cluster on the items the search found, the diff names the leak.
  3. Custom eval. Run both models on your own versioned suite of at least 200 cases with edge cases, kept separate from every training and prompt example. A real gain shows there with an interval; a leak does not.
  4. Separation check. Confirm that no eval case overlaps with the prompt examples or the fine tuning data, because a test case that was also a training example measures memorization.

What to publish.

The custom eval numbers with intervals, the contamination share and the method, and the benchmark score with that caveat attached. Never a bare benchmark number. And add the dedup step to the training pipeline so the next corpus is checked against every eval set before training starts.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "We did not include the benchmark on purpose, so it is clean." Web scale scraping includes it without intent; the lesson calls this the normal path to contamination.
  • "Eight points is too large to be noise." Noise is not the alternative; memorization is, and it produces large jumps.
  • "Announce the benchmark and add the caveat later." Publish the custom eval with intervals and the contamination share; a bare benchmark number is the claim you cannot defend.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) The search finds exact matches for a small share and paraphrases for more. Which count? Expected: both; paraphrases leak the answer as well, so report exact and near matches separately with the method.
  • (level 3) The custom eval also rose. Is the jump real? Expected: more likely, if the suite is versioned, separate from training data and large enough that the rise exceeds its interval.
  • (level 4) How do you prevent the question next time? Expected: deduplicate training corpora against every eval set before training, keep eval data out of prompts and fine tuning data, and record the check in the training report.

Take-homeTake home. We give you a small instruction tuned model, a held out corpus, a set of short factual prompts with gold answers, a set of open form prompts with reference answers, and no network access. Build an evaluation pipeline that reports more than one number and explains its aggregate, and describe how it would run in CI once a network and a judge model are available. What do you deliver, and what do you leave out on purpose?

Level 4 of 4 · about 60 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Deliver one runnable file with four evals: held out perplexity with honest token accounting, normalized exact match on the factual set, token F1 on the open form set, and a deterministic mock judge on a 1 to 5 scale with the same interface a real judge will have. Report every per eval number, a weighted aggregate with the weights in a config, and per example records for diagnosis. Leave out the real judge, intervals on the aggregate and anything that needs a network, and say so in the readme.

The four evals.

Each eval covers a dimension the others miss. Perplexity says whether the model fits the language at all. Exact match says whether it produces the gold string on short answers. Token F1 forgives paraphrase on open answers. The judge scores quality that string metrics cannot see. Each is a function from the model and a dataset to a result with the metric, the per example details and a name.

The details a reviewer checks.

  • Perplexity. Sum the loss over real target positions, exclude pad targets from the denominator, shift the targets by one, divide once at the end.
  • Normalization. Lowercase, strip, collapse whitespace, drop trailing terminal punctuation when the sides differ only by it. Token F1 is one when both sides are empty and zero when only one is.
  • Mock judge. A 5 when the normalized strings match, a 4 at token F1 of at least 0.8, a 3 between 0.5 and 0.8, a 2 between 0.2 and 0.5, a 1 otherwise, with a one line rationale. It is not a judge. It fixes the interface so a real model is a one function swap.

The aggregate.

Normalize each eval into the unit range: perplexity as one over one plus the log of perplexity, exact match and F1 as they are, the judge divided by 5. The default weights are 0.2, 0.3, 0.3 and 0.2. Put them in a config and print them in the report, because the choice is a product decision, not a fact. Write the report with stable JSON keys and a table for the terminal.

The CI story.

When a network exists, swap the mock judge for a real one and calibrate it against at least 50 hand labeled cases, with a Spearman correlation of 0.7 or more, before its scores gate anything. Grow the suite to about 200 cases, compare two runs with Wilson and bootstrap intervals, block on a regression beyond 0.3 on any criterion, trigger on prompt and model paths, and upload the report as an artifact.

What to leave out and why.

The real judge, because there is no network and no human labels to calibrate it. Intervals on the aggregate, because the weights make it a product number; put intervals on the components. Contamination checks, because the training corpus is not provided. Say each omission in the readme.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "One accuracy number is the deliverable." A single metric never describes a model; the brief asks for several and for an explanation of the aggregate.
  • "Skip perplexity, it is not a task metric." It is the cheapest check that the model fits the language at all, and wrong accounting there is what a reviewer looks for.
  • "Hard code the weights." The weights are the product decision; expose them and print them next to the numbers.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) Why keep a mock judge in the deliverable at all? Expected: it fixes the interface and the aggregation path so the real judge is a one function swap, and it keeps the loop runnable offline.
  • (level 3) Why is the weighted mean a product decision? Expected: weights encode what the product values; the pipeline exposes them in config and reports every component so a reviewer can see what the aggregate hides.
  • (level 4) What is the first thing you add when a network appears? Expected: a real judge behind the same function, then a calibration run against hand labels before any of its scores gate anything.

Estimate itA prompt change scores 90 percent on your 50 case golden set, and the baseline scored 86 on the same set. The author wants to ship it as a four point win. How sure can you be, and what sample size would you need to make this call?

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. At 50 cases and 90 percent observed accuracy, the 95 percent interval runs from 78 to 97 percent, a 19 point spread. A four point gap sits well inside that spread, so the win cannot be told from noise. You need about 200 cases to detect a 5 percent change, and 500 or more when two systems are close.

The table.

Cases Observed accuracy 95 percent interval width Detects a 5 percent regression
50 90 percent 19 points no
100 90 percent 12 points barely
200 90 percent 9 points yes
500 90 percent 5 points with confidence
1000 90 percent 3 points precisely

The width falls roughly with the square root of the sample size. Four times the cases cut the width by about half.

The interval.

For a pass rate the lesson uses the Wilson interval, which stays correct at small samples and at proportions near zero or one. The normal interval fails in both places, and those are the places where releases get decided.

The paired case.

Both versions ran on the same 50 cases, so compare them as pairs. A paired test or a paired bootstrap on the per case differences is tighter than two separate intervals. It still will not rescue a four point gap on 50 cases, but it is the right test once the set is large enough.

How to build to 200.

The lesson splits the set into a golden core of 50 to 100 curated cases, 20 to 50 adversarial cases and 100 to 200 samples of real traffic. Version the set, and compare two runs only on the same version.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Four points is four points." On 50 cases the interval is 19 points wide; a four point gap is noise.
  • "Run the same 50 cases three times." Repeating the cases measures the model's variance, not the population; the interval over cases stays wide.
  • "Always use a thousand cases." A thousand cases give a 3 point width but cost five times a 200 case run; use 200 for routine decisions and 500 or more for close calls.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why does the interval shrink slowly as you add cases? Expected: the width falls with the square root of the sample size, so four times the cases halve the width.
  • (level 2) The two runs used identical cases. Does that help? Expected: yes; a paired comparison on per case differences is tighter than two independent intervals.
  • (level 3) Which interval does the lesson use for a pass rate, and why? Expected: the Wilson interval, because it behaves correctly at small samples and extreme proportions where the normal approximation fails.
  • (level 3) You can afford 200 judge calls per run. How do you split them? Expected: a golden core, an adversarial slice and a sample of production traffic, versioned and fixed between the two runs you compare.

Defend a designYou run three candidate models on the same fifty tasks and must publish a leaderboard with a winner. The means are 0.78, 0.75 and 0.10. The second team says their model is tied with the first. Design the aggregation and the statistics that settle it.

Level 3 of 4 · about 10 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Report a mean with a bootstrap interval, a win rate, and a paired interval on the difference for each pair of models. Resample the tasks with replacement to get the interval on each mean. For the tie claim, bootstrap the per task difference between the first two models; if that interval includes zero, the leaderboard shows them as tied.

The input.

One record per model and task: model id, task id, metric name, a score in the unit range, and a category. The aggregator refuses any score outside the unit range, because a task that reports 0 to 100 would silently dominate the mean. Normalization belongs in the metric, upstream.

Two rankings.

  • Mean score is the headline. It is sensitive to outliers and to task imbalance.
  • Win rate counts the tasks where the model has the best score; a tie gives every tied model a full win. It is less sensitive to outliers and to scale, and it loses magnitude information.

Report both. Rank by mean by default and keep the win rate column.

The intervals.

Bootstrap over task ids: resample the fifty tasks with replacement, compute the mean, repeat, and take the percentile interval at the 0.05 level. The low level helpers default to 1000 resamples and the public aggregators to 500. For a pair of models, bootstrap the per task difference and read whether the interval excludes zero. That is the test for the tie claim, and it is paired because both models ran the same tasks.

Categories and rendering.

If each task carries a category, report a per category mean. A model that is good overall and weak on code shows up there and nowhere else. Render a table sorted by mean with rank, model, mean, interval to two decimals, win rate and task count.

What this leaves out.

Task weighting; every task counts the same. And independence: ten arithmetic items that share one error pattern are not fifty independent samples. A hierarchical bootstrap that resamples task families, then tasks, is the follow up.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Two means with overlapping intervals are tied." The paired difference is the test; separate intervals can overlap while the difference excludes zero.
  • "Rank by win rate, it is robust." It loses magnitude; a model that wins every task by a hair and loses one badly looks perfect. Report both.
  • "Weight the tasks by importance now." The capstone counts every task the same; add weights only with a documented reason and keep the unweighted table.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) Why bootstrap over tasks and not over predictions within a task? Expected: the unit of replication is the task; resampling within a task understates the variance across tasks.
  • (level 3) When does win rate rank differently from the mean? Expected: when one model wins many tasks by a little and loses a few by a lot; the mean punishes the big losses, the win rate counts the wins.
  • (level 4) Tasks come in families that share error patterns. What breaks? Expected: the tasks are not independent, so the interval is too narrow; resample families first, then tasks.
  • (level 4) One task's metric returns 0 to 100 instead of 0 to 1. What should the aggregator do? Expected: refuse the run; a silent rescale would hide the bug, and the metric must return a fraction.

Debug a failureYou compare two prompt versions by showing a judge model both outputs and asking which one is better. Version B wins 70 percent of the pairs. You then swap the two outputs and version A wins most of them. What went wrong, and how do you run the comparison?

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. The judge has position bias: it prefers the option it reads first. Your first run measured the order, not the prompt. Score every pair in both orders, count a win only when the same version wins twice, and treat split decisions as ties. Then report the win rate with a confidence interval.

Why pairwise at all.

A pairwise judge never has to decide whether an output is a 3 or a 4 on a scale. It only picks a winner, so it avoids the scale calibration problem of a single grader. That makes it the right tool for a head to head test of two prompt versions.

The bias.

Judges prefer the first option presented. The lesson's rule is short: always randomize the order and run both. One order gives you a number about the layout of the prompt.

The protocol.

  1. For each input, score the pair in both orders.
  2. A version wins the pair only when it wins in both orders.
  3. A pair that flips with the order is a tie.
  4. Report the win rate and its confidence interval. A win rate near one half means the judge cannot separate the versions.

Also check length. If version B is longer, the judge may reward verbosity. Normalize for length or state in the rubric that length does not count, and see if the preference survives.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The judge is random, so use a different judge." Position bias is systematic, not random; another judge has it too, and the protocol is the fix.
  • "Always put the baseline first." That fixes the bias in one direction and hides it; run both orders.
  • "Pairwise comparison is only for human raters." Judges run pairwise well once the order is randomized and both orders are scored.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why does a pairwise comparison avoid rubric calibration problems? Expected: the judge only picks a winner and never places an output on a numeric scale.
  • (level 2) Version B is also longer. What else do you control? Expected: verbosity bias; normalize for length or exclude it in the rubric, then check whether the preference remains.
  • (level 3) After both orders, 40 percent of the pairs flip with the order. What does that tell you? Expected: the judge cannot tell the versions apart on those pairs; count them as ties and expect a win rate close to one half.
  • (level 3) How many pairs make a 55 percent win rate real? Expected: enough that the interval excludes one half; use a Wilson or bootstrap interval, and expect to need hundreds of pairs.

Weigh a trade-offYou can score a summarization feature in three ways: n-gram overlap against a reference, a strong model that grades with a rubric, or human experts. The team has one afternoon and a small budget. How do you decide which to use, and for what?

Level 1 of 4 · about 6 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Use all three, each for a different job. Overlap metrics are free and instant but agree with people only 40 to 60 percent of the time, so they serve as a baseline and a smoke test. A judge model costs a few dollars per thousand cases and agrees 82 to 88 percent with people on a well anchored rubric, so it is the default scorer. People are the ground truth, so spend them on calibrating the judge and on edge cases, not on every commit.

The trade.

Method Speed Cost per thousand Agreement with people Use
BLEU or ROUGE under a second nothing 40 to 60 percent baselines, smoke tests
Embedding similarity about 30 seconds nothing 55 to 70 percent screening
Judge model, small tier about 3 minutes about 8 dollars 82 to 86 percent default CI judge
Judge model, large tier about 5 minutes about 25 dollars 85 to 88 percent high stakes, safety
Human expert about 2 hours about 500 dollars ground truth calibration, policy

Why each one stays.

Overlap metrics miss meaning. Two answers with no shared words can both be right, and a high ROUGE answer can be wrong in context. They stay because they cost nothing, run in seconds and do not drift when a judge model changes.

The judge captures relevance, correctness, helpfulness and safety. It stays the workhorse because it scales. It needs a rubric with anchored levels and a calibration run against human labels before its number means anything.

Human review is slow and expensive. It stays because it is the only ground truth. Reserve it for calibrating the automated scores and for the cases the judge cannot settle.

For the afternoon.

Run the overlap metric on everything to catch gross breakage. Run the judge on the golden set with the four criteria. Have two people label a small slice and compare it with the judge before anyone reads the mean.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "People are the gold standard, so grade everything by hand." Human review takes hours and costs about 500 dollars per thousand cases, so it cannot run on every change.
  • "Overlap metrics are useless for generation." They catch gross regressions and cost nothing; they only miss meaning.
  • "Pick one metric and optimize it." Optimizing only correctness yields terse answers that nobody can use; score several criteria.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 1) What does ROUGE measure? Expected: recall of reference n-grams, built for summarization.
  • (level 2) Why keep the overlap metric once you have a judge? Expected: it is free and fast, it catches gross regressions in seconds, and it does not move when the judge model changes.
  • (level 2) When is a judge not enough? Expected: high stakes or policy decisions, and any time the judge has not been calibrated against human labels on this task.
  • (level 3) The lesson says the judge must be at least as capable as the model it grades. Why? Expected: a weak judge gives noisy, inconsistent scores and cannot see the errors a stronger model makes.

Defend a designDesign the evaluation gate that runs on every pull request that touches prompts or model code for a support assistant. Say what runs, what blocks the merge, what it costs per month, and how you stop the gate from blocking on noise.

Level 3 of 4 · about 12 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Trigger on changes to the prompt and model paths, run a fixed versioned suite of about 200 cases through the proposed prompt, score four anchored criteria with a frozen calibrated judge, and compare with the stored scores of the last known good baseline. Block when any criterion drops beyond the threshold and the interval on the difference excludes zero, and store the results as artifacts. With a small judge tier a 200 case run costs about 4 dollars, so ten merges a week cost about 160 dollars a month.

What runs.

  • Trigger. A path filter on the prompt directory and the model code, so documentation commits do not pay for a run.
  • Suite. A golden core of 50 to 100 curated cases, 20 to 50 adversarial cases such as injections, out of domain questions and harmful requests, and 100 to 200 samples of real traffic. Version the suite and tag every change.
  • Scoring. Deterministic assertions first, such as a contains check or a regex, because they are free. Then the judge on relevance, correctness, helpfulness and safety, each on an anchored 1 to 5 scale, with the judge model and version frozen and calibrated against hand labels.

What blocks.

Score the baseline once, when it becomes the last known good version, and store its scores. Each pull request scores only the new prompt on the same suite version. For each criterion compute the mean difference and the pass rate at 4 or more with a Wilson interval. The lesson's code blocks on any mean drop of more than 0.3. It computes a bootstrap interval for each run separately, not an interval on the difference, so a noisy drop can block. Its written workflow asks for a statistical test, a paired t test or a bootstrap. Add a paired bootstrap on the per case differences to the code, and block only when both hold: a drop of more than 0.3 and an interval on the difference that excludes zero. The report lists the cases that degraded so the author can read them.

How to keep noise out.

At 50 cases the interval is 19 points wide and a 5 percent regression is invisible, so the suite needs about 200 cases. Freeze the judge, because a judge upgrade moves every score. Keep the suite versioned and never edit it inside the pull request that fails it. Add a bottom quantile alert and a per category breakdown, because a prompt can improve overall and regress on safety.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Run the eval nightly instead." Nightly finds the regression after the merge; the lesson's story is 11 days of a shipped drop. Run before merge.
  • "Fifty golden cases are enough for a gate." At 50 cases the interval is 19 points wide, and a 5 percent regression cannot be detected.
  • "Use the newest judge model on each run." Judge changes move every score; freeze the judge and change it only with a parallel baseline.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) Why a path filter on the trigger? Expected: the eval costs money and minutes; run it when prompts or model code change, not on every commit.
  • (level 3) A criterion moved by 0.1 and the author calls it a regression. Expected: the threshold is 0.3 on the mean with intervals; 0.1 is inside noise at 200 cases, so report it and do not block.
  • (level 4) The gate blocks the merge and the author says the suite is stale. What is the process? Expected: suite changes are their own reviewed and versioned change; rerun the baseline when the suite version changes, and never edit the suite inside the failing pull request.
  • (level 4) How do you catch an aggregate improvement that regresses one category? Expected: a stratified per category report with intervals and a block rule on any category.

Where it is taught. The evaluation and testing lesson gives the pipeline, the thresholds and the cost table, and the LLM evaluation frameworks lesson gives the test gate pattern and the calibration rules.

Explain the mechanismYour eval report shows two kinds of numbers: a pass rate, which is the share of cases that scored 4 or more, and a mean judge score on a 1 to 5 scale. A reviewer asks for error bars on both. Which interval do you use for each, and why not the same one?

Level 3 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Use a Wilson interval for the pass rate and a bootstrap interval for the mean score. A pass rate is a proportion, and the Wilson interval stays correct at small samples and near zero or one, where the normal approximation breaks. A mean of ordinal judge scores has no clean closed form, and the bootstrap resamples the scores with replacement and works with any distribution.

The Wilson interval.

center = (p + z^2 / (2n)) / (1 + z^2 / n)
spread = z * sqrt( (p (1 - p) + z^2 / (4n)) / n ) / (1 + z^2 / n)

With z at 1.96 for 95 percent. The center moves toward one half when n is small, and the bounds are clamped to zero and one. At 50 cases and 90 percent accuracy it gives 78 to 97 percent. The normal interval would give bounds that can leave the unit range and a width that is wrong at the extremes.

The bootstrap interval.

Resample the n scores with replacement, compute the mean, repeat a thousand times, sort the means and read the percentiles at alpha over two from each end. It assumes only that the sample represents the population. It cannot repair a biased or tiny sample, and it costs compute rather than algebra.

Comparing two runs.

Neither interval compares two systems by itself. For two prompt versions that ran on the same cases, build the interval on the paired per case difference, by bootstrap over cases or a paired test. Two separate intervals that overlap do not prove equality, and two that do not overlap are not needed to prove a difference.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Plus or minus the standard error is fine." The normal interval fails at small samples and extreme proportions, exactly where releases are decided.
  • "Bootstrap works for the pass rate too, so use it everywhere." It works, but Wilson is closed form, cheap and built for proportions; keep the code auditable.
  • "Overlapping intervals mean no difference." Two separate intervals can overlap while the paired difference excludes zero; compare the difference.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) What goes wrong with a plus or minus one standard error interval on a proportion? Expected: at small n or near zero or one it gives bounds outside the unit range and the wrong width; Wilson corrects both.
  • (level 3) What does a bootstrap assume? Expected: that the sample represents the population; it needs no distribution shape, but it cannot fix a biased or tiny sample.
  • (level 4) Two prompt versions ran on the same 200 cases. Which interval decides? Expected: an interval on the paired per case difference; two overlapping intervals do not prove equality.

Check your understanding

Q1Why is manually reading a few LLM outputs not a reliable evaluation method?

Reading 10 outputs shows you 10 points in a distribution. A prompt change might improve 90% of outputs but break 10% of edge cases. Without systematic evaluation, you'll miss the regression until users report it.

Q2What is regression testing in the context of LLM applications?

Every prompt change, model swap, or temperature tweak changes the output distribution. Regression tests catch cases where a change that improves one area silently degrades another.

Q3What is the LLM-as-judge evaluation approach?

LLM-as-judge sends (input, output, rubric) to a strong model (e.g., GPT-4) which scores the output. It's cheaper and faster than human evaluation, though it has known biases (e.g., preferring verbose responses).

Q4What makes a good evaluation dataset for an LLM application?

A good eval set covers the distribution: happy path cases, edge cases (empty input, very long input), adversarial inputs (prompt injection), and ambiguous queries. Each example has a clear expected output or scoring rubric.

Q5How should you handle non-deterministic LLM outputs in evaluation?

Even at temperature 0, some providers introduce sampling variation. Running each test 3-5 times and measuring pass rate or average score gives a more reliable picture than a single run that might hit a lucky/unlucky sample.
