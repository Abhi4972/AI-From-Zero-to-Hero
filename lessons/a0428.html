The problem

In plain words first

A video generator continues a scene: given a prompt or a first frame, it predicts what the next seconds look like. A world model adds controls: it predicts what happens next given an action, such as "press left" or "turn the steering wheel". Run it in a loop, feeding each predicted frame back in with the next action, and you have a learned game engine or driving simulator that a robot or an agent can practise in. The same ingredients as image generation (latent patches, transformers, diffusion or flow) are used, with time added as a third axis.

Video generation and world modelling have been converging since 2024. A model that can generate a coherent long video has, in some sense, learned how the world moves: object permanence, gravity, causality, style. If you condition that prediction on actions (walk left, open the door), the video model becomes a learnable simulator that can replace a game engine, a driving simulator, or a robotics environment.

The stakes are concrete. Genie 3 generates playable environments from a single image. Runway GWM-1 Worlds synthesises infinite explorable scenes. Sora 2 (September 2025) produced short clips with synchronised audio and noticeably better physics, before OpenAI announced in March 2026 that it would discontinue the Sora app and API; Google's Veo 3.1 and Kuaishou's Kling offer similar capabilities. NVIDIA Cosmos-Drive and Wayve Gaia-2 generate realistic driving video for autonomous-vehicle training data. The world-model paradigm is quietly taking over sim-to-real for robotics.

This lesson is the "big picture" lesson for Phase 4. It connects image generation, video understanding, and agentic reasoning into the architecture pattern dominant research is moving toward.

Idea 1 · Three families of world-modelling

  • Sora 2 is pure video generation conditioned on prompts. No action interface. You cannot "steer" it mid-rollout.
  • Genie 3, GWM-1 Worlds and Mirage 2 are action-conditioned world models. Infer latent actions from observed video, then condition future frame predictions on actions. Interactive — you press keys or move a camera and the scene responds.
  • DreamerV3 and the classic RL world-model family predict in a latent space with explicit action conditioning, trained on a reward signal. Less visual; more useful for sample-efficient RL.

Idea 2 · Video DiT architecture

Video latent:          (C, T, H, W)
Patchify (spatial):    grid of P_h x P_w patches per frame
Patchify (temporal):   group P_t frames into a temporal patch
Resulting tokens:      (T / P_t) * (H / P_h) * (W / P_w) tokens

Positional encoding is 3D: a rotary or learned embedding per (t, h, w) coordinate. Attention can be:

  • Full joint — all tokens attend to all tokens. O(N2) with N tokens. Prohibitive for long videos.
  • Divided — alternate temporal attention (same spatial position, across time: (H*W) * T^2) and spatial attention (same timestep, across space: T * (H*W)^2). Used by TimeSformer and most video DiTs.
  • Window — local windows in (t, h, w). Used by Video Swin.

Most current video diffusion models use one of these three patterns, usually with AdaLN conditioning (Lesson 4.23) and rectified flow.

Worked example
Why divided attention

A 5-second, 24 fps, 480 × 832 clip has 121 frames. A typical video VAE compresses time by 4 and space by 8, giving a latent of T=31, H=60, W=104; 2 × 2 spatial patches leave 30×52=1,560 tokens per latent frame and N=31×1,560=48,360 tokens in all.

  • Full joint attention compares every pair: N2≈2.34 billion pairs per head per layer.
  • Divided attention: temporal HW⋅T2=1,560×961=1.5 million, plus spatial T⋅(HW)2=31×2,433,600=75.4 million, about 77 million.

Divided attention is about 30 times cheaper here, and the gap grows with clip length. The price is that a token never directly attends to a different place at a different time in one step; it takes two hops.

Idea 3 · Conditioning on actions: latent action models

Genie learns a latent action per frame by discriminatively predicting the action between a pair of consecutive frames. The model's decoder then conditions on the inferred latent action — not on explicit keyboard keys. At inference, a user can specify a latent action (or sample one from a fresh prior) and the model generates the next frame consistent with that action.

Sora skips the action interface entirely. Its decoder predicts next spacetime tokens from past spacetime tokens. Prompt conditions the start; nothing steers it mid-generation.

Idea 4 · Physical plausibility

Sora 2's release (September 2025) explicitly advertised physical plausibility: weight, balance, object permanence, cause-and-effect. Measured by the team via hand-rated plausibility scores; the model visibly improves on dropped objects, characters colliding, and failures-on-purpose (a missed jump) versus Sora 1.

Plausibility remains the dominant failure mode. 2024-2025 videos of people eating spaghetti or drinking from glasses revealed the model's lack of persistent object representation. Newer models (Sora 2, Runway Gen-4.5, HunyuanVideo) reduce but do not eliminate these.

Idea 5 · Autonomous driving world models

Driving world models generate realistic road scenes conditioned on trajectories, bounding boxes, or navigation maps. Usage:

  • Cosmos-Drive-Dreams (NVIDIA) — generates minutes of driving video for RL training.
  • Gaia-2 (Wayve) — trajectory-conditioned scene synthesis for policy evaluation.
  • DrivingWorld (academic, 2024) — a GPT-style video world model for driving that simulates varied conditions.
  • Vista (OpenDriveLab, 2024) — a generalisable driving world model with action control.

They replace expensive real-world data collection for corner cases — pedestrian jaywalks at night, icy intersections, unusual vehicle types — that would otherwise require millions of miles of driving.

Idea 6 · Robotics stack: VLM + video model + inverse dynamics

The emerging three-component robotics loop:

  1. VLM parses the goal ("pick up the red cup"), plans a high-level action sequence.
  2. Video generation model simulates what executing each action would look like — predicts observations N frames ahead.
  3. Inverse dynamics model extracts the concrete motor commands that would produce those observations.

This replaces reward shaping and sample-heavy RL. The world model does the imagination; the inverse dynamics closes the loop on actuation. Genie Envisioner is one instantiation; many research groups are converging on this structure.

Idea 7 · Evaluation

  • Visual quality — FVD (Fréchet Video Distance), user studies.
  • Prompt alignment — CLIPScore per frame, VQA-style evaluation.
  • Physical plausibility — hand-rated on a benchmark suite (Sora 2's internal benchmark, VBench).
  • Controllability (for interactive world models) — action → observation consistency; can you go back to a prior state?

The same idea as a formula

A world model predicts the next observation. Given past frames o≤t​ and an action at​, it models p(ot+1​∣o≤t​,at​); rolling it forward imagines trajectories without the real world.

Video as tokens. A video VAE compresses T frames of H×W by factors (ct​,cs​,cs​) in time and space, and a DiT cuts the latent into patches of p×p:

N=ct​T​⋅cs​pH​⋅cs​pW​

For 121 frames of 720×1280 with ct​=4, cs​=8 and p=2, that is about 30×45×80=108,000 tokens, which is why video models need attention optimisations and large clusters. The training objective is the same denoising or flow-matching loss as for images, applied to the whole space-time latent.

Latent actions. Internet video has no action labels. A latent action model learns an inverse-dynamics encoder a^t​=g(ot​,ot+1​) and a forward model that must reconstruct ot+1​ from ot​ and a^t​; a small bottleneck forces a^t​ to capture only what changed. A few labelled robot episodes then map latent actions to real controls.

Evaluation. Fréchet Video Distance (FID's idea on video features) for realism; for control, whether a policy trained or planned inside the model succeeds in the real environment, and physics checks (objects persist, do not pass through each other, fall under gravity).

Step 1 · 3D patchify for video

import torch
import torch.nn as nn


class VideoPatch3D(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_channels=4, dim=64, patch_t=2, patch_h=2, patch_w=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.proj = nn.Conv3d(
            in_channels, dim,
            kernel_size=(patch_t, patch_h, patch_w),
            stride=(patch_t, patch_h, patch_w),
        )
        self.patch_t = patch_t
        self.patch_h = patch_h
        self.patch_w = patch_w

    def forward(self, x):
        # x: (N, C, T, H, W)
        x = self.proj(x)
        n, c, t, h, w = x.shape
        tokens = x.reshape(n, c, t * h * w).transpose(1, 2)
        return tokens, (t, h, w)

A 3D conv with stride equal to kernel acts as the spatio-temporal patchifier. (T, H, W) -> (T/2, H/2, W/2) grid of tokens.

Step 2 · 3D rotary position encoding

Rotary Position Embeddings (RoPE) separately applied along t, h, w axes:

def rope_3d(tokens, t_dim, h_dim, w_dim, grid):
    """
    tokens: (N, T*H*W, D)
    grid: (T, H, W) sizes
    t_dim + h_dim + w_dim == D
    """
    T, H, W = grid
    n, seq, d = tokens.shape
    if t_dim + h_dim + w_dim != d:
        raise ValueError(f"t_dim+h_dim+w_dim ({t_dim}+{h_dim}+{w_dim}) must equal D={d}")
    assert seq == T * H * W
    t_idx = torch.arange(T, device=tokens.device).repeat_interleave(H * W)
    h_idx = torch.arange(H, device=tokens.device).repeat_interleave(W).repeat(T)
    w_idx = torch.arange(W, device=tokens.device).repeat(T * H)
    # Simplified: just scale channels by frequencies. Real RoPE rotates pairs.
    freqs_t = torch.exp(-torch.log(torch.tensor(10000.0)) * torch.arange(t_dim // 2, device=tokens.device) / (t_dim // 2))
    freqs_h = torch.exp(-torch.log(torch.tensor(10000.0)) * torch.arange(h_dim // 2, device=tokens.device) / (h_dim // 2))
    freqs_w = torch.exp(-torch.log(torch.tensor(10000.0)) * torch.arange(w_dim // 2, device=tokens.device) / (w_dim // 2))
    emb_t = torch.cat([torch.sin(t_idx[:, None] * freqs_t), torch.cos(t_idx[:, None] * freqs_t)], dim=-1)
    emb_h = torch.cat([torch.sin(h_idx[:, None] * freqs_h), torch.cos(h_idx[:, None] * freqs_h)], dim=-1)
    emb_w = torch.cat([torch.sin(w_idx[:, None] * freqs_w), torch.cos(w_idx[:, None] * freqs_w)], dim=-1)
    return tokens + torch.cat([emb_t, emb_h, emb_w], dim=-1)

Simplified additive form. Real RoPE rotates paired channels at frequencies; the positional information is the same.

Step 3 · Divided attention block

class DividedAttentionBlock(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, dim=64, heads=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.time_attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(dim, heads, batch_first=True)
        self.space_attn = nn.MultiheadAttention(dim, heads, batch_first=True)
        self.ln1 = nn.LayerNorm(dim)
        self.ln2 = nn.LayerNorm(dim)
        self.ln3 = nn.LayerNorm(dim)
        self.mlp = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(dim, 4 * dim), nn.GELU(), nn.Linear(4 * dim, dim))

    def forward(self, x, grid):
        T, H, W = grid
        n, seq, d = x.shape
        # time attention: same (h, w), across t
        xt = x.view(n, T, H * W, d).permute(0, 2, 1, 3).reshape(n * H * W, T, d)
        a, _ = self.time_attn(self.ln1(xt), self.ln1(xt), self.ln1(xt), need_weights=False)
        xt = (xt + a).reshape(n, H * W, T, d).permute(0, 2, 1, 3).reshape(n, seq, d)
        # space attention: same t, across (h, w)
        xs = xt.view(n, T, H * W, d).reshape(n * T, H * W, d)
        a, _ = self.space_attn(self.ln2(xs), self.ln2(xs), self.ln2(xs), need_weights=False)
        xs = (xs + a).reshape(n, T, H * W, d).reshape(n, seq, d)
        xs = xs + self.mlp(self.ln3(xs))
        return xs

The time attention attends within each spatial position across time; the space attention attends within each frame across positions. Two O(T2+(HW)2) operations instead of one O((THW)2). This is the core of TimeSformer and every modern video DiT.

Step 4 · Compose a tiny video DiT

class TinyVideoDiT(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, in_channels=4, dim=64, depth=2, heads=2):
        super().__init__()
        self.patch = VideoPatch3D(in_channels=in_channels, dim=dim, patch_t=2, patch_h=2, patch_w=2)
        self.blocks = nn.ModuleList([DividedAttentionBlock(dim, heads) for _ in range(depth)])
        self.out = nn.Linear(dim, in_channels * 2 * 2 * 2)

    def forward(self, x):
        tokens, grid = self.patch(x)
        for blk in self.blocks:
            tokens = blk(tokens, grid)
        return self.out(tokens), grid

Not a working video generator; a structural demo that every piece shapes correctly.

See it drawn
A world model you can train in a minute

The "game": a ball on a 16 × 16 grid; actions move it one cell. Two identical small CNNs learn to predict the next frame, one given the action, one with the action input zeroed (a pure video predictor). Then both dream 20 frames from the same start and the same action sequence.

Show the code that draws this 
import time
import torch.nn.functional as F
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

GRID = 16
MOVES = torch.tensor([[0, -1], [0, 1], [-1, 0], [1, 0]])          # up, down, left, right as (dx, dy)
def render(pos):                                                   # a soft ball at each (x, y)
    yy, xx = torch.meshgrid(torch.arange(GRID), torch.arange(GRID), indexing="ij")
    d2 = (xx[None] - pos[:, 0, None, None]) ** 2 + (yy[None] - pos[:, 1, None, None]) ** 2
    return torch.exp(-d2 / 2.0)[:, None].float()
def world_step(pos, action):
    return (pos + MOVES[action]).clamp(0, GRID - 1)

class ToyWorldModel(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, use_action=True):
        super().__init__()
        self.use_action = use_action
        self.net = nn.Sequential(nn.Conv2d(5, 32, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU(), nn.Conv2d(32, 32, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU(),
                                 nn.Conv2d(32, 32, 3, padding=1), nn.ReLU(), nn.Conv2d(32, 1, 3, padding=1))
    def forward(self, frame, action):
        a = F.one_hot(action, 4).float() if self.use_action else torch.zeros(len(action), 4)
        return self.net(torch.cat([frame, a[:, :, None, None].expand(-1, -1, GRID, GRID)], 1))

def train_world_model(use_action, steps=1200):
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    m = ToyWorldModel(use_action)
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(m.parameters(), lr=2e-3)
    for _ in range(steps):
        pos, act = torch.randint(0, GRID, (128, 2)), torch.randint(0, 4, (128,))
        loss = F.mse_loss(m(render(pos), act), render(world_step(pos, act)))
        opt.zero_grad(); loss.backward(); opt.step()
    return m

@torch.no_grad()
def dream(m, start, actions):
    frame, frames = render(start), []
    for a in actions:
        frame = m(frame, a[None]).clamp(min=0)                      # the model's own output is the next input
        frames.append(frame[0, 0])
    return frames

start_time = time.time()
with_action, without_action = train_world_model(True), train_world_model(False)
print(f"trained both models in {time.time() - start_time:.0f} s")

start = torch.tensor([[3, 8]])
plan = torch.tensor([3] * 6 + [0] * 5 + [2] * 4 + [1] * 5)          # right x6, up x5, left x4, down x5
truth, pos = [], start
for a in plan:
    pos = world_step(pos, a[None]); truth.append(render(pos)[0, 0])
dreams = {"true world": truth, "world model (sees actions)": dream(with_action, start, plan),
          "video predictor (no actions)": dream(without_action, start, plan)}
for name, frames in dreams.items():
    err = [float(((torch.tensor([int(f.flatten().argmax()) % GRID, int(f.flatten().argmax()) // GRID]) -
                   torch.tensor([int(t.flatten().argmax()) % GRID, int(t.flatten().argmax()) // GRID])).float().norm()))
           for f, t in zip(frames, truth)]
    print(f"{name:30s} ball position error after 5 / 10 / 20 steps: {err[4]:.1f} / {err[9]:.1f} / {err[19]:.1f} cells")

steer = dream(with_action, start, torch.tensor([1] * 6))           # same start, a different plan: down x6
show = [0, 2, 5, 8, 11, 14, 19]
fig, axes = plt.subplots(4, len(show), figsize=(10, 6))
rows = list(dreams.items()) + [("world model, plan 'down x6'", steer + [steer[-1]] * 14)]
for r, (name, frames) in enumerate(rows):
    for c, k in enumerate(show):
        axes[r, c].imshow(frames[k], cmap="magma", vmin=0, vmax=1); axes[r, c].set_xticks([]); axes[r, c].set_yticks([])
        if r == 0: axes[r, c].set_title(f"step {k + 1}", fontsize=8)
    axes[r, 0].set_ylabel(name, fontsize=7)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

Reading this output

The action-conditioned model follows the true path for the first 15 or so steps: given "right ×6, up ×5, left ×4, down ×5" it moves the ball exactly as the real world would, and given a different plan from the same start (last row) it produces a different future. That is what makes it steerable. Look at its later frames, though: the ball grows brighter and wider, and by step 20 its centre is about two cells off. Each prediction is very slightly wrong, and because predictions are fed back in, the errors compound. The video predictor saw the same frames but no actions, so its best guess is the average of four possible moves; that blur, fed back into itself, spreads out and fades to nothing within a few steps. Real video generators face the same ambiguity (resolved by a prompt at the start rather than an action at every step), and real world models the same compounding error over millions of pixels.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • A video generator continues a scene; a world model predicts the next observation given an action, so it can be steered.
  • Video DiTs patchify space and time and use 3D positions with joint, divided or windowed attention; divided attention cuts cost by orders of magnitude.
  • Latent action models infer actions from unlabelled video, so world models can learn from footage without control labels.
  • Rollouts feed predictions back in, so errors compound; physics and object permanence remain the hard part.
  • Driving and robotics use world models as simulators; check each model's licence and access terms before building on it.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · One stays sharp, the other blurs

The action-conditioned model tracks the true path exactly for the first ten steps or more (the printed errors), because the action removes all ambiguity in this toy world, but small prediction errors compound and the ball drifts by about two cells by step 20. The model without actions cannot know which way the ball will go, so under squared error it predicts the average of the four possible next frames; feeding that blur back in spreads and dims it until nothing is left. Conditioning information decides whether the future is predictable; compounding error decides how long a rollout stays trustworthy.

SolutionB1 · It learns to ignore the controls

With shuffled actions, the action input carries no information about the move, so training drives the network to ignore it: it becomes the no-action video predictor in disguise. Its rollout blurs and drifts just like the bottom row, and changing the plan no longer changes the dream. Real latent-action models face this risk too, which is why Genie-style training makes the latent action the only way to explain the difference between consecutive frames.

4. `quality_target == premium` and `license_need == api_ok` -> **Sora 2** (best quality + synchronised audio) or **Runway Gen-5**.
5. `quality_target in [prototype, production]` and `license_need == permissive` -> **HunyuanVideo** (13B) or **Wan-Video 2.1** (14B).
6. `duration_s > 30` -> **Sora 2** only; open models top out at ~10-20 seconds.
7. default -> **Runway Gen-5** (API) for static video generation.

# Physical Plausibility Checks

Production deployments of generated video need automated guardrails. Human review does not scale; physics checks catch the classic failure modes.

## When to use

- Any product that generates video from text or image prompts.
- Automating QA on a video generation API endpoint.
- Monitoring a video model's quality drift after fine-tuning or a base-model update.

## Inputs

- `video`: a tensor `(T, H, W, 3)` or a path to an mp4.
- Optional reference info: expected number of objects, initial scene description.

## Checks

### 1. Object permanence
Track every detection across frames with SAM 3.1 Object Multiplex. Flag when a stable track disappears for <=3 frames and reappears — the model lost the object temporarily. Hard fail when an object disappears near the frame centre (not at an edge); soft fail at edges.

### 2. Motion smoothness
Optical flow between consecutive frames should be mostly continuous. Sudden per-pixel flow spikes indicate teleportation. Compute flow with RAFT; flag frames where the 99th-percentile flow magnitude exceeds the median by a factor > 10.

### 3. Gravity / support
For objects detected as solid (food, balls, tools), check that their vertical position is non-increasing in the absence of a lifting action. Flag upward drift unless a "grasping hand" is detected near the object.

### 4. Identity consistency
For people or characters, use a face-recognition embedding across frames. Cosine similarity should stay > 0.8 across 5-frame windows for a persistent identity. Below threshold means the character morphed.

### 5. Hands and limbs
Run a pose estimator (Lesson 21). Flag frames where a hand has > 5 or < 4 visible fingers; where an arm length doubles between frames; where limbs intersect the body through a surface.

### 6. Text rendering (if prompt asked for text)
If the user prompt included a string in quotes, OCR the generated frames and compute CER against the requested string. Flag > 20% CER.

## Report

```
[plausibility]
  video frames:           <T>
  permanence violations:  <N>
  smoothness violations:  <N>
  gravity violations:     <N>
  identity drift:         <N of 5-frame windows>
  limb anomalies:         <N>
  OCR CER vs requested:   <float>

[verdict]
  ship | hold | reject

[samples for review]
  frame ranges where each failure occurred
```

## Rules

- Do not hard-block on any single check; aggregate scores and hold the video for review when total anomalies exceed a threshold.
- Weight identity drift and permanence violations highest — users notice them first.
- Log per-check failure rates over time; a rising trend usually means the base model was updated or the prompt distribution shifted.
- Never delete the flagged video; keep it for model debugging and post-mortems.
- For sensitive content (people, children, public figures), require human review of every video regardless of score.
