In plain words first
The idea in one line

The 2026 observability market splits into two categories. Development platforms (LangSmith, Langfuse, Comet Opik) bundle monitoring with evals, prompt management, session replays. Gateway/instrumentation tools (Helicone, SigNoz, OpenLLMetry, Phoenix) focus on telemetry. Langfuse is MIT-licensed core with strong OSS balance (50K events/month free cloud). Phoenix is OpenTelemetry-native under Elastic License 2.0 — excellent for drift/RAG visualization, not a persistent production backend. Arize AX uses zero-copy Iceberg/Parquet integration claiming 100x cheaper than monolithic observability. LangSmith leads for LangChain/LangGraph, $39/user/mo, self-host in Enterprise only. Helicone is proxy-based with 15-30 min setup, 100K req/mo free, but less depth on agent traces. Common production pattern: Gateway (Helicone/Portkey) + eval platform (Phoenix/TruLens) glued by OpenTelemetry.

The problem

In plain words first

Observability means being able to see what your system did and why. For an LLM feature that means recording, for every call, the prompt, the answer, the tokens, the latency, the cost and the model, and linking calls that belong to one user action into a trace. Tools split into two kinds. Development platforms (LangSmith, Langfuse, Opik) add evaluations, prompt versions and datasets, so you can test a new prompt against old cases. Telemetry and gateway tools (Helicone, Phoenix, SigNoz) focus on recording calls and dashboards. OpenTelemetry is the open standard for sending this data, and its GenAI conventions name the fields (gen_ai.request.model, gen_ai.usage.input_tokens and so on), so one instrumentation can feed several tools. Because traces are big, production systems keep only a sample, chosen so that errors and expensive calls are never thrown away.

You shipped an LLM feature. It works. You have no visibility into prompt failures, tool loops, latency regressions, cost spikes, or prompt-cache hit rate. You Google "LLM observability" and get eight tools all claiming they solve the same problem at three different price points.

They don't solve the same problem. LangSmith answers "why did this LangGraph run fail?" Phoenix answers "is my RAG pipeline drifting?" Helicone answers "which app is burning tokens?" Langfuse answers "can I self-host the whole thing?" Different tools, different audiences.

Picking involves four axes: stack (LangChain? raw SDK? multi-vendor?), license tolerance (MIT only? Elastic OK? commercial fine?), budget (free tier? $100/mo? $1000/mo?), and self-host (must? nice-to-have? never?).

Idea 1 · Two categories

Development platforms bundle observability with evals, prompt management, dataset versioning, session replay. You run experiments, see which prompt worked, dataset-regression a new prompt against old winners. LangSmith, Langfuse, Comet Opik.

Gateway/telemetry tools instrument inference calls — prompt, response, tokens, latency, model, cost. Helicone, SigNoz, OpenLLMetry, Phoenix. Minimalist. Can be combined with a separate eval tool via OpenTelemetry.

Idea 2 · Langfuse — OSS balance

  • Core Apache / MIT licensed; self-host via Docker.
  • Cloud free tier: 50K events/month. Paid: $29/mo for team.
  • Evals, prompt management, traces, datasets. Reasonable coverage of all four dev-platform features.
  • Sweet spot: you want LangSmith-class features but must self-host or stay on OSS license.

Idea 3 · Phoenix (Arize) — telemetry-first, OpenTelemetry-native

  • Elastic License 2.0; self-host trivial.
  • Excellent at RAG and drift visualization. Embedding-space scatter plots shipped as first-class.
  • Not designed as persistent production backend — primarily development-time observability.
  • Sweet spot: RAG pipeline development, drift debugging, pairs with a separate gateway for production.

Idea 4 · Arize AX — the scale play

  • Commercial. Zero-copy data lake integration via Iceberg/Parquet.
  • Claims ~100x cheaper than monolithic observability (Datadog-class) at scale. The math: you store traces in your own Parquet on S3; Arize reads directly.
  • Sweet spot: >10M traces/day, existing data lake, want LLM-specific dashboards without Datadog pricing.

Idea 5 · LangSmith — LangChain/LangGraph first

  • Commercial, $39/user/month. Self-host only on Enterprise.
  • Best-in-class for LangChain and LangGraph stacks. If you are not on either, it is less compelling.
  • Sweet spot: team committed to LangChain, willing to pay.

Idea 6 · Helicone — proxy-based minimum viable

  • 15-30 minute setup by swapping your OPENAI_API_BASE to Helicone proxy.
  • MIT licensed; 100K req/mo free, paid $20/mo+.
  • Includes failover, caching, rate limits — acts as a gateway too.
  • Less depth on agent / multi-step traces.
  • Sweet spot: quick start, single-stack app, need gateway + observability in one.

Idea 7 · Opik (Comet) — OSS dev platform

  • Apache 2.0, fully OSS.
  • Similar feature set to Langfuse with Comet heritage.
  • Sweet spot: ML teams already on Comet, want LLM observability in the same pane.

Idea 8 · SigNoz — OpenTelemetry-first full APM

  • Apache 2.0. Handles general APM plus LLM via OpenTelemetry.
  • Sweet spot: unified observability across services and LLM calls.

Idea 9 · The glue: OpenTelemetry + GenAI semantic conventions

OpenTelemetry published GenAI semantic conventions in late 2025 (gen_ai.system, gen_ai.request.model, gen_ai.usage.input_tokens). Tools that consume OTel can interoperate. The production pattern emerging:

  1. Emit OTel with GenAI conventions from every LLM call.
  2. Route to gateway (Helicone / Portkey) for day-to-day.
  3. Dual-ship to eval platform (Phoenix / Langfuse) for regressions.
  4. Archive in data lake (Iceberg) for long-term analysis via Arize AX or DuckDB.

Idea 10 · The trap: instrumenting at the wrong layer

Instrumenting inside your agent framework (e.g., adding LangSmith traces) couples you to that framework. Instrumenting at the HTTP/OpenAI-SDK layer (via OpenLLMetry or your gateway) is portable.

Idea 11 · Sampling — you can't keep everything

At >1M requests/day, full-trace retention costs more than the LLM calls. Sample by rules: 100% errors, 100% high-cost, 5% success. Keep aggregates always; keep raw for the long tail.

Idea 12 · Numbers you should remember

  • Langfuse free cloud: 50K events/month.
  • LangSmith: $39/user/month.
  • Helicone free: 100K req/month.
  • Arize AX claim: ~100x cheaper than monolithic at scale.
  • OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions: 2025 shipping, 2026 widely adopted.

Worked example
What a sampling policy keeps

A service handles 1,000,000 traces a day. 3% are errors and 1% are unusually expensive (over $0.10).

  1. Keep everything: 1,000,000 traces.
  2. Random 10%: 100,000 traces, but only about 3,000 of the 30,000 errors. Most failures you will need to debug are gone.
  3. "5% of successes + all errors": 0.05 × 970,000 + 30,000 ≈ 78,500 traces, every error kept.
  4. Add "all expensive calls": about 10,000 more, so about 88,000 traces, under 9% of the volume, with every error and every cost outlier.

Sample by what you will ask later, not uniformly.

Check your mental model

Which statements are right?

  • ✓ Instrumenting at the SDK or HTTP layer keeps you portable between frameworks.
  • ✗ Random sampling is fine because errors are rare. Rare is exactly why random sampling loses them; keep 100% of errors.
  • ✓ OpenTelemetry's GenAI conventions let several tools read the same traces.
  • ✗ LangSmith is the best choice for every stack. It is strongest on LangChain and LangGraph; elsewhere, other tools fit better.
Predict before you run

Before running the code below: with errors at 3% of traffic, roughly what share of errors does a 10% random sample keep?

See it drawn
Random sampling against a rule-based sample
import random
random.seed(5)
traces = [{"error": random.random() < 0.03, "cost": random.expovariate(1 / 0.02)} for _ in range(200_000)]
errors = sum(t["error"] for t in traces)

def keep_random(t):  return random.random() < 0.10
def keep_rules(t):   return t["error"] or t["cost"] > 0.10 or random.random() < 0.05

for name, rule in (("random 10%", keep_random), ("5% + errors + expensive", keep_rules)):
    kept = [t for t in traces if rule(t)]
    print(f"{name:24s} kept {len(kept) / len(traces):5.1%} of traces, {sum(t['error'] for t in kept) / errors:5.1%} of errors")
What you should see
random 10%               kept 10.0% of traces,  9.7% of errors

Reading this output

Both policies keep roughly the same volume, under 10%, but random sampling keeps only about a tenth of the errors while the rule-based policy keeps all of them. The cost of the rule is a little more storage; the benefit is that every incident has its traces.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

code/main.py simulates a 1M-trace day across retention strategies (100% ingest, sampling, sampling + errors). Reports storage cost and what's lost under each.

Here is code/main.py, in 3 parts. Each part runs on this page, in order, in one Python session; the last part does what python3 code/main.py does.

Part 1 of 3 · Strategy, simulate_day

"""Observability sampling and cost simulator — stdlib Python.

Simulates a 1M-trace day across retention strategies. Reports storage cost
and what's lost under each. Pedagogical: costs are 2026 approximations.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass
import random


BYTES_PER_TRACE = 4_500            # prompt + response + metadata
COST_PER_GB_MONTH = 0.023          # S3 standard
OBSERVABILITY_INGEST_PER_GB = 0.50 # e.g. Datadog-class
ARIZE_AX_PER_GB = 0.005            # zero-copy claim


@dataclass
class Strategy:
    name: str
    sample_rate: float
    keep_errors: bool
    keep_highcost: bool


STRATEGIES = [
    Strategy("100% retain",                1.00, True, True),
    Strategy("10% random sample",          0.10, False, False),
    Strategy("5% success + 100% errors",   0.05, True, False),
    Strategy("5% success + errors + $$$",  0.05, True, True),
    Strategy("1% aggregates only",         0.01, True, True),
]


def simulate_day(strategy: Strategy, traces_per_day: int = 1_000_000) -> dict:
    rng = random.Random(7)
    retained = 0
    lost = 0
    for i in range(traces_per_day):
        is_error = rng.random() < 0.02
        is_highcost = rng.random() < 0.01
        keep = rng.random() < strategy.sample_rate
        if strategy.keep_errors and is_error:
            keep = True
        if strategy.keep_highcost and is_highcost:
            keep = True
        if keep:
            retained += 1
        else:
            lost += 1
    bytes_retained = retained * BYTES_PER_TRACE
    gb = bytes_retained / 1e9
    return {
        "name": strategy.name,
        "retained": retained,
        "lost": lost,
        "gb_per_day": gb,
        "s3_month": gb * 30 * COST_PER_GB_MONTH,
        "monolithic_month": gb * 30 * OBSERVABILITY_INGEST_PER_GB,
        "arize_month": gb * 30 * ARIZE_AX_PER_GB,
    }

Part 2 of 3 · report, main

def report(row: dict) -> None:
    print(f"{row['name']:30}  retained={row['retained']:7}  "
          f"lost={row['lost']:7}  {row['gb_per_day']:6.2f} GB/day  "
          f"mono=${row['monolithic_month']:8.2f}  "
          f"arize=${row['arize_month']:6.2f}  "
          f"s3=${row['s3_month']:5.2f}")


def main() -> None:
    print("=" * 120)
    print("OBSERVABILITY SAMPLING — 1M traces/day, 2026 price approximations")
    print("=" * 120)
    for s in STRATEGIES:
        report(simulate_day(s))

    print()
    print("Read: 100% retention on Datadog-class costs hundreds of $/day.")
    print("5% success + 100% errors + high-cost keeps signal, cuts 90% of bill.")
    print("Arize AX zero-copy pattern wins at scale when you already have a data lake.")

Break it on purpose

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Development platforms add evals and prompt management; telemetry tools record calls.
  • Emit OpenTelemetry with GenAI conventions; send it to a gateway, an eval platform and an archive.
  • Instrument at the SDK or HTTP layer, not inside one framework.
  • Sample: all errors, all expensive calls, a few per cent of the rest.
  • Pick by stack, licence, budget and whether you must self-host.

Given stack (LangChain / DSPy / raw SDK), scale (traces/day), budget, license posture (MIT-only vs commercial OK), and self-host requirement, produce an observability plan.

Produce:

1. Development platform choice. Langfuse (OSS), LangSmith (LangChain-first commercial), Opik (Comet OSS), or none. Justify with stack and license.
2. Gateway/telemetry choice. Helicone (proxy + gateway), SigNoz (full APM), OpenLLMetry (pure OTel). If already using an AI gateway (Phase 17 · 19), name the integration.
3. Scale/lake layer. Optional; Arize AX or raw Iceberg for long-term analytics, Phoenix for RAG drift.
4. OTel GenAI conventions. Specify the minimum attribute set: `gen_ai.system`, `gen_ai.request.model`, `gen_ai.usage.input_tokens`, `gen_ai.usage.output_tokens`, `gen_ai.request.temperature`, `gen_ai.response.finish_reasons`, plus org-specific (tenant_id, user_id, task).
5. Sampling policy. 100% errors, 100% high-cost (>$0.10/call), N% success sampling rate. Raw-retention window (14d / 30d / 90d). Aggregates retained longer.
6. Alerting. Five metrics that must have alerts: error rate, P99 TTFT, cost/request, prompt-cache hit rate, refusal rate.

Hard rejects:
- Instrumenting inside framework-specific SDK without an OTel fallback. Refuse — framework lock-in.
- Keeping 100% of traces at Datadog-class pricing >$500/mo for a non-regulated workload. Refuse — recommend sampling.
- Ignoring OpenTelemetry GenAI conventions. Refuse — 2026 interop requires them.

Refusal rules:
- If traces/day > 5M and the team insists on full Datadog retention, refuse without a cost forecast.
- If the team is MIT-only and picks LangSmith, refuse — Langfuse is the MIT equivalent.
- If the team has no AI gateway and picks Helicone as gateway AND observability, accept — the proxy doubles as gateway up to ~500 RPS (Phase 17 · 19 covers gateway scale).

Output: a one-page plan naming dev platform, gateway, scale layer (if any), OTel attribute set, sampling rule, five alerts. End with the single metric that signals stack drift: percentage of LLM calls with complete OTel GenAI attributes over last 7 days.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Sampling cost in code/main.py

At 1M traces a day, keeping everything costs about $67.50 a day on a Datadog-class backend; "5% of successes + all errors + high-cost" costs about $5.30 and keeps the signal you debug with. On a data-lake pattern (Arize AX reading your own Parquet) even full retention costs well under a dollar a day, which is why large teams store everything there and sample only the expensive backend.

SolutionB1 · Framework-coupled tracing

Instrument at the model-call layer, through OpenTelemetry with the GenAI conventions (OpenLLMetry or the gateway), so every framework's calls produce the same spans. Framework-specific traces can be added on top for detail, but the baseline must not depend on one framework.

Interview questions

Try answering out loud first, then open one answer at a time.

Questions from real AI engineering interviews that this lesson prepares you for. Answer out loud first, then open the worked answer.

Defend a designYour service makes more than a million model calls a day. Keeping every trace now costs more than the model calls themselves. Design a retention policy that keeps the traces you will need during an incident and drops the rest.

Level 2 of 4 · about 10 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Sample by rule, not by a flat rate. Keep every errored trace and every high-cost trace, keep a small share of the successes, and keep the aggregates always. Make the decision at the collector with a tail sampler after the trace completes, so that the rule can see the status and the cost. The capstone keeps all errored traces and 10 percent of the successes.

Why a flat rate fails.

A flat 5 percent keeps 5 percent of the errors too. The trace you need in an incident is the one you dropped, and the aggregate error count tells you only that it existed.

The rules.

Class Keep
errored traces all of them
high-cost traces, long outputs or expensive models all of them
successes 5 to 10 percent
aggregates always, from every request

The observability lesson states the rule as 100 percent of errors, 100 percent of high cost and 5 percent of successes. Aggregates do not need traces: counters and histograms count every request even when the trace is dropped.

Decide at the tail.

Head sampling decides when the trace starts, before anything knows whether it will fail. Tail sampling at the collector decides after the trace completes, so it can read the status, the cost and the eval score. The price is buffer memory at the collector, because whole traces wait until the decision.

Stratify, and know the bias.

The capstone's exercise keeps every trace with a toxicity score above 0.5 plus a 10 percent stratified sample of the rest, and asks you to measure the bias that the sampling adds. Any analysis over sampled traces overweights the classes you kept in full. Weight by the inverse of the sampling rate, or run the per-trace evals on the stratified share only.

Tier the storage.

Spans go to a columnar store for queries, and raw events go to object storage for the long tail. The archive is cheap, and long-term analysis runs there.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Sample 5 percent of everything." Most of the errors you need in an incident are gone, and the aggregate tells you only that they happened.
  • "Keep everything for a week, then delete it." A week of full traces at this volume is the cost you are trying to remove.
  • "Traces are enough, drop the metrics." A percentile over an hour needs every request, which only a histogram gives.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why does head sampling lose the errors? Expected: it decides before the span ends, so it cannot see the status.
  • (level 2) What survives from a dropped trace? Expected: the metrics. Counters and histograms count every request, whether or not its trace is kept.
  • (level 3) Your eval jobs run over the sampled traces. What bias do they inherit? Expected: a sample that keeps all errors overweights failures, so weight by the inverse sampling rate or evaluate the stratified share only.
  • (level 3) The collector runs out of memory during a burst. Why, and what do you change? Expected: tail sampling buffers whole traces until they complete. Cap the buffer, shorten the decision wait, and shed with a head rule under pressure.

Where it is taught. The observability stack lesson gives the sampling rules, and the dashboard capstone configures the tail sampler and measures its bias.
