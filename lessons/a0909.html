In plain words first
The idea in one line

Humans cannot write a reward function for "good assistant response," but they can compare two responses and pick the better one. Fit a reward model to those comparisons, then RL the language model against it. Christiano 2017. InstructGPT 2022. The recipe that turned GPT-3 into ChatGPT. Today, offline preference methods such as DPO and RL with automatically checked rewards do much of this work — but the mental model stays.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
RLHF "Alignment RL" Three-stage SFT + RM + PPO pipeline (Christiano 2017, Ouyang 2022).
Reward Model (RM) "The scoring net" Learned scalar function fit to pairwise preferences via Bradley-Terry.
Bradley-Terry "Pairwise logistic loss" $P(y_+ \succ y_-) = \sigma(R(y_+) - R(y_-))$; the standard RM objective.
KL penalty "Stay near the reference" $\beta \cdot \mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta \Vert \pi_{\text{ref}})$ in the reward; the anti-reward-hacking regularizer.
Reward hacking "Goodhart's law" Policy exploits RM flaws; symptoms: reward up, human eval flat.
RLAIF "AI-labeled preferences" RLHF where labels come from another LM instead of humans.
PRM "Process Reward Model" Scores partial reasoning steps; used in reasoning pipelines.
Constitutional AI "Anthropic's method" AI-generated preferences guided by explicit rules.

The problem

In plain words first

Nobody can write down a formula for "a good answer", but people can look at two answers and say which one is better. RLHF (reinforcement learning from human feedback) turns many such comparisons into a reward model: a network that reads a prompt and a reply and outputs one number, trained so that the reply people preferred gets the higher number. A language model is then tuned with reinforcement learning to write replies that score well, with a penalty for drifting too far from where it started, because a reward model is only trustworthy on the kind of replies it was trained on. This lesson runs the lesson's toy pipeline, computes exactly what the penalty does and how optimising a flawed reward model goes wrong, and then scores real conversations and real model replies with a current open reward model. No model is trained or fine-tuned.

You trained a language model on the next-token-prediction objective. It writes grammatical English. It also lies, rambles, and refuses to refuse. You cannot fix this with more pretraining — web text is the problem, not the cure.

You want a scalar reward that says "response A is better than response B for instruction X." Writing that reward function by hand is impossible. "Helpfulness" is not a closed-form expression over tokens. But humans can compare two outputs and mark a preference. That is cheap to collect at scale.

RLHF (Christiano et al. 2017; Ouyang et al. 2022) converts preferences into a reward model, then optimizes the LM via PPO against that reward. In three steps: SFT → RM → PPO. It is the recipe behind ChatGPT and the first generation of aligned chat assistants.

For preference tuning, the PPO step is now often replaced by DPO (Phase 10 · 08), which is cheaper and simpler; online RL against reward models and verifiers is still used where the extra quality pays for itself. But the reward model piece still underlies every Best-of-N sampler, every RL-from-verifiable-rewards pipeline, and every reasoning model using a process reward model. Understand RLHF and you understand the entire alignment stack.

The idea

Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). Start from a pretrained base model. Fine-tune on human-written demonstrations of the target behavior (instruction-following responses, helpful replies, etc.). Result: a model πSFT​ that is biased toward good behavior but still has an unbounded action space.

Stage 2: Reward Model training.

  • Collect pairs of responses (y+​,y−​) to prompts x, labeled by humans as "y_+ is preferred over y_-."
  • Train a reward model Rϕ​(x,y) to assign higher scores to y+​.
  • Loss: the Bradley-Terry pairwise logistic:

L(ϕ)=−E[logσ(Rϕ​(x,y+​)−Rϕ​(x,y−​))]

σ is the sigmoid. The difference in reward implies a log-odds of preference. BT has been the standard since 1952 (Bradley-Terry) and is the dominant choice in modern RLHF.

  • Rϕ​ is usually initialized from the SFT model with a scalar head on top. Same transformer backbone; a single linear layer outputs the reward.

Stage 3: PPO against the RM with KL penalty.

  • Initialize the trainable policy πθ​ from πSFT​. Keep a frozen reference πref​=πSFT​.
  • Reward at the end of a response y:

rtotal​(x,y)=Rϕ​(x,y)−β⋅KL(πθ​(⋅∣x)∥πref​(⋅∣x))

The KL penalty prevents πθ​ from drifting arbitrarily from πSFT​ — it is a regularizer, not a hard trust region. β typically 0.01-0.05. - Run PPO (Lesson 9.8) with this reward. Advantages are computed on the token-level trajectory, but the RM scores only the full response.

Why the KL? Without it, PPO will happily find reward-hacking strategies — the RM was only trained on in-distribution completions. An out-of-distribution response might score higher than any human-written one. The KL keeps πθ​ near the manifold where the RM was trained. It is the single most important knob in RLHF.

Where things stand now:

  • DPO (Rafailov 2023): closed-form algebra collapses Stage 2+3 into a single supervised loss over preference data. No RM, no PPO. Competitive on many alignment benchmarks for a fraction of the compute; careful comparisons still find online RL with a good reward model somewhat stronger. Covered in Phase 10 · 08.
  • GRPO (DeepSeek 2024–2025): PPO with a group-relative baseline instead of a critic, reward from a verifier (code runs / math answer matches) instead of a human-trained RM. The usual choice for training open reasoning models. Covered in Phase 9 · 12.
  • Process reward models (PRMs): score partial solutions (each reasoning step), used in both RLHF and GRPO variants for reasoning.
  • Constitutional AI / RLAIF: use an aligned LLM to generate preferences instead of humans. Scales the preference budget.

The same idea as a formula

Bradley–Terry. The probability that a labeler prefers y+​ to y−​ is modelled as

P(y+​≻y−​∣x)=σ(R(x,y+​)−R(x,y−​)),L=−logσ(Δ),Δ=R(x,y+​)−R(x,y−​)

with gradient ∂L/∂Δ=−(1−σ(Δ)): pairs the model already gets confidently right contribute almost nothing. Adding a constant to every reward changes nothing, so the scale and offset of an RM's scores carry no meaning by themselves; only differences between replies to the same prompt do.

The KL-regularised objective. RLHF maximises, for each prompt,

J(π)=Ey∼π​[R(x,y)]−βKL(π(⋅∣x)∥πref​(⋅∣x))

and the maximiser has a closed form:

π∗(y∣x)=Z(x)πref​(y∣x)eR(x,y)/β​,J(π)=βlogZ(x)−βKL(π∥π∗)

so the best policy re-weights the reference by eR/β: small β chases reward, large β stays put. DPO (Phase 10 · 08) starts from this formula.

Per-sample penalty. In practice the penalty is applied to each sampled reply, r(x,y)=R(x,y)−βlogπref​(y∣x)π(y∣x)​ (summed over tokens for text), whose average over y∼π is exactly R−βKL.

Best-of-N. Sampling N replies from πref​ and keeping the one with the highest R needs no training, and moves the output distribution by KL≤logN−NN−1​.

Worked example
Bradley–Terry and the optimum by hand

One preference pair. R(y+​)=1.2, R(y−​)=0.4, so Δ=0.8:

  • P(y+​≻y−​)=σ(0.8)=0.690; loss =−ln0.690=0.371.
  • Gradient scale 1−0.690=0.310: every token of y+​ gains 0.310×lr, every token of y−​ loses the same, as in rm_train_step.

The optimum for two replies. πref​=(0.5,0.5), rewards R=(1,0), β=0.5:

  • Weights 0.5e1/0.5=3.695 and 0.5e0=0.5; Z=4.195, so π∗=(0.881,0.119).
  • KL(π∗∥πref​)=0.881ln0.50.881​+0.119ln0.50.119​=0.499−0.171=0.328.
  • J=0.881−0.5×0.328=0.717, and βlogZ=0.5ln4.195=0.717: the formula checks out.

With β=0.1 the same computation gives π∗=(0.99995,0.00005): almost all probability on the higher-scored reply, whatever its true quality.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "The reward model measures how good a reply is."
    ✓ It predicts which of two replies a labeler would pick, biases included; in the reward-model run under Use it, appending a content-free polite sentence raises the score of 11 of 12 replies.
  • ✗ "Higher reward model score means a better model."
    ✓ Only up to a point: in the run after Step 4 the true quality peaks and then falls while the RM score keeps rising.
  • ✗ "The KL penalty is a number subtracted from the reward, so any form works."
    ✓ It has to differ between the samples it compares; a penalty that is the same for every reply to a prompt is cancelled by the baseline and does almost nothing (Solution P1).
Predict before you run

The lesson's main.py trains the toy policy with three KL coefficients, β=0.01, 0.1 and 1.0, a hundredfold range. Its KL term is kl(probs_new, probs_ref), the KL of the whole current distribution, the same for every sample of a prompt. How different will the three final KLs be? (Solution P1.)

Step 1 · synthetic preference data

This lesson uses tiny synthetic "prompts" and "responses" represented as strings. The RM is a linear scorer over a bag-of-tokens representation. No real LLM — the shape of the pipeline matters, not the scale. See code/main.py.

PROMPTS = ["help me", "answer me", "explain this"]
GOOD_WORDS = {"clear", "specific", "kind", "thorough"}
BAD_WORDS = {"vague", "rude", "wrong", "short"}

def make_pair(rng):
    x = rng.choice(PROMPTS)
    y_good = rng.choice(list(GOOD_WORDS)) + " " + rng.choice(list(GOOD_WORDS))
    y_bad = rng.choice(list(BAD_WORDS)) + " " + rng.choice(list(BAD_WORDS))
    return (x, y_good, y_bad)

In real RLHF this is replaced by human labelers. The shape — (prompt, preferred_response, rejected_response) — is identical.

Step 2 · Bradley-Terry reward model

Linear score: R(x, y) = w · bag(y). Train to minimize the BT pairwise log-loss:

def rm_train_step(w, x, y_pos, y_neg, lr):
    r_pos = dot(w, bag(y_pos))
    r_neg = dot(w, bag(y_neg))
    p = sigmoid(r_pos - r_neg)
    for tok, cnt in bag(y_pos).items():
        w[tok] += lr * (1 - p) * cnt
    for tok, cnt in bag(y_neg).items():
        w[tok] -= lr * (1 - p) * cnt

After a few hundred updates, w assigns positive weights to good-word tokens and negative to bad.

Step 3 · PPO-like policy on top of RM

Our toy policy produces a single token from a vocabulary. We score the token under the RM, compute logπθ​(token∣prompt), add a KL-to-reference penalty, and apply the clipped PPO surrogate.

def rlhf_step(theta, ref, w, prompt, rng, eps=0.2, beta=0.1, lr=0.05):
    logits_theta = policy_logits(theta, prompt)
    probs = softmax(logits_theta)
    token = sample(probs, rng)
    logits_ref = policy_logits(ref, prompt)
    probs_ref = softmax(logits_ref)
    reward = dot(w, bag([token])) - beta * kl(probs, probs_ref)

Step 4 · monitor the KL

Track mean KL(πθ​∥πref​) every update. If it creeps past ~5-10 (per-response KL in nats, summed over tokens, the unit TRL logs) the policy has drifted far from πSFT​: either β is too low (raise it to pull the policy back) or reward hacking is starting. This is the top diagnostic in real RLHF.

Stage 1. The reward model learns what the synthetic labels encode: every "good" word gets a weight near +1.1, every "bad" word near −1.1, and it ranks 100% of held-out pairs correctly. That is easy here because every preferred reply is made of good words; real preference data is far messier (the run under Use it).

Stage 2. All three runs end almost in the same place, with final KLs of 2.29, 2.27 and 1.83 although β varies a hundredfold. The largest possible KL from the uniform reference over 12 tokens is ln12=2.48, so every run has become nearly deterministic, β=1 included. The reason is the form of the penalty: kl(probs_new, probs_ref) is the KL of the whole current distribution, identical for every sample of a prompt, and the advantage normalisation subtracts the batch mean, which removes most of it. Real RLHF applies the penalty per sample, −βlogπref​(y)π(y)​, which is large exactly for the replies the policy has made much more likely. The first part of the next run shows where the correct objective lands: KL 0.37 at β=1.

The exact optimum and over-optimisation

Everything exact, no sampling. A: the closed-form optimum π∗∝πref​eR/β for the lesson's reward model. B: 2,000 candidate replies to one prompt with a "gold" score (what people actually prefer) and a reward model that is right on average but noisy and fond of padding; for decreasing β (KL-regularised optimum) and increasing N (Best-of-N), the KL from the reference, the RM score and the gold score.

import numpy as np, random
from main import train_rm, VOCAB

# A. What the toy loop should converge to: the KL-regularised optimum pi*(y) = pi_ref(y) exp(R(y)/beta) / Z
w = train_rm(n_pairs=600, rng=random.Random(42))
R = np.array([w[t] for t in VOCAB]); ref = np.full(len(VOCAB), 1 / len(VOCAB))
def tilt(ref, r, beta):
    z = np.log(ref) + r / beta; p = np.exp(z - z.max()); return p / p.sum()
def kl(p, q): return float(np.sum(p * np.log(p / q)))
print("A. exact optimum for the lesson's reward model (uniform reference over 12 one-token responses)")
print(" beta | expected RM score | KL(pi* || ref) | P(a good token)")
for beta in (0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0):
    p = tilt(ref, R, beta)
    print(f" {beta:>4} | {p @ R:>17.3f} | {kl(p, ref):>14.3f} | {p[R > 0].sum():>14.3f}")

# B. Over-optimisation: a reward model that is right on average but wrong in one systematic way
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 2000                                    # candidate responses to one prompt
gold = rng.normal(0, 1, n)                  # what people actually prefer
padded = rng.random(n) < 0.05               # 5% are padded with filler
gold[padded] -= 1.0                         # people dislike the padding ...
rm = gold + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)           # ... the learned RM is noisy ...
rm[padded] += 2.5                           # ... and rewards length
ref = rng.dirichlet(np.ones(n))             # the SFT model's probabilities for the candidates
print()
print(f"B. {n} candidate responses; RM vs gold correlation {np.corrcoef(rm, gold)[0, 1]:.2f}; {padded.mean():.0%} padded")
print("   KL-regularised optimum                         | best-of-N under the reference")
print("   beta |   KL | RM score | gold score | padded   |    N |   KL | RM score | gold score")
order = np.argsort(rm); cdf = np.cumsum(ref[order])
rows = []
for beta, N in zip((4.0, 1.0, 0.5, 0.3, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05), (1, 4, 16, 64, 256, 1024, 4096)):

print(f"   best possible gold score: {gold.max():.2f}; the reference's own: {ref @ gold:.2f}")

Step 5 · the production recipe with TRL

Once you understand the toy pipeline, here is the same loop as a real library user writes it. Hugging Face's TRL is the reference implementation — RewardTrainer for Stage 2 and PPOTrainer (with a KL-to-reference built in) for Stage 3.

# Stage 2: reward model from pairwise preferences (TRL 1.x)
from datasets import load_dataset
from transformers import AutoModelForSequenceClassification, AutoTokenizer
from trl import RewardConfig, RewardTrainer

base = "Qwen/Qwen3-0.6B"                       # start from your SFT model in practice
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(base)
rm = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained(base, num_labels=1)   # backbone + one scalar head
rm.config.pad_token_id = tok.pad_token_id

# rows: {"chosen": [...messages...], "rejected": [...messages...]}: the Bradley-Terry format
prefs = load_dataset("trl-lib/ultrafeedback_binarized", split="train")
trainer = RewardTrainer(
    model=rm,
    processing_class=tok,
    train_dataset=prefs,
    args=RewardConfig(output_dir="./rm", num_train_epochs=1, learning_rate=1e-5,
                      center_rewards_coefficient=0.01),   # keeps the scores centred near 0
)
trainer.train()
trainer.save_model("./rm")

# Stage 3: PPO against the RM with a KL penalty to the frozen SFT reference (TRL 1.x)
from datasets import load_dataset
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoModelForSequenceClassification, AutoTokenizer
from trl.experimental.ppo import PPOConfig, PPOTrainer      # PPO now lives under trl.experimental

sft = "Qwen/Qwen3-0.6B"                         # your SFT checkpoint
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(sft, padding_side="left")
policy = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(sft)
ref = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(sft)                                      # frozen copy
rm = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained("./rm", num_labels=1)       # from Stage 2
value = AutoModelForSequenceClassification.from_pretrained("./rm", num_labels=1)    # critic, initialised from the RM

prompts = load_dataset("trl-lib/ultrafeedback_binarized", split="train").select(range(10_000))
prompts = prompts.map(lambda r: {"input_ids": tok.apply_chat_template(r["chosen"][:-1], add_generation_prompt=True)},
                      remove_columns=prompts.column_names)

trainer = PPOTrainer(
    args=PPOConfig(output_dir="./ppo", learning_rate=3e-6, kl_coef=0.05, cliprange=0.2,
                   num_ppo_epochs=4, response_length=128, total_episodes=20_000, missing_eos_penalty=1.0),
    processing_class=tok, model=policy, ref_model=ref, reward_model=rm, value_model=value,
    train_dataset=prompts,
)
trainer.train()
# logged every few steps: objective/scores (RM), objective/kl, objective/rlhf_reward, policy/clipfrac_avg, loss/value_avg

Three things the library does for you. adap_kl_ctrl=True implements the adaptive-β schedule: if observed KL exceeds target_kl, β doubles; if below half, β halves. The reference model is frozen by convention — you must not accidentally share parameters with policy. And the value head lives on the same backbone as the policy (AutoModelForCausalLMWithValueHead attaches a scalar MLP head), which is why TRL reports policy/kl and value/loss separately.

Pitfalls

  • Over-optimization / reward hacking. The RM is imperfect; πθ​ finds adversarial completions that score high but are bad. Symptoms: reward climbs indefinitely while human eval score plateaus or drops. Fix: stop early, raise β, broaden RM training data.
  • Length hacking. RMs trained on helpful responses often implicitly reward length. The policy learns to pad responses. Remediation: length-normalized reward, or RLAIF with a length-aware RM.
  • Too-weak RM. A small or under-trained RM is easier to exploit. RMs smaller than the policy are common (InstructGPT used a 6B RM for a 175B policy), but larger RMs over-optimise more slowly (Gao et al. 2022).
  • KL tuning. Too low β → drift and reward hacking. Too high β → policy barely changes. The standard trick is an adaptive β that targets a fixed KL per step.
  • Preference-data noise. Labelers often disagree: in InstructGPT, trained labelers agreed with each other on about 73% of comparisons. Noise shrinks the reward gaps the RM learns (Solution B1); filter for agreement or collect several labels per pair.
  • Off-policy problems. PPO data is slightly off-policy after the first epoch. Monitor clip fraction as in Lesson 08.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

Production post-training is layered:

Layer Target Method
Instruction following, helpfulness, harmlessness Alignment DPO-family offline methods (Phase 10 · 08) are common; online RL against an RM where a strong RM exists.
Reasoning correctness (math, code) Capability GRPO with verifier reward (Phase 9 · 12).
Long-horizon multi-step tasks Agentic PPO / GRPO with process reward models over steps.
Safety / refusal behavior Safety RLHF-PPO with separate safety RM, or Constitutional AI.
Best-of-N at inference Fast alignment Use RM at decode time; no policy training needed.
Reward distillation Inference compute Train a small "reward head" on top of a frozen LM.

Open post-training recipes such as Tülu 3 chain SFT, offline preference optimisation (DPO) and RL with verifiable rewards; Llama 3 used a reward model for rejection sampling before DPO. Reward models remain central for Best-of-N, rejection sampling, data filtering and online RL.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Post-training recipes. Published pipelines combine SFT, reward-model-based rejection sampling and offline preference optimisation (Llama 3: rejection sampling plus DPO; Tülu 3: SFT, DPO, then RL with verifiable rewards). DPO-family losses are the common default for preference tuning.
  • Verifiable rewards for reasoning. Reasoning models (DeepSeek-R1, Qwen3) are trained with GRPO-style online RL where the reward comes from answer checkers and unit tests rather than a learned RM; learned RMs are still used for open-ended qualities.
  • AI feedback. Preference labels from models guided by written principles (Constitutional AI, RLAIF) and LLM-as-judge scoring are used at scale alongside human labels.
  • Tooling. TRL provides RewardTrainer, DPOTrainer and GRPOTrainer (PPO now under trl.experimental); OpenRLHF and verl run large-scale PPO and GRPO.

Sources: Llama 3 paper, Tülu 3, DeepSeek-R1, Constitutional AI, Scaling laws for reward model overoptimization, TRL reward trainer, TRL PPO trainer.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • A reward model is fit to pairwise preferences with the Bradley–Terry loss −logσ(R+​−R−​); only score differences mean anything.
  • The KL-regularised optimum is πref​eR/β/Z: β sets how hard the policy chases the reward model.
  • Apply the KL penalty per sample, −βlog(π/πref​); a per-prompt constant is cancelled by the baseline.
  • Optimising a flawed reward model raises its score while true quality peaks and falls; watch KL and hold out human or gold evaluations.
  • Best-of-N uses a reward model with no training, at a KL cost of at most logN−(N−1)/N.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Almost the same

Almost the same: final KLs of 2.29, 2.27 and 1.83, all close to the maximum ln12=2.48, although β changes a hundredfold. The penalty kl(probs_new, probs_ref) is a property of the whole distribution for a prompt, so every sample from that prompt receives the same deduction. Policy gradients compare samples with each other, and the advantage normalisation subtracts the batch mean, so a deduction shared by all samples cancels; only small differences between prompts survive. To make β work, penalise each sample by its own log-ratio, reward = rm_score - beta * (log(probs_new[token]) - log(probs_ref[token])), whose average is the KL and which is largest for the replies the policy has inflated most. With that penalty the optimum is πref​eR/β/Z, which the second run computes exactly: KL 0.37 at β=1, 0.92 at β=0.1.

SolutionB1 · Smaller weights, same ranking with enough data

With 600 pairs, holdout accuracy falls from 100% to 87%; with 6,000 pairs it is back at 100%. The weights shrink a lot: the average absolute weight drops from about 1.1 to about 0.25–0.33. Both follow from the Bradley–Terry model. With a quarter of the labels flipped, a good pair is preferred only 75% of the time, so the best fit has σ(Δ)=0.75, a reward gap of ln3=1.10 per pair, or about 1.10/4=0.27 per word (each pair has two good and two bad words). The ranking is still right, so with enough data accuracy recovers, but the RM becomes less confident, and with little data the noise flips some rankings. Without noise the opposite happens: the data are perfectly separable, Bradley–Terry has no finite best fit, and the weights keep growing with more training (1.1 after 600 pairs, 1.7 after 6,000), an over-confident RM. At 45% flipped labels, 600 pairs give 49% accuracy (chance) and 6,000 pairs 90%.

Explain the mechanismYour rater budget is gone. A colleague proposes to replace the human labelers with a model that reads a short list of principles and ranks the pairs, and to run the same RLHF pipeline on those labels. Is this just a cheaper RLHF? What changes?

Level 2 of 4 · about 10 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. It is the constitutional recipe, and it changes the failure modes rather than removing them. Phase one has the model critique and revise its own answers against sampled principles and trains on the revisions; phase two trains a reward model on pairs ranked by a feedback model and runs the usual RL stage. Rater psychology stops being the bias, and the reading of the principle set becomes the bias, applied uniformly over the whole dataset. Sycophancy toward a rater drops, and the proxy problem stays.

The two phases.

  1. Self-critique and revision. Start from a helpful but not yet harmless SFT model. For a red-team prompt, the model writes an answer, a second pass reads one sampled principle and critiques the answer, and a third pass revises it. The revision is the SFT target. The original work used 16 principles and kept the set small so that critiques stay focused.
  2. RL from AI feedback. Generate pairs, let a feedback model rank them against sampled principles, train a reward model on those rankings, and run PPO against it. Everything after the label is the standard pipeline.

Why it is not only cheaper.

  • The bias moves. A human rater is inconsistent and pleased by agreement; a feedback model applies one reading of a principle to every pair, strict or loose, but uniform.
  • The signal is legible. You can read the principle, the critique and the revision for any example; human labels are opaque.
  • Goodhart stays. The proxy is now a model's reading of the principle set, still an imperfect measure, so over-optimization against it is still possible.
  • The labeler is the same class of model you train, so a bias in it can be amplified rather than averaged out.

What the original work claimed.

The trained model was more harmless and about as helpful as an RLHF model with comparable data. That result has held across labs.

A gate next to the training.

A parallel defense trains light classifiers that read the same principles and gate the outputs. The first version cost 23.7 percent extra compute; the later version about 1 percent. Training shapes behavior and classifiers enforce invariants; neither one is enough alone.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The labels are free now, so collect ten times more pairs." More pairs with the same uniform bias make the bias stronger, not weaker; the volume gain does not fix the proxy.
  • "An AI labeler removes human bias from alignment." It replaces rater bias with principle-interpretation bias; the principles and the labeler's reading of them are now the proxy.
  • "This is only a safety trick; it does not touch helpfulness." The original work reports about the same helpfulness as RLHF with comparable data, and the same critique loop can target any principle you write.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why does the recipe sample one principle per critique instead of giving the model the whole list? Expected: a focused critique is more specific and more useful as a training target; the list as a whole is covered over many samples.
  • (level 2) Which parts of the pipeline change and which stay? Expected: the label source and the SFT targets change; the reward model, the RL stage and the KL penalty stay.
  • (level 3) Name a sycophancy-like failure that still occurs with an AI labeler, and a check for it. Expected: the feedback model can prefer answers that quote the principles without following them; rate a held-out set with people and compare the two rankings.
  • (level 4) How do you argue for a priority order among principles? Expected: conflicts between principles need a resolution rule; a tiered order resolves them top-down, from catastrophic harm to platform rules to general ethics to helpfulness.

Where it is taught. The Constitutional AI lesson walks both phases and the shift in failure modes, and the reward modeling lesson places RLAIF among the sources of a preference signal.

Explain the mechanismA staff engineer argues that moving every alignment run from PPO to direct preference methods ends the reward-hacking problem, because there is no reward model left to hack. Make the counter-argument with the mechanism, and say what survives in your pipeline either way.

Level 4 of 4 · about 12 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Direct methods remove the explicit reward model, not the proxy. The quantity they optimize is the log ratio of policy to reference, an implicit reward that the preference data pins down only where the data has coverage. Under optimization the policy queries that ratio out of distribution, and the published curves of gold reward against KL show the same peak and then collapse as reward-model pipelines, with the KL regularization failing to stabilize it. What survives: a blind held-out eval, early stopping, broader preference data, and ensembles of signals.

The mechanism.

DPO rewrites the reward as beta times the log ratio of policy to reference. That function is fit from pairs, and it is constrained only on the pairs. Everywhere else its value is whatever the parameterization implies. As the policy moves to raise the margin, it samples from regions no pair covered, where the implicit reward is arbitrary. The study of scaling laws for over-optimization in direct alignment trained policies across KL budgets and found the same shape as reward-model over-optimization: the gold reward rises, peaks, and falls as the KL grows.

Where Goodhart bites now.

In the PPO stack the proxy is a trained reward model. In the direct stack the proxy is the ratio against the reference. The failures look different: the degraded chosen response, where the chosen answer loses probability while the margin grows; length exploitation, where a longer winner pads the gap; arbitrary rewards on pairs that are rare under the reference. They are one disease in a different organ.

Why the KL does not save it.

In PPO the KL term is explicit and tunable, and an adaptive controller holds it near a target. In DPO the anchor is structural: the sigmoid saturates as the ratio grows. The study's result is that this structural regularization does not stop the collapse. Beta changes the speed, not the shape.

What survives in any pipeline.

  • A blind, held-out human or trusted-judge eval on a fixed prompt set, read at every checkpoint. It is the one signal the policy cannot game.
  • Early stopping on that eval, not on the training margin.
  • Data: coverage of the regions the policy drifts into, collected from the current policy's samples.
  • Ensembles: several reward signals or several losses on one battery, because one exploit rarely fits all of them.
  • Logs of the absolute chosen log-probability and of the KL to the reference, so that the drift is visible before the eval falls.

The honest version of the claim.

Direct methods do remove a model to train and host, PPO's instability, and the class of exploits that live in reward model weights. They are the default for most frontier post-training runs for those reasons. They do not remove the need to guard against over-optimization, so the guard moves into the pipeline design.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "No reward model means nothing to hack." The implicit reward is a function the data pins down only on the pairs; the policy can push it anywhere else.
  • "The structural KL in DPO is enough regularization." The published curves show the same peak and collapse across KL budgets; beta changes the speed, not the shape.
  • "Use a smaller beta so the policy learns faster and you can stop earlier." A smaller beta lets the policy move further from the reference per unit of margin, which is the direction of the failure.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) What does DPO remove from the PPO stack, concretely? Expected: a reward model to train and host, the PPO loop with its clip and value head, and an explicit KL coefficient; it keeps the frozen reference.
  • (level 4) Which variant bounds the gap, and what does that buy against this failure? Expected: the identity-mapping loss, whose margin target is one over two beta; it stops a weak preference from producing an unbounded implicit reward, which removes one path to collapse but not the out-of-distribution query.
  • (level 4) How do you detect over-optimization without a gold reward? Expected: a held-out eval with generation, the absolute chosen log-probability, the KL to the reference, and a comparison across checkpoints; a margin that grows while the eval falls is the signature.
  • (level 4) Does iterating, with fresh pairs from the current policy each round, help? Expected: yes, it moves the data coverage to where the policy lives, which is what the reference-ratio proxy lacks; it also reintroduces an online loop that needs its own stop rule.

Where it is taught. The DPO family lesson states the universal over-optimization result and the fixes that transfer, and the reward modeling lesson lists the over-optimization guards that every pipeline keeps.

Explain the mechanismIn the PPO stage of RLHF, the objective subtracts a KL term between the policy and a frozen copy of the SFT model. A new hire asks why the pipeline keeps a second copy of the model in memory for that one term. Explain the failure it prevents and how you tune its coefficient.

Level 2 of 4 · about 10 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. The reward model was trained on answers that look like SFT output. Without the term, PPO moves the policy into regions the reward model never saw and finds strings that score high but that people dislike. The term charges the policy for leaving the SFT distribution, so it is a regularizer, not a hard constraint. Tune beta from the measured KL: too low and the reward climbs together with the KL, too high and the policy barely moves from SFT.

The objective.

maximize  E[ R(x, y) ]  -  beta * KL( policy(. | x) || reference(. | x) )

The reference is the SFT checkpoint, frozen. The reward model scores whole answers; the KL is summed over the tokens of each answer and subtracted from the reward.

Without the KL term.

The reward model is a finite fit to human preferences and has blind spots. Repeated phrases that signal helpfulness, verbose formal text with no content, bullet points everywhere: each scored high in real pipelines. With no anchor the policy collapses toward a few high-reward modes. The toy three-stage pipeline in the InstructGPT lesson shows this inside 50 update steps when beta is 0.

Reading the KL.

Track the mean KL per answer on every update. The lesson on reward modeling gives 5 to 10 nats, summed over tokens, as the point where the policy has drifted far from SFT: either beta is too low, or reward hacking has started.

Tuning beta.

Reference values: 0.01 to 0.05 in the lesson on reward modeling, 0.02 in the pipeline that the RLHF lesson describes. The common trick is an adaptive beta with a target KL. When the observed KL passes the target, beta doubles; when it falls under half the target, beta halves. The library the lesson shows uses a target of 6.0 with the adaptive controller on.

The second anchor.

The KL protects style and distribution. It does not protect benchmark skill that the reward never asks for. That drop is the alignment tax, and the fix mixes pretraining gradients into the RL objective.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Drop the reference model to save memory and clip the PPO ratio harder." The clip bounds one update; it does not bound the drift over many updates, which is what the KL does.
  • "Set beta high to be safe." A high beta pins the policy to SFT, and the run buys nothing for its compute.
  • "KL is a safety filter for harmful text." It measures distance from the SFT distribution and knows nothing about content; a safe reference is what makes it useful.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why is the term a regularizer and not a trust region? Expected: it adds a cost that grows with the distance; nothing stops the policy from paying it, so a large reward can still buy a large move.
  • (level 2) What two things must be true of the reference model in code? Expected: its weights never receive gradients, and it never shares parameters with the policy; a shared module silently removes the anchor.
  • (level 3) The reward rises, the KL rises, and human ratings stay flat. Which knob first? Expected: raise beta or stop early; then widen the reward model's training data so that the exploited region is covered.
  • (level 3) After RLHF the model scores lower on reading comprehension benchmarks. Is that the KL's job? Expected: no; that is the alignment tax, addressed by a pretraining log-likelihood term in the objective, as the PPO-ptx variant does.

Where it is taught. The reward modeling lesson explains the KL as the anti-reward-hacking knob, the RLHF lesson builds the penalized objective, and the alignment signal lesson covers the alignment tax.

Debug a failureFour days into a PPO run, the mean score from the reward model keeps climbing. A blind human rating of samples from each checkpoint is flat, and the last two checkpoints read slightly worse. The team wants to keep training because the curve looks great. What do you say?

Level 3 of 4 · about 12 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Stop, and treat it as reward hacking. The policy has found outputs that the reward model scores high but people do not prefer, because the reward model is a finite proxy with blind spots. Read the KL to the reference and the length and format of recent samples; a KL that climbs with the reward and answers that grow longer or more formulaic confirm it. Roll back to the checkpoint where the human rating peaked, raise the KL coefficient or stop earlier, and widen the reward model's training data to cover what the policy exploited.

The signature.

Reward up, human eval flat or down. The reward model was trained on in-distribution answers; the policy under optimization leaves that distribution and the reward model extrapolates. The RLHF lesson's table of what real pipelines saw:

Failure What the samples show Why the reward model liked it
Verbosity longer and longer answers raters preferred detail, so the model learned length
Sycophancy agreement with the user's premise agreeable answers were rated higher
Hedging no commitment to an answer hedged answers are rarely marked wrong
Format gaming headers and bullets everywhere formatted answers looked polished

The checks.

  1. The mean KL to the reference per answer. The warning line in the lesson on reward modeling is 5 to 10 nats summed over tokens; past it, the policy has drifted far from SFT.
  2. The response length by checkpoint. Length hacking is the most common form.
  3. Fifty samples read side by side across checkpoints. The failure modes above are visible to the eye.
  4. The blind human eval you already have. Keep it blind and held out; it is the one measure the policy cannot game.

The fixes.

  • Roll back to the best checkpoint by human rating; the reward curve is not the objective.
  • Raise beta, or use an adaptive beta with a target KL, so that the policy pays more to leave the reference.
  • Normalize the reward for length, or add a length-aware term.
  • Add the exploited answers, rated by people, to the reward model's data, retrain it, and continue from the rollback.
  • Train more than one reward model on different data; one exploit rarely fits all of them.

Why the curve is not the goal.

The reward model is a statistical proxy for a sample of human rankings, not for human values. Optimizing a proxy hard enough finds its errors. Every alignment pipeline since has carried a blind human eval for that reason.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The human raters are noisy; trust the reward model." The reward model was fit to those raters; when they disagree with it on new samples, the model is extrapolating wrong.
  • "Lower beta so the policy can find even better answers." A lower beta lets the policy move further from the distribution the reward model knows, which is the cause of the problem.
  • "Keep training; the human eval will catch up." The lessons describe a plateau or a decline in human ratings while the reward climbs; more steps make the exploit stronger.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) How do you tell reward hacking from a reward model that is right and raters who are wrong? Expected: read the samples; if people consistently dislike what scores high, the proxy is wrong, because the raters define the objective.
  • (level 3) The reward model is small to save compute. Does that matter here? Expected: yes; a reward model that cannot represent the policy's outputs has more blind spots, and the lessons warn against one smaller than the policy.
  • (level 4) You switch to DPO to get rid of the reward model. Does the problem go away? Expected: no; the implicit reward can be over-optimized the same way, with the same peak-and-collapse curve, so the blind eval and early stopping stay.
  • (level 4) Design a guard that stops a run on its own. Expected: a held-out human or trusted-judge eval on a fixed prompt set at each checkpoint, a KL budget, and a stop rule for an eval that falls while the reward rises.

Where it is taught. The reward modeling lesson names reward hacking and length hacking as the top pitfalls, the RLHF lesson tabulates the failure modes, and the alignment signal lesson explains why the reward model is a proxy for a sample of rankings.
