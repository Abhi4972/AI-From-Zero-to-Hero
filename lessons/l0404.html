Why this came about

Search engines needed a way to rank documents for a query long before neural networks could read. Through the 1970s and 1980s, Stephen Robertson, Karen Spärck Jones and colleagues worked out a probabilistic account of relevance, and in 1994 it produced the ranking function BM25 ("best match", attempt 25). It became the default in Lucene, Elasticsearch, OpenSearch and almost every search product. When RAG arrived, many teams used only embedding search and found that it missed exact terms such as order numbers, product codes and names. Keyword search came back, and today BM25 runs beside vector searchlater · 14.71 in most production retrieval systems.

In plain words first
Matching the exact words

Keyword search finds passages that contain the words of the question. BM25 decides the order with three sensible ideas. A word that appears in few passages (an order number, "OTP") says more about a match than a word that appears everywhere ("the", "order"). A passage that uses a query word several times is probably more about it, but the tenth mention adds far less than the first. And a short passage that matches is a better sign than a long one that happens to contain the word somewhere. It needs no model and no GPU, and it is excellent for exact terms, which meaning-based search often blurs.

Keyword search works on tokens, here simply lowercase words and numbers. (This is a different, simpler tokeniser than the subword tokenisers language models use, Lesson 5.31.) Split every chunk from Lesson 14.70:

Step 2 · Rare words carry more information

Count, for each word, how many chunks contain it (its document frequency). A word in nearly every chunk cannot tell chunks apart; a word in one chunk points straight at it. Inverse document frequency (IDF) turns that into a weight:

N = len(docs)
df = Counter(word for d in docs for word in set(d))           # document frequency: in how many chunks does each word appear?

def idf(word):
    return math.log((N - df[word] + 0.5) / (df[word] + 0.5) + 1)

print(f"{'word':<10}{'chunks containing it':>22}{'IDF':>8}")
for word in ["the", "refund", "order", "cod", "otp", "sms"]:
    print(f"{word:<10}{df[word]:>22}{idf(word):>8.2f}")
What you should see
word        chunks containing it     IDF
the                            6    0.43
refund                         1    1.90
order                          2    1.39
cod                            1    1.90
otp                            1    1.90
sms                            1    1.90

"the" appears in six of the nine chunks and gets a low weight. "cod", "otp" and "sms" each appear in one chunk and get the highest weight. Look at "refund": it is also in only one chunk, although several chunks talk about refunds. They say "refunds", "refundable" and "refunded", and to a keyword index those are four different words. Keep that in mind for Step 6.

Reading this output
Reading the IDF line

math.log((N - df + 0.5) / (df + 0.5) + 1): with N chunks in total and df of them containing the word, the fraction is roughly "chunks without the word, divided by chunks with it". The logarithm stops very rare words from dominating completely, and the + 0.5 and + 1 keep the result positive and stable for tiny collections. This is the variant used by Lucene and Elasticsearch.

Step 3 · Repetition counts, but less each time

If a chunk mentions "refund" five times, is it five times as relevant as a chunk that mentions it once? Plain TF-IDF (Lesson 5.32) says yes. BM25 says the first mention matters most, and applies saturation:

k1 = 1.5
print("times a word appears :", [1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 50])
print("credit it earns      :", [round(tf * (k1 + 1) / (tf + k1), 2) for tf in [1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 50]])
What you should see
times a word appears : [1, 2, 3, 5, 10, 50]
credit it earns      : [1.0, 1.43, 1.67, 1.92, 2.17, 2.43]

One mention earns 1.0. Ten mentions earn a little over twice that, not ten times. Fifty mentions earn barely more than ten. A chunk cannot win by repeating a word.

Step 4 · The whole formula

BM25 adds up, for every query word found in the chunk, its IDF times its saturated count. One more correction: a long chunk has more chances to contain any word, so the count is adjusted by the chunk's length relative to the average.

avg_len = sum(len(d) for d in docs) / N

def bm25_score(query, doc, k1=1.5, b=0.75):
    tf = Counter(doc)
    score = 0.0
    for word in tokenize(query):
        if word not in tf:
            continue
        saturation = tf[word] * (k1 + 1) / (tf[word] + k1 * (1 - b + b * len(doc) / avg_len))
        score += idf(word) * saturation
    return score

query = "refund for a COD order"
scores = [bm25_score(query, d) for d in docs]
for i in sorted(range(N), key=lambda i: -scores[i])[:3]:
    print(f"{scores[i]:5.2f}  {CHUNKS[i][0]:<16} {CHUNKS[i][2][:70]}...")
What you should see
 5.09  cod#0            Cash on delivery (COD). COD is available for orders up to INR 10,000 i...
 3.45  refund-policy#0  Refund policy. Customers can request a refund within 7 days of deliver...
 2.55  cancellation#0   Cancelling an order. You can cancel any order before it is dispatched,...

The COD chunk wins clearly: it contains "cod" (rare, and mentioned several times), "refunds" does not count as "refund", and "order" is fairly common. The refund policy chunk comes second on the strength of the single word "refund".

The same idea as a formula
  • score(query, chunk) = sum over the query's words w of IDF(w) × tf × (k1 + 1) / (tf + k1 × (1 − b + b × length / average length))
  • tf: how many times w appears in the chunk
  • k1 (usually 1.2 to 2.0): how slowly repetition saturates
  • b (usually 0.75): how strongly long chunks are penalised; b = 0 switches length normalisation off

Step 5 · The lab's keyword search

In practice you use a library or a search engine. The rest of this chapter's lab uses the small rank_bm25 package, wrapped in one function that returns chunk IDs:

# Step 3 · Keyword retrieval with BM25
from rank_bm25 import BM25Okapi

def tokenize(text: str) -> list[str]:
    return re.findall(r"[a-z0-9]+", text.lower())

bm25 = BM25Okapi([tokenize(c[2]) for c in CHUNKS])

def search_bm25(query: str, k: int = 3) -> list[str]:
    scores = bm25.get_scores(tokenize(query))
    ranked = sorted(range(len(CHUNKS)), key=lambda i: scores[i], reverse=True)
    return [CHUNKS[i][0] for i in ranked[:k]]
Reading this output
Scores differ between implementations

rank_bm25 uses a slightly different IDF formula from the one in Step 2 (the original 1994 form, with a floor for very common words), so its scores are not the same numbers as ours. The ranking is nearly always the same. This is normal: BM25 scores have no fixed scale, differ between engines, and change when documents are added. Never compare a BM25 score with a threshold you copied from somewhere else, and never add it to a score from another kind of search (Lesson 5.38).

Step 6 · What keyword search finds, and what it misses

Four test questions, chosen to show the edge of what keyword search can do. Two use the same words as the documents. Two mean the same thing as a document but share no words with it. For each, print the top results and the best score:

for q in ["refund for a COD order", "is there a fee for cash on delivery", "pay when it arrives", "stop my purchase"]:
    scores = bm25.get_scores(tokenize(q))
    print(f"{q!r:<40} -> {search_bm25(q)}   best score {max(scores):.2f}")
What you should see
'refund for a COD order'                 -> ['cod#0', 'refund-policy#0', 'cancellation#0']   best score 4.05
'is there a fee for cash on delivery'    -> ['cod#0', 'warranty#1', 'warranty#0']   best score 5.70
'pay when it arrives'                    -> ['shipping#1', 'cancellation#0', 'refund-policy#0']   best score 1.24
'stop my purchase'                       -> ['refund-policy#0', 'refund-policy#1', 'shipping#0']   best score 0.00
  • The first two questions share words with the right chunk ("cod"; "fee", "cash", "delivery") and it comes first.
  • "pay when it arrives" means cash on delivery, but shares no distinctive word with that chunk. BM25 returns a shipping chunk, on the strength of small words.
  • "stop my purchase" means cancel. No query word appears in any chunk in a way that scores, so the best score is 0.00 and the "top three" are simply the first three chunks. A score of zero means no match, and a system should treat it that way instead of passing those chunks to a model.

This is the vocabulary gap: keyword search cannot connect "money back" with "refund", "stop" with "cancel", or "pasword" with "password". It is the reason the next lesson exists.

Step 7 · Why it is fast: the inverted index

Scoring every chunk for every query would be slow for millions of chunks. Search engines build an inverted index once: for each word, the list of chunks that contain it. A query then touches only the lists for its own words.

index = {}                                                    # word -> the chunks that contain it
for i, d in enumerate(docs):
    for word in set(d):
        index.setdefault(word, []).append(i)

print("chunks containing 'otp':", [CHUNKS[i][0] for i in index["otp"]])
candidates = set().union(*(index.get(w, []) for w in tokenize("OTP by SMS")))
print("only these need scoring for 'OTP by SMS':", sorted(CHUNKS[i][0] for i in candidates), f"({len(candidates)} of {N})")
What you should see
chunks containing 'otp': ['account#0']
only these need scoring for 'OTP by SMS': ['account#0'] (1 of 9)

Only one chunk out of nine needs scoring for this query. With a million chunks and a query of rare words, an engine still scores only a few hundred. This structure, plus compression and caching, is how keyword search answers in milliseconds over billions of documents, and it is why keyword search is so cheap compared with running a model.

Keyword search in real systems

Piece What it does Why it matters for RAG
Analyzer The tokenising rules: lowercase, split, remove stop words, reduce words to a stem ("refunds", "refunded" → "refund") Stemming would have fixed the "refund" problem from Step 2. Analyzers are per language
Synonyms A reviewed list such as "COD = cash on delivery" Closes known vocabulary gaps cheaply

Filters Restrict by metadata (tenant, date, product) before scoring Permissions and freshness (Lesson 14.73)
Identifiers Keep codes such as SK-48213 or E-4102 as single tokens The default tokeniser may split them into pieces that match everywhere

Where BM25 lives: Elasticsearch and OpenSearch (the full-featured engines), Postgres full-text search and SQLite FTS5 (good enough for many applications), the built-in keyword or hybrid searchlater · 5.38 of vector databaseslater · 14.71 such as Weaviate, Qdrant, Milvus and Azure AI Search, and libraries such as Tantivy, bm25s and rank_bm25 for small or embedded use.

In production
BM25 in practice

Keyword search is the part teams most often drop and later regret: vector-only systems fail on order numbers, SKUs, error codes and people's names, which are a large share of real support and operations questions. Keep a keyword index from day one. Its weak spots are synonyms and spelling ("cancel" against "stop my order"), which is exactly what vectors cover, hence hybrid search in Lesson 5.38. For Hindi and Hinglish text, check the tokenizer: splitting on spaces works for Hindi in Devanagari, but transliterated and code-mixed text needs normalization or it will miss matches.

Go deeper (optional)
Learned sparse retrieval

Models such as SPLADE and Elastic's ELSER use a transformer to produce a sparse vector over the vocabulary: weights for the words in the text plus related words that were never written ("refund" also lights up "money", "return"). The result is searched with an ordinary inverted index, so it keeps keyword search's speed and exact matching while closing part of the vocabulary gap. It costs a model call at indexing and query time.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Keyword search is obsolete."
    ✓ It is still best for exact identifiers and rare terms, and it is part of most strong retrieval systems.
  • ✗ "BM25 understands synonyms."
    ✓ It matches words; "refund" does not match "money back", or even "refunds" without stemming.
  • ✗ "More matching words always means a better passage."
    ✓ Rare words weigh more, repetition saturates, and long passages are penalised.
  • ✗ "The top result is always relevant."
    ✓ Search always returns something. Check the score: zero, or far below the usual range, means nothing matched.

Exercises

Predict before you run

BM25 scores the query "SK-1042 refund" against two chunks: one containing "SK-1042" once, another containing "refund" five times. Which ranks higher, and why? (Solution P1.)

Break it on purpose

Search BM25 for "money back" when the corpus says "refund". What does it return, and what does that tell you about keyword search? (Solution B1.)

Debugging exercise

"SK-1042" returns nothing useful, although a chunk contains it. The tokenizer lowercases and splits on anything that is not a letter or digit. Find the bug. (Solution D1.)

Make it your own

Add a tiny stemmer to tokenize that removes a final "s", "ed" or "able" from words longer than five letters. Rebuild the index and print the document frequency of "refund" again. Which queries from Step 6 change? (Solution M1.)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Rare words weigh more

Usually the chunk with "SK-1042": BM25 weights terms by rarity (inverse document frequency), and an order ID is rare while "refund" appears in many chunks. Term frequency also saturates, so five mentions of "refund" are worth much less than five times one.

SolutionB1 · No shared words, no match

Nothing relevant, or something matched by accident through a small word such as "back". BM25 matches words, not meaning. That is the vocabulary gap that vector search covers (Lesson 14.71), and why hybrid search combines both (Lesson 5.38).

SolutionD1 · Tokenization destroys IDs

Splitting on the hyphen turns "SK-1042" into "sk" and "1042". "sk" then appears in every order number, and "1042" may be the only useful piece. Keep IDs, codes and numbers as single tokens, for example with a pattern that matches [a-z]+-\d+ before the general rule, apply the same rule to documents and queries, and test with real IDs.

SolutionM1 · A stemmer changes what matches

After stemming, "refunds", "refunded" and "refundable" all become "refund", so its document frequency rises from 1 to several and its IDF falls. Queries about refunds now reach every chunk that discusses them, which raises recall. The questions about paying on arrival and stopping a purchase do not change: stemming fixes word forms, not different words for the same idea. Real analyzers use tested stemmers (Porter, Snowball) or lemmatisers instead of rules like these.

Check your understanding

Explain the mechanismHow does BM25 work, and when does it beat vector search?

Worked answer. BM25 scores a document by the query terms it contains, weighting rare terms more (IDF), saturating repeated terms, and normalizing for length. It beats vector search on exact tokens: IDs, product codes, error messages, names and rare jargon, which embeddings blur. It needs no model and is fast.

A plausible wrong answer. "BM25 is obsolete since embeddings." Production systems still combine it with vectors for exactly these cases.

Follow-ups. What are k1 and b in BM25?

Debug a failureKeyword search works for English and fails for Hindi queries. What do you check?

Worked answer. The tokenizer and the text: whether Devanagari is tokenized correctly, whether queries and documents use the same script (Hinglish in Latin letters against Hindi in Devanagari), and stemming or stop-word rules built for English. Fix tokenization per language, or rely on multilingual embeddings for those queries.

A plausible wrong answer. "Hindi does not work with BM25." It works with a suitable tokenizer.

Follow-ups. How would you evaluate multilingual retrieval?

Defend a designElasticsearch or OpenSearch for keyword search, or a vector database's built-in keyword search?

Worked answer. A search engine when keyword search must be excellent: analyzers per language, synonyms, filters and operations at scale. A vector database's built-in keyword or hybrid search when it is good enough on your eval and one system is simpler to run. Measure both on your questions.

A plausible wrong answer. "Whatever the vector database offers." It may lack analyzers your languages need.

Recap
  • BM25 ranks chunks by the query words they contain: rare words weigh more (IDF), repetition saturates (k1), long chunks are penalised (b).
  • It needs no model, runs in milliseconds through an inverted index, and is the best tool for exact terms, codes and names.
  • It cannot bridge different words for the same meaning; a zero or very low score means nothing matched.
  • Analyzers (stemming, stop words, synonyms, keeping identifiers whole) decide much of its quality.
  • Keep keyword search in every retrieval system; Lesson 5.38 combines it with vector search.
