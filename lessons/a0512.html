In plain words first
The idea in one line

Extractive systems tell you what the document said. Abstractive systems tell you what the author meant. Different tasks, different pitfalls.

The problem

In plain words first

Summarization turns a long text into a short one. There are two ways to do it. Extractive summarization picks the most important sentences of the original and copies them out unchanged. Abstractive summarization writes new sentences, as a person would. Extractive output can never state something the source did not say, but it can be choppy and miss points spread across the text. Abstractive output reads well, but it can invent facts. This lesson builds TextRank, the classic extractive method, which scores sentences by how much they have in common with all the others, the same idea Google's PageRank used to rank web pages. It then compares TextRank with the simple "first three sentences" baseline and a small pretrained abstractive model on real news articles, and scores all three with ROUGE. Finally it shows the uncomfortable fact behind every summarization metric: a summary with a wrong number can score almost as well as a correct one.

A 2,000-word news article lands in your feed. You need 120 words that capture it. You can either pick the three most important sentences from the article (extractive) or rewrite the content in your own words (abstractive). Both are called summarization. They are completely different problems.

Extractive summarization is a ranking problem. Score every sentence, return the top-k. The output is always grammatical because it is lifted verbatim. The risk is missing content that is distributed across the article.

Abstractive summarization is a generation problem. A transformer produces new text conditioned on the input. The output is fluent and compressive but may hallucinate facts that were not in the source. The risk is confident fabrication.

This lesson builds both, with the failure mode each one owns.

The idea

Extractive. Treat the article as a graph where nodes are sentences and edges are similarities. Run PageRank (or something like it) over the graph to score sentences by how connected they are to everything else. Highest-scoring sentences are the summary. The canonical implementation is TextRank (Mihalcea and Tarau, 2004).

Abstractive. Fine-tune a transformer encoder-decoder (BART, T5, Pegasus) on document-summary pairs. At inference, the model reads the document and generates the summary token-by-token via cross-attention. Pegasus in particular is pretrained with gap-sentence generation: whole important sentences are removed from a document and the model must write them back, a task very close to summarization, so it needs less fine-tuning data.

Evaluation with ROUGE (Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation). ROUGE-1 and ROUGE-2 score unigram and bigram overlap. ROUGE-L scores longest common subsequence. Higher is better, but like BLEU the numbers only compare systems on the same dataset: on CNN/DailyMail news, strong systems reach ROUGE-1 in the low 40s, and even human-written alternative summaries do not score near 100. Every paper reports all three. Use the rouge-score package.

The same idea as a formula

TextRank builds a weighted graph with one node per sentence. Mihalcea and Tarau's similarity counts shared words, normalised by sentence length:

wij​=log∣Si​∣+log∣Sj​∣∣{w:w∈Si​ and w∈Sj​}∣​

and the score of sentence j is defined by the scores of the sentences that point to it, each sharing its score in proportion to edge weight:

WS(Sj​)=(1−d)+di=j∑​∑k​wik​wij​​WS(Si​)

This is PageRank. Read as a random walk: a reader jumps from sentence to sentence following shared words, and with probability 1−d (0.15) jumps to a random sentence instead. The scores are the long-run share of time spent at each sentence, the stationary distribution of that walk, and repeating the update (power iteration) converges to it because the damping makes the walk mix. Sentences that share vocabulary with many central sentences are central themselves.

ROUGE-N is recall of reference n-grams:

ROUGE-Nrecall​=∑g∈n-grams(ref)​countref​(g)∑g∈n-grams(ref)​min(countref​(g),countsum​(g))​

with precision defined the same way over the summary's n-grams, and F1​ their harmonic mean (what rouge-score reports as fmeasure). ROUGE-L uses the longest common subsequence (LCS), words in the same order but not necessarily adjacent: recall =LCS/∣ref∣, precision =LCS/∣sum∣.

Abstractive training is Lesson 5.9's objective on (document, summary) pairs: minimise −∑t​logP(yt​∣y<t​,document).

Worked example
ROUGE and one TextRank step by hand

ROUGE. Reference "police arrested two men on friday", summary "two men were arrested by police". Reference unigrams: 6, summary unigrams: 6. Shared: police, arrested, two, men, so 4. ROUGE-1 recall =4/6=0.667, precision =4/6=0.667, F1​=0.667. Bigrams: the reference has "police arrested", "arrested two", "two men", "men on", "on friday"; the summary shares only "two men", so ROUGE-2 F1​=1/5=0.2. "police" comes first in the reference but last in the summary, and "arrested" comes before "two men" in one and after it in the other, so the longest in-order match, the LCS, is "two men" (2 words): ROUGE-L F1​=2/6=0.333. Same facts, reordered: unigram credit survives, order-sensitive credit collapses.

One TextRank step. Three sentences with symmetric weights wAB​=2, wAC​=1, wBC​=0, all scores starting at 1, d=0.85. A sends its score out in the ratio 2:1 to B and C; B and C each send everything to A. New scores: WS(A)=0.15+0.85(1+1)=1.85; WS(B)=0.15+0.85⋅32​=0.717; WS(C)=0.15+0.85⋅31​=0.433. A, the sentence linked to both others, already leads after one step.

Step 1 · TextRank (extractive)

import math
import re
from collections import Counter


def sentence_split(text):
    return re.split(r"(?<=[.!?])\s+", text.strip())


def similarity(s1, s2):
    w1 = Counter(s1.lower().split())
    w2 = Counter(s2.lower().split())
    intersection = sum((w1 & w2).values())
    denom = math.log(len(w1) + 1) + math.log(len(w2) + 1)
    if denom == 0:
        return 0.0
    return intersection / denom


def textrank(text, top_k=3, damping=0.85, iterations=50, epsilon=1e-4):
    sentences = sentence_split(text)
    n = len(sentences)
    if n <= top_k:
        return sentences

    sim = [[0.0] * n for _ in range(n)]
    for i in range(n):
        for j in range(n):
            if i != j:
                sim[i][j] = similarity(sentences[i], sentences[j])

    scores = [1.0] * n
    for _ in range(iterations):
        new_scores = [1 - damping] * n
        for i in range(n):
            total_out = sum(sim[i]) or 1e-9
            for j in range(n):
                if sim[i][j] > 0:
                    new_scores[j] += damping * sim[i][j] / total_out * scores[i]
        if max(abs(s - ns) for s, ns in zip(scores, new_scores)) < epsilon:
            scores = new_scores
            break
        scores = new_scores

    ranked = sorted(range(n), key=lambda k: scores[k], reverse=True)[:top_k]
    ranked.sort()
    return [sentences[i] for i in ranked]

Two things worth naming. The similarity function uses log-normalized word overlap, which is the original TextRank variant. Cosine of TF-IDF vectors works too. The damping factor 0.85 and iteration count are the PageRank defaults.

Step 3 · ROUGE evaluation

from rouge_score import rouge_scorer

scorer = rouge_scorer.RougeScorer(["rouge1", "rouge2", "rougeL"], use_stemmer=True)
scores = scorer.score(reference_summary, generated_summary)
print({k: round(v.fmeasure, 3) for k, v in scores.items()})

Always use stemming. Without it, "running" and "run" count as different words and ROUGE undercounts.

Build · Beyond ROUGE (2026 summarization eval)

ROUGE has been the dominant summarization metric for twenty years and it is insufficient on its own in 2026. The current landscape of alternatives:

  • BERTScore (contextual embedding similarity) gained ground through 2023 and is now reported alongside ROUGE in most summarization papers.
  • BARTScore treats evaluation as generation: score the summary by how likely a pretrained BART assigns it given the source.
  • MoverScore (Earth Mover's Distance over contextual embeddings) matches summary and reference words by meaning rather than exact form, so paraphrases are not punished the way ROUGE punishes them.
  • FactCC and QA-based faithfulness were common 2021-2023, now often replaced by G-Eval (a GPT-4 prompt chain that scores coherence, consistency, fluency, relevance with chain-of-thought reasoning).
  • G-Eval and similar LLM-judge approaches correlate with human ratings much better than ROUGE or BERTScore: on the SummEval benchmark, G-Eval with GPT-4 reached a Spearman correlation of about 0.51 with human scores (Liu et al., 2023), still far from perfect agreement.

Production recommendation: report ROUGE-L for legacy comparison, BERTScore for semantic overlap, G-Eval for coherence and factuality. Calibrate against 50-100 human-labeled summaries.

Step 4 · the factuality problem

Abstractive summaries are prone to hallucination. Extractive summaries carry a much lower hallucination risk because the output is lifted verbatim from the source, though they can still mislead if source sentences are decontextualized, outdated, or quoted out of order. This is the single biggest reason production systems still prefer extractive methods for compliance-adjacent content.

Hallucination types to name:

  • Entity swap. Source says "John Smith." Summary says "John Brown."
  • Number drift. Source says "25,000." Summary says "25 million."
  • Polarity flip. Source says "rejected the offer." Summary says "accepted the offer."
  • Fact invention. Source does not mention the CEO. Summary says the CEO approved.

Evaluation approaches that work:

  • FactCC. A binary classifier trained on entailment between source sentence and summary sentence. Predicts factual/not-factual.
  • QA-based factuality. Ask a QA model questions whose answers are in the source. If the summary supports different answers, flag.
  • Entity-level F1. Compare named entities in source vs summary. Entities present only in the summary are suspect.

For anything user-facing where factuality matters (news, medical, legal, financial), extractive is the safer default. Abstractive needs a factuality check in the loop.

    x = t5_tok("summarize: " + text, return_tensors="pt", truncation=True, max_length=512)
    with torch.no_grad():
        ids = t5.generate(**x, num_beams=4, max_new_tokens=80, no_repeat_ngram_size=3)
    return t5_tok.decode(ids[0], skip_special_tokens=True)

Lead-3 is the baseline every news summarizer must beat, and it is strong: journalists write the most important facts first (the "inverted pyramid"), and the human highlights largely restate the opening paragraph. Here the small abstractive t5-small edges past it by two or three points: it was trained on this very genre, and its short, compressed output shares the highlights' key phrases without carrying lead-3's extra words. Note also what t5-small does well and badly: its summary is fluent and faithful here, but it lowercases the first words, and it misses the highlights' point about Israel and the United States opposing the move. On 20 articles, differences of a point or two are partly noise; the literature uses the full 11,490-article test set.

The chosen sentences are the hubs, with the most and thickest links. Here they are three long quotations: a long sentence shares some words with almost every other sentence, and the log-length normalisation in similarity only partly offsets that. Short sentences sit at the edge with few, thin links whatever they say. Reading this output

Every damaged version keeps a ROUGE score around 90: one changed number, name or verb is a few percent of the words. To ROUGE they are nearly perfect summaries; to a reader they are false. The entity check catches the changed amount and the invented name, because neither appears in the source, but it is blind to "rejected", which reverses the meaning without adding any new entity. That is why serious summarization evaluation layers a faithfulness check (entailment-based, question-answering based, or an LLM judge) on top of overlap metrics, and why extractive methods stay the default where a false statement is unacceptable.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Summaries are a built-in product feature. Meeting, email and document summaries ship in Microsoft 365 Copilot, Google Workspace with Gemini and Zoom, all generated zero-shot by LLMs; reference-based ROUGE is least reliable exactly for these systems, so teams evaluate with human ratings and LLM judges.
  • Grounding checks as a guardrail. Managed checks such as Amazon Bedrock Guardrails' contextual grounding check and Azure AI Content Safety's groundedness detection, or Vectara's open HHEM model, flag summary sentences not supported by the source.
  • Extractive-then-abstractive. For long or regulated documents, a common pattern is to extract the relevant sentences first, then let a model rewrite only those, so every claim can be traced to a source sentence.

Sources: Bedrock contextual grounding check, Azure groundedness detection, Vectara HHEM, G-Eval.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Extractive = rank and copy sentences (safe, choppy); abstractive = generate new text (fluent, can invent facts).
  • TextRank is PageRank on a sentence-similarity graph: the stationary distribution of a random walk with damping.
  • ROUGE-N is n-gram recall/precision against a reference; ROUGE-L uses the longest common subsequence.
  • Always compare with the lead-3 baseline; on news it is hard to beat.
  • Overlap metrics cannot see factual errors: add entity, entailment or LLM-judge faithfulness checks.

Exercises

  1. Easy. Run TextRank on 5 news articles. Compare the top-3 sentences to a reference summary. Measure ROUGE-L. You should see 30-45 ROUGE-L on CNN/DailyMail-style articles.
  2. Medium. Implement entity-level factuality: extract named entities from source and summary (spaCy), compute recall of source entities in summary and precision of summary entities against source. High precision and low recall mean safe but terse; low precision means hallucinated entities.
  3. Hard. Compare BART-large-CNN against an LLM (Claude or GPT-4) on 50 CNN/DailyMail articles. Report ROUGE-L, factuality (by entity F1), and cost per summary. Document where each wins.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · t5-small, with lead-3 close behind

In the run, t5-small scores highest on all three ROUGE variants, lead-3 is a few points behind, and TextRank is far behind both. The surprise for most people is how strong lead-3 is: news puts the key facts first, and CNN/DailyMail's highlights restate the opening, so copying the first three sentences was a famously hard baseline in the early abstractive-summarization papers. A model needs to be trained on the genre, as t5-small was, to beat it. TextRank optimises centrality, not position, and on this data its preference for long, word-rich sentences picks quotations.

SolutionB1 · Every sentence scores the same

With d=0, the update is WS(Sj​)=1−0=1 for every sentence, whatever the graph: the random walk always teleports and never follows an edge. All scores tie at 1.0, sorted keeps the original order for ties, and the "summary" is simply the first top_k sentences, lead-3 by accident. The damping factor is the only thing that lets the graph structure influence the scores; at d=1 the walk never teleports, which can trap all the score in a disconnected cluster, which is why PageRank uses a value in between.
