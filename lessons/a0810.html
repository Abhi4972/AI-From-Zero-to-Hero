In plain words first
The idea in one line

An image is a 2-D tensor. A video is a 3-D one. The theory is the same; the compute is 10-100x harder. OpenAI's Sora (February 2024) showed it was possible. Hosted systems such as Google's Veo 3.1, Kuaishou's Kling and ByteDance's Seedance now make short clips with sound, and open-weight models (Wan, HunyuanVideo, LTX-2) run on a single GPU.

The problem

In plain words first

A video is a stack of images that must agree with each other: the same objects, lighting and identities, moving smoothly. Generating each frame separately with an image model gives flicker, because nothing ties one frame's choices to the next. Video diffusion models denoise the whole clip at once: a video VAE compresses it in space and time into a small block of numbers, the block is cut into patches, and a transformer lets every patch attend to patches in other frames, so the frames are decided together. The cost is enormous, which is why video models use heavy compression, why their attention is the bottleneck, and why most of them run in data centres. This lesson measures flicker exactly on the lesson's one-dimensional clips, counts tokens and attention for a real video latent, and runs the real Wan 2.1 video VAE on a short clip. Nothing is trained.

A 10-second 1080p video at 24fps is 240 frames of 1920×1080×3 pixels. That's ~1.5 GB of raw data per clip. Pixel-space diffusion is infeasible. You need:

  1. Spatiotemporal compression. A VAE that encodes videos, not frames, into a sequence of spatial-temporal patches.
  2. Temporal coherence. Frames need to share content, lighting, and object identity over seconds. The net has to model motion.
  3. Compute budget. Video training is 10-100x more expensive than image for the same model size.
  4. Conditioning. Text, image (first-frame), audio, or another video. Most production models accept all four.

The architecture that solved this is the Diffusion Transformer (DiT) applied to spatiotemporal patches, trained on huge (prompt, caption, video) datasets. Same diffusion loss as Lesson 06.

The idea

Idea 1 · Patchify

Encode the video with a 3D VAE (learned spatiotemporal compression). The latent is shape [T_latent, H_latent, W_latent, C_latent]. Split into patches of size [t_p, h_p, w_p]. For Sora-style models, t_p = 1 (per-frame patches) or t_p = 2 (every two frames). A 10-second 1080p video compresses to ~20,000-100,000 patches.

Idea 2 · Spatiotemporal DiT

A transformer processes the flat sequence of patches. Each patch has a 3D positional embedding (time + y + x). Attention is usually factorized:

  • Spatial attention within each frame's patches.
  • Temporal attention across frames at the same spatial location.
  • Full 3D attention over all patches at once costs far more (21 times more score pairs for a 5-second clip in the run after Step 4), yet it is what current open models (CogVideoX, HunyuanVideo, Wan) use, made affordable by strong VAE compression, FlashAttention and splitting the sequence across GPUs. Factorised attention was typical of earlier models (Video LDM, AnimateDiff).

Idea 3 · Text conditioning

Cross-attention or joint attention with a large text encoder (T5-XXL in CogVideoX, umT5 in Wan 2.1, a multimodal LLM in HunyuanVideo). Long prompts matter: Sora's technical report describes re-captioning the training videos with a captioning model to get detailed descriptions, as DALL·E 3 did for images.

Idea 4 · Training

Standard diffusion loss (ε or v prediction) over spatiotemporal latents. Data: web video + ~100M curated clips + synthetic text captions. Compute: tens of thousands of GPU hours even for a modest open model; frontier-scale budgets are undisclosed and far larger.

The same idea as a formula

A clip is one sample. With frames x(1),…,x(F), a video model learns the joint distribution p(x(1),…,x(F)). Running an image model on each frame samples ∏f​p(x(f)) instead: every frame is plausible, but frames are independent. For Gaussian data with covariance Σ, the frame-to-frame change has variance

Var(x(f+1)−x(f))=Σff​+Σf+1,f+1​−2Σf,f+1​

and independent sampling drops the −2Σf,f+1​ term, which is what makes flicker.

The exact denoiser for Gaussian data N(0,Σ): ε∗(xt​)=1−αˉt​​(αˉt​Σ+(1−αˉt​)I)−1xt​. The off-diagonal entries of Σ are what couple the frames; in a transformer, attention between patches of different frames plays that role.

Causal video VAE. F frames become 1+(F−1)/4 latent frames (the first frame alone, so a single image is a valid one-frame video), each downsampled 8× in height and width, with C channels.

Tokens and attention. With patches pt​×ph​×pw​ the sequence has N=pt​T′​⋅ph​H′​⋅pw​W′​ tokens. Full attention scores N2 pairs per head per layer; factorised attention scores T′S2+ST′2 with S tokens per latent frame.

Worked example
A 5-second clip, by hand

Wan 2.1 at 480×832, 81 frames (5 seconds at 16 frames per second):

  • Latent frames: 1+80/4=21; spatial size 60×104; channels 16.
  • Tokens with 1×2×2 patches: 21×30×52=32,760.
  • Full attention: 32,7602≈1.07×109 score pairs per head per layer.
  • Factorised: 21×1,5602+1,560×212≈5.2×107, 21 times fewer.

Flicker by hand. The lesson's clips are xt​=b+st+et​ with b∼N(0,1), s∼N(0,0.32), et​∼N(0,0.052). Consecutive frames differ by s+et+1​−et​, variance 0.09+0.005=0.095, so the mean absolute change is 2/π​×0.095​≈0.25. Sampled independently, frames 2 and 3 differ with variance Σ22​+Σ33​=(1+0.36)+(1+0.81)≈3.2: mean absolute change about 1.4.

Step 1 · patchify a synthetic 1-D "video"

code/main.py simulates the core spatiotemporal DiT idea: patchify a small synthetic video, add a per-patch position embedding, and denoise the whole sequence with a transformer-style attention over patches. No numpy; pure Python. We show that temporal coherence emerges even in 1-D when adjacent-frame patches share a denoiser and position embeddings.

def make_video(T_frames=8, rng=None):
    # a "video" is a sequence of 1-D values following a smooth trajectory
    base = rng.gauss(0, 1)
    return [base + 0.3 * t + rng.gauss(0, 0.1) for t in range(T_frames)]

Step 2 · position embedding per frame

def pos_embed(t, dim):
    return sinusoidal(t, dim)

Step 3 · denoiser sees the whole sequence

Instead of denoising each frame independently, our tiny net concatenates all frame values + their position embeddings and predicts the noise for all frames jointly.

Step 4 · temporal coherence test

After training, sample a video. Measure the frame-to-frame delta. If the model has learned temporal structure, the deltas stay smaller than sampling each frame independently.

Flicker, measured exactly

main.py's make_video clips (6 frames: a random start, a random slope, a little noise) and its schedule (its main() trains a network, which this page does not run). The clips are Gaussian, so the best possible denoiser has a formula. The run samples 4,000 clips with it jointly, and frame by frame with a denoiser that knows each frame's distribution but not how frames relate. Then it counts tokens and attention for a real video latent.

import math, random, runpy, numpy as np
m = runpy.run_path("main.py")          # the lesson's clip generator and schedule; main() is not run, because it trains
F = m["T_FRAMES"]
rng = random.Random(0)
clips = np.array([m["make_video"](rng) for _ in range(20_000)])
cov = np.cov(clips.T)                    # the data are Gaussian: base + slope * t + small noise
print(f"{F}-frame clips; correlation of frame 0 with frames 1, {F // 2}, {F - 1}: "
      f"{np.corrcoef(clips.T)[0, 1]:.3f}, {np.corrcoef(clips.T)[0, F // 2]:.3f}, {np.corrcoef(clips.T)[0, F - 1]:.3f}")

alphas, abar = m["make_schedule"](1000)
def sample(cov_used, n=4000, seed=1):
    """DDPM reverse chain with the exact noise predictor for N(0, cov_used):
    eps*(x_t) = sqrt(1 - ab) (ab*cov + (1 - ab) I)^-1 x_t."""
    g = np.random.default_rng(seed); x = g.normal(size=(n, F)); I = np.eye(F)
    for t in range(999, -1, -1):
        ab = abar[t]
        eps = math.sqrt(1 - ab) * x @ np.linalg.inv(ab * cov_used + (1 - ab) * I).T
        b = 1 - alphas[t]
        x = (x - b / math.sqrt(1 - ab) * eps) / math.sqrt(alphas[t])
        if t > 0: x = x + math.sqrt(b) * g.normal(size=x.shape)
    return x

joint = sample(cov)                                   # one model for the whole clip (frames see each other)
per_frame = sample(np.diag(np.diag(cov)))             # an "image model" per frame: right marginals, no coupling
delta = lambda v: np.abs(np.diff(v, axis=1)).mean()
print(f"mean |frame-to-frame change|: real clips {delta(clips):.3f} | joint sampling {delta(joint):.3f} | "
      f"independent frames {delta(per_frame):.3f}")
print(f"per-frame spread (should match the data): real {clips.std(0).mean():.3f}, joint {joint.std(0).mean():.3f}, "
      f"independent {per_frame.std(0).mean():.3f}")
print("one joint sample:      ", " ".join(f"{v:+.2f}" for v in joint[0]))
print("one independent sample:", " ".join(f"{v:+.2f}" for v in per_frame[0]))

# The same coupling at real scale: tokens in a Wan 2.1-style latent for 81 frames at 480 x 832.
lat_t, lat_h, lat_w = 1 + 80 // 4, 480 // 8, 832 // 8                # causal VAE: 4x in time, 8x in space
tokens_per_frame = (lat_h // 2) * (lat_w // 2)                          # 1 x 2 x 2 patches
n = lat_t * tokens_per_frame
print(f"\nlatent {lat_t} x {lat_h} x {lat_w} -> {n:,} tokens ({tokens_per_frame:,} per latent frame)")
full = n * n; spatial = lat_t * tokens_per_frame ** 2; temporal = tokens_per_frame * lat_t ** 2
print(f"attention score pairs per layer: full 3D {full:.2e} | spatial-only {spatial:.2e} + temporal-only {temporal:.2e} "
      f"= {(spatial + temporal):.2e} ({full / (spatial + temporal):.0f}x fewer)")

Reading this output

Frames of the same clip are strongly related: frame 0 correlates 0.96 with frame 1 and still 0.55 with frame 5. Sampling jointly with the exact denoiser reproduces the clips: mean frame-to-frame change 0.247 against the real 0.246, and the sample shown moves smoothly. Sampling each frame with a denoiser that knows its own distribution but not its neighbours gets every frame's spread exactly right (1.317) and the motion completely wrong: an average jump of 1.46 between frames, six times the real change, and a sample that leaps between +0.12 and −3.48. That is flicker, from the formula box's missing −2Σf,f+1​ term. At real scale the coupling is expensive: a 5-second 480p clip in a Wan-style latent is 32,760 tokens, and full 3D attention scores 1.07×109 pairs per head and layer, 21 times more than attending within frames and along time separately.

Pitfalls

  • Independent per-frame sampling = flicker. If you run image diffusion on each frame separately, the output flickers because each frame's noise is independent. Video diffusion fixes this by coupling the frames through attention or shared noise.
  • Naive 3D attention = OOM. Full 3D attention over a 10-second 1080p latent means half a million tokens attending to each other in every layer. Production models keep full attention but shrink the sequence (stronger VAE compression, larger patches) and split it across GPUs; factorised or windowed attention is the cheaper alternative.
  • Captions matter. Sora's technical report credits re-captioning (a captioning model writing detailed descriptions of every training video, as DALL·E 3 did for images) for better prompt following; later open models (CogVideoX, HunyuanVideo, Wan) re-caption their data too.
  • First-frame conditioning. Most production models also accept an image as the first frame. This is "image-to-video" mode; training includes this variant.
  • Physics drift. Long clips (>10s) accumulate subtle inconsistencies. Sliding-window generation + keyframe anchoring helps.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • A video model learns the joint distribution of a clip; per-frame sampling loses the coupling and flickers.
  • Causal video VAEs compress 4× in time and 8× in space: F frames become 1+(F−1)/4 latent frames.
  • Tokens = latent frames × patches per frame; full attention scales with their square.
  • Current open models use full 3D attention, made affordable by compression and sequence parallelism.
  • Cost is dominated by attention over hundreds of thousands of tokens per step.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · The per-frame image latent, but it is the wrong question

The per-frame image latent has fewer numbers: 29,952 against Wan's 36,864. Wan compresses time 4× but stores 16 channels per position instead of 4, which more than cancels the temporal saving. That comparison misses the point, though. What matters to the diffusion transformer is the number of tokens: Wan's latent has 4 latent frames instead of 13, so with 2×2 patches it has 576 tokens against 1,872, and attention, which scales with the square of the token count, is about ten times cheaper. The extra channels let each token carry more detail, so reconstruction stays sharp.

SolutionB1 · Motion dictated by the noise

With shared noise, every frame follows the same noise path, so the frames are almost perfectly correlated: frame 0 and frame 5 correlate at 0.94, against 0.55 in real clips, and the average frame-to-frame change is 0.15, smaller than the real 0.25. The clip no longer moves the way the data moves; its motion comes from how the frames' spreads differ, so most clips drift steadily away from zero in the direction they started (83% of them), whatever slope real clips would have. In images, shared noise gives a nearly frozen picture with textures that "swim" or stick to the screen. Consistency must come from a model that knows how frames relate, not from forcing them to share randomness.

Production note: video latents are a memory-bandwidth problem

A 10-second 1080p clip at 24 fps is 240 frames × 1920 × 1080 × 3 ≈ 1.5 GB of raw pixels. A Wan-style causal VAE (4× in time, 8× in each spatial direction, 16 channels) turns that into about 61 × 135 × 240 × 16 ≈ 32M numbers, ~63 MB in bf16, about 39× smaller (the run under "Use it" measures the same ratio). With 2 × 2 patches that is about 490,000 tokens. Attention over them dominates every step, so long, high-resolution video is limited by compute and by the memory needed for activations, more than by the bandwidth for reading the weights.

Three production knobs, all straight from production-inference literature inference chapter:

  • Split the sequence across GPUs. Video DiTs are parallelised over the token sequence (sequence or context parallelism, such as the xDiT/Ulysses support in Wan's and HunyuanVideo's inference code), because one clip's activations outweigh the model's weights. Latency per step drops with the number of GPUs until communication dominates.
  • Frame batching = continuous batching. At generation time, video is conceptually a batch of frames linked by attention. Continuous batching (in-flight scheduling) applies: start rendering frame t+1 while frame t-1 is being returned, if the model architecture allows sliding-window generation.
  • Clip-level prefill cache. For image-to-video, the first-frame conditioning is analogous to an LLM's prompt prefill: compute it once, reuse across the temporal decoder passes. This is effectively a KV-cache for video.
