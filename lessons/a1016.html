In plain words first
The idea in one line

Softmax attention spreads a small amount of probability over every non-matching token. Over 100k tokens that noise adds up and drowns the signal. Differential Transformer (Ye et al., ICLR 2025) fixes it by computing attention as the difference of two softmaxes, subtracting the shared noise floor. DIFF V2 (Microsoft, January 2026) is the production-stack rewrite: matching decode latency to baseline Transformer, no custom kernels, FlashAttention-compatible. The problem

In plain words first

Softmax attention can never give a token exactly zero weight, so every irrelevant token in a long context gets a little attention, and over a hundred thousand tokens those little amounts add up. Differential attention computes two attention maps and subtracts one from the other, like noise-cancelling headphones: whatever both maps agree on (the background) cancels, while what only the first map sees (the signal) survives. Version 2 (January 2026) makes the idea cheap: the same speed and memory at decode as a normal Transformer and no special kernels. It is still a research architecture; no widely used open model ships it yet. This lesson runs the lesson's toy, computes exactly when subtraction helps and when it hurts, and measures how a real model's attention heads actually behave on a long context with one fact hidden in it. No model is trained.

Standard softmax attention has a mathematical property that turns into an operational headache at scale. For a query q, the attention weights are softmax(qK^T / sqrt(d)). Softmax can never produce exact zeros: every non-matching token gets some positive mass. That residual mass is noise, and it scales with context length. At 128k tokens, even if each non-matching token gets only 0.0001% of the probability, 127,999 of them combined contribute about 13% of the total. The model has to learn to route around a noise floor that grows with context.

Empirically this shows up as attention-head interference: hallucinated citations in long-context RAG, lost-in-the-middle failures on 100k-token retrieval tasks, and subtle accuracy degradation on needle-in-haystack benchmarks past 32k. The Differential Transformer paper (arXiv:2410.05258, ICLR 2025) measured the gap: DIFF Transformers hit lower perplexity, higher long-context accuracy, and fewer hallucinations than same-size baselines.

DIFF V1 had three problems that kept it out of frontier pre-training pipelines. Its value cache had to be loaded twice per decode step, it required custom CUDA kernels that broke FlashAttention compatibility, and its per-head RMSNorm had to scale outputs up by about √n (about 90× at 8K tokens), which made training unstable at high learning rates. DIFF V2 (Microsoft unilm blog, January 20, 2026) fixed all three. This lesson walks both versions, builds the difference operator, and benchmarks noise cancellation on a toy query.

Idea 1 · The noise floor of softmax

For a query q and keys K = [k_1, ..., k_N], attention weights are:

wi​=∑j​exp(q⋅kj​/d​)exp(q⋅ki​/d​)​

No w_i is ever zero. If k_i is completely unrelated to q, the score q . k_i is not 0 — it fluctuates around zero with variance ||q||^2 / d. After softmax normalization, each unrelated token still contributes O(1/N) to the weighted sum. The total contribution of unrelated tokens is O((N-1)/N) = O(1) — not a small quantity.

What the model wants is something like a hard top-k: high weight on matching tokens, near-zero weight everywhere else. Softmax is too smooth to do that directly.

Idea 2 · The differential idea

Split each head's Q and K projections into two: Q=(Q1​,Q2​) and K=(K1​,K2​). Compute two attention maps:

A1​A2​​=softmax(Q1​K1T​/d​)=softmax(Q2​K2T​/d​)​

Output:

DiffAttn=(A1​−λ⋅A2​)V

The subtraction cancels whatever noise distribution the two maps share. If both maps have roughly uniform weight on the 127k unrelated tokens (which they will, at random initialization), those cancel. The signal — peaked weight on the few actually relevant tokens — only cancels if it appears in both maps at the same magnitude, which it will not once the model trains.

lambda is a learnable scalar per head, parameterized as lambda = exp(lambda_q1 dot lambda_k1) - exp(lambda_q2 dot lambda_k2) + lambda_init. It can be negative. In V1, lambda_init depends on the layer l: 0.8 - 0.6 * exp(-0.3 * (l - 1)), which rises from 0.2 in the first layer toward 0.8. V2 replaces all of this with a per-token, per-head λ: a small projection of the token's hidden state passed through a sigmoid, so λ lies between 0 and 1.

Idea 3 · Why this matches headed noise-canceling

Think of two noisy microphones recording the same voice. Both pick up the speaker plus correlated background noise. Subtract one from the other and the shared noise drops out. The voice survives because the two signals differ in phase or amplitude by enough to prevent full cancellation. The per-head lambda learns exactly this balance.

Idea 4 · V1 vs V2: the diff

V1 kept the parameter count equal to the baseline Transformer. To get two queries per head it halved the head dimension. That cost head expressiveness and — more painfully — halved the value cache per head. Decode had to load the value cache twice per step (once per softmax branch). Result: decode slower than baseline despite matching parameter count.

V2 doubles the number of query heads and keeps the KV heads the same. Adjacent query heads form a pair that shares the same keys and values (the same GQA group), so one standard FlashAttention call computes both maps of every pair. The head dimension stays the same as baseline. After the subtraction, the extra dimension is projected back down to match baseline Transformer's OW​ projection. Three things happen at once:

  1. Decode speed matches baseline (KV cache is loaded once).
  2. FlashAttention runs unchanged (no custom kernel).
  3. Arithmetic intensity at decode goes up (more compute per byte loaded from HBM).

V2 also removes the per-head RMSNorm that V1 applied after the subtraction. When attention is spread out, the subtracted output is small (about 1/√n of the values' size), so the norm must multiply it by about √n, roughly 90× at 8,192 tokens, and the resulting large gradients made training unstable at high learning rates. With the per-token sigmoid λ and shared values, the authors found the norm unnecessary, and V2's gradient norms match a standard Transformer's.

Idea 5 · When to reach for it

Workload Benefit
Long-context RAG (64k+) Cleaner attention maps, fewer hallucinated citations
Needle-in-haystack benchmarks Substantial accuracy lift past 32k
Multi-document QA Less cross-document interference
Code completion at 8k Marginal, not worth the architecture change
Short chat (< 4k) Essentially indistinguishable from baseline

The value grows with context length. At 4k tokens the noise floor is small enough that standard attention is fine. At 128k it is hurting you.

The same idea as a formula

Noise floor. One matching key with logit margin m over N−1 unrelated keys gets weight

wmatch​=em+N−1em​,
which falls toward 0 as N grows unless the margin grows like lnN.

Differential attention for one head pair:

DiffAttn(X)=(softmax(d​Q1​K1⊤​​)−λsoftmax(d​Q2​K2⊤​​))V.
Each row of the difference sums to 1−λ, and weights can be negative.

What cancels. Write each map as a shared part plus its own part, A1​=S+E1​+signal and A2​=S+E2​. Then A1​−λA2​=(1−λ)S+E1​−λE2​+signal: the shared part shrinks by 1−λ, but the independent parts add in variance, Var(E1​)+λ2Var(E2​).

λ. V1: λ=eλq1​⋅λk1​−eλq2​⋅λk2​+λinit​, one per layer. V2: λt​=σ(w⊤xt​) per token and head.

Worked example
How much weight does the match get?

A match with margin m=8 (the matching logit is 8 above the typical unrelated logit):

  • N=1,000: e8=2,981, so w=2,981/(2,981+999)=0.75.
  • N=128,000: w=2,981/(2,981+127,999)=0.023; the other 98% of the weight is spread over unrelated tokens.
  • To keep 50% at 128K, the margin must be ln127,999=11.8.

With differential attention and λ=0.8, a perfectly shared floor of 0.98 shrinks to (1−0.8)×0.98=0.196, while the match keeps nearly its full weight because the second map does not see it.

Step 1 · standard softmax attention

code/main.py implements differential attention in pure Python. A toy query with known signal-plus-noise structure lets you measure the noise-cancellation ratio directly.

Stdlib matrix ops: lists of lists, manual matmul, softmax with numerical-stability subtraction of the max.

def softmax(row):
    m = max(row)
    exps = [math.exp(x - m) for x in row]
    s = sum(exps)
    return [e / s for e in exps]

Step 2 · split Q, K into two halves

V1 style: halve the head dimension. V2 style: keep the head dimension and double the number of heads. The toy implementation uses V1 for pedagogical clarity — the math is identical, only the bookkeeping differs.

Step 3 · two softmax branches + subtraction

import math, random
random.seed(0)
def dot(a, b): return sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))
def softmax(row):
    m = max(row); exps = [math.exp(x - m) for x in row]; s = sum(exps)
    return [e / s for e in exps]
d, n, d_v, lam = 4, 6, 3, 0.8                       # 6 tokens, head dimension 4
rnd = lambda r, c: [[random.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(c)] for _ in range(r)]
Q1, Q2, K1, K2, V = rnd(n, d), rnd(n, d), rnd(n, d), rnd(n, d), rnd(n, d_v)
scale = math.sqrt(d)

A1 = [softmax([dot(q1, k) / scale for k in K1]) for q1 in Q1]
A2 = [softmax([dot(q2, k) / scale for k in K2]) for q2 in Q2]
diff_weights = [[a1 - lam * a2 for a1, a2 in zip(r1, r2)] for r1, r2 in zip(A1, A2)]
out = [[sum(w * v[j] for w, v in zip(row, V)) for j in range(d_v)] for row in diff_weights]

print("row 0 of A1          :", [round(x, 3) for x in A1[0]])
print("row 0 of A1 - lam*A2 :", [round(x, 3) for x in diff_weights[0]])
print(f"each differential row sums to 1 - lam = {sum(diff_weights[0]):.3f}; output shape {len(out)} x {len(out[0])}")
What you should see
row 0 of A1          : [0.018, 0.327, 0.116, 0.241, 0.163, 0.135]
row 0 of A1 - lam*A2 : [-0.311, 0.279, -0.099, 0.163, 0.101, 0.067]
each differential row sums to 1 - lam = 0.200; output shape 6 x 3

Note: the output weights can be negative. That is fine — the value cache still handles signed contributions. The subsequent V projection absorbs the sign.

Reading this output

Step 1. The "trained" map puts 0.045 on the signal and 0.955 on the 1,023 other positions, a signal-to-noise ratio (signal weight over the average |weight| elsewhere) of 48. Subtracting 0.8 times a second map of pure noise barely changes the signal weight (that map has nothing special at position 500) and removes much of the flat floor, so the ratio rises to 92. Note what the second map is here: an independent random map. What cancels is the average level of the floor, about 1/N per position in both maps, not the noise itself; the random fluctuations of the two maps add.

Step 2. That is why the advantage disappears as the noise grows: at a noise standard deviation of 1.0 the two ratios are equal (35), and at 1.5 and 2.0 differential attention is worse (11.8 vs 15.9, 5.4 vs 8.5). The exact computation below shows the other side: when the two maps share their noise, the gain is large.

Step 3. The script's V2 count is off. It gives the output projection 2⋅h⋅d inputs, but V2 projects back to h⋅d before WO​, as the text says; and it compares a GQA V2 (8 KV heads) with a full multi-head baseline (67.1M). Against a GQA baseline of 41.9M, V2 has about 58.9M attention parameters per layer, 17M more (next run, part D). The final line's "V2 brings the parameter cost down" is also wrong: V2 adds parameters and keeps the KV cache. The V1 count, equal to the baseline plus a few thousand λ parameters, is right.

Reading this output

A. With a logit margin of 4, a match gets 5% of the attention at 1K tokens and essentially none at 128K. Even a margin of 8, which dominates at 1K (75%), drops to 2.3% at 128K. To keep half the weight, the margin must grow like lnN: 11.8 at 128K. This is the noise floor of the lesson, stated exactly.

B. The lesson's toy with a dial for how much noise the two maps share. With no sharing (the toy's setting), subtraction helps at low noise (48 to 91) but hurts at high noise (18.5 to 13.8). As the shared part grows, the gain grows: at full sharing the ratio is 289 at low noise and 88 instead of 18 at high noise. Differential attention only works if training makes the second map learn the same distractions as the first and not the signal; it cannot remove noise the maps do not share.

C. When a head spreads its weight evenly over n positions, the output is the average of n random value vectors, whose size shrinks like 1/n​: 0.044 at 512 positions, 0.011 at 8,192. V1's per-head RMSNorm rescales the output back to size 1, multiplying it by 23 to 93, close to n​. Large multipliers mean large gradients, which is why V1 trained unstably at high learning rates and why V2, whose per-token λ can simply turn the subtraction down, removes the norm.

D. For a 7B-class layer with 8 KV heads, V2 needs 58.9M attention parameters against 41.9M for the baseline. That is 22% less than a plain Transformer with the same 64 query heads (75.5M), close to the blog's "about 25%". The KV cache is identical to the baseline's, 4 KB per token per layer in BF16, which is why decoding speed matches.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

As of 2026, differential attention is a research architecture. The V2 blog reports preliminary pre-training results from Microsoft: dense models and a 30B-A3B MoE trained on trillions of tokens, with a language-modelling loss about 0.02 to 0.03 lower at 1T tokens, fewer loss spikes and smaller activation outliers; long-context results were not yet published. No widely used open-weight model ships it. What production models use against the same softmax problems is listed under "What's current" below.

When you would reach for this in 2026:

  • Training a new model from scratch targeting 64k-plus effective context. Add differential attention from the start; retraining later is expensive.
  • Diagnosing long-context failures in any model: the noise-floor arithmetic applies to every softmax head, whether or not you change the architecture.

When you would not:

  • You are serving a pre-trained dense model with stable long-context performance. The retraining cost rarely pays back on existing weights.
  • Your context is always under 16k. Noise floor is negligible.

Reading this output

The noise-floor argument does not translate into failure here. At every length from 127 to 4,095 tokens, the model answers "7341" and gives the first digit a probability of 0.93 to 0.98. A few heads in the upper-middle layers do the retrieval, each putting about 96% of its attention on the four digits, compared with 0.1% for a head spreading attention evenly at 4,095 tokens. Their focus hardly changes with length: a large enough logit margin (part A of the previous run) beats a few thousand distractors. Most heads are simply not looking for the code (median weight on it 0.0001 or less).

Many heads use the first token as a parking place: at up to 2,047 tokens the median head puts 35 to 44% of its weight on token 0, and at 4,095 tokens 30% of the 480 heads still put more than half there. This attention sink is how a softmax head says "nothing here is relevant", since it cannot give everything zero. It is exactly the problem that learned sinks (gpt-oss) and output gates (Qwen3-Next) address in production, and that differential attention addresses by subtraction.

Two limits of this test. A single exact-match fact is the easiest long-context task; failures appear with several similar facts, paraphrased questions or much longer contexts, which is where the differential-attention papers report gains. And 4K is half of this model's trained context; real lost-in-the-middle tests go to 32K and beyond.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)

Differential attention itself is not yet used in widely deployed models. Production models handle the same softmax problems in other ways:

  • Attention sinks: softmax needs somewhere to put weight when nothing is relevant. gpt-oss adds a learned sink logit to each head; StreamingLLM-style serving keeps the first tokens in the cache for the same reason.
  • Gated attention: Qwen3-Next multiplies each head's output by a sigmoid gate computed from the token, which removes the sink and reduces activation outliers.
  • QK-norm: OLMo 2, Qwen3 and Gemma 3 normalise queries and keys before the dot product to keep attention logits from growing and destabilising training.

Sources: Differential Transformer, Differential Transformer V2 (Hugging Face blog), gpt-oss model card, Gated Attention for LLMs, StreamingLLM, Qwen3 technical report.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Softmax gives every token some weight; a match needs a logit margin of about ln N to dominate.
  • Differential attention: (softmax₁ − λ softmax₂) V cancels what the two maps share.
  • Independent noise does not cancel; it adds.
  • V2: twice the query heads, same KV heads and cache, one FlashAttention call, per-token sigmoid λ, no per-head norm.
  • Production models use attention sinks, output gates and QK-norm instead.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Focus and answer both hold

Both hold. The best head puts 0.96 to 0.98 of its attention on the four digits at every length up to 4,095 tokens, and the model answers "7341" each time, with a first-digit probability of 0.93 to 0.98. A trained head can make the logit margin of the right token large enough that a few thousand distractors do not matter: from part A of the exact computation, a margin of 12 keeps 95% of the weight even among 8,000 tokens. The noise floor becomes a real problem only when the margin cannot be made that large: much longer contexts, several similar candidates (other codes, other vaults), or facts that must be combined.

SolutionB1 · Zero, always

Zero. With identical projections the two maps are the same, A1​=A2​, so A1​−1⋅A2​=0 and the head outputs nothing whatever the input: every row sums to 1−λ=0 and all its weights are zero. Gradients to the query and key projections are zero too, so the two branches could only become different through the λ parameters. This is why the two branches need separate projections (V1) or separate query heads (V2), and why λ should start below 1: V1 initialises it between 0.2 and 0.8, and V2's sigmoid keeps it between 0 and 1, so at the start the difference keeps at least some of the first map.
