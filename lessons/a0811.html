In plain words first
The idea in one line

Audio is a 1-D signal at 16-48 kHz. A five-second clip is 80-240k samples. No transformer attends to that sequence directly. The standard solution: a neural codec (Encodec, SoundStream, DAC) compresses audio to discrete tokens at 50-75 Hz, and a transformer or diffusion model generates tokens.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means

RVQ "Residual VQ" Cascade of K quantizers; each models the residual of the previous.
Token "One codec symbol" Discrete index into a codebook; 1024 or 2048 typical.
Delayed parallel "Offset codebooks" Emit K token streams with staggered offsets to reduce sequence length.

Vocoder "Mel to wave" Neural component that converts mel spectrograms back to audio.

The problem

In plain words first

Sound is a long list of air-pressure readings, 16,000 to 48,000 per second, far too many for a model to generate one at a time. Modern audio generators therefore work in two stages. A neural codec compresses sound into a short stream of tokens, tens of frames per second, each frame described by a few code numbers from learned codebooks, and can turn tokens back into sound. A generator then produces those tokens: a transformer writing them one step at a time, like a language model (good for speech, because it streams), or a diffusion model shaping a whole clip of continuous codes at once (common for music and effects). This lesson runs the lesson's token-counting toy, then a real codec, Meta's EnCodec, on real speech at five bit rates, and a real token generator, MusicGen, making a few seconds of music on this page's CPU. Nothing is trained.

Three audio generation tasks:

  1. Text-to-speech. Given text, produce speech. Clean speech is narrow-band and has strong phonetic structure — solved well by transformer-over-tokens. VALL-E (Microsoft), NaturalSpeech 3, ElevenLabs, OpenAI TTS.
  2. Music generation. Given a prompt (text, melody, chord progression, genre), produce music. Much broader distribution. MusicGen (Meta), Stable Audio 2.5, Suno, Udio, ElevenLabs Music.
  3. Audio effects / sound design. Given a prompt, produce ambient sound or Foley. AudioGen, AudioLDM 2, Stable Audio Open.

All three run on the same substrate: neural audio codec + token-AR or diffusion generator.

The idea

Idea 1 · Neural audio codecs

Encodec (Meta, 2022), SoundStream (Google, 2021), Descript Audio Codec (DAC, 2023). A convolutional encoder compresses waveform to a per-timestep vector; residual vector quantization (RVQ) converts each vector to a cascade of K codebook indices. Decoder reverses it. EnCodec at 24 kHz produces 75 frames per second; 8 RVQ codebooks of 1,024 entries give 600 tokens per second, 6 kbps (the run under "Use it").

waveform (16000 samples/sec)
    └─ encoder conv ─┐
                     ├─ RVQ layer 1 → indices at 75 Hz
                     ├─ RVQ layer 2 → indices at 75 Hz
                     ├─ ...
                     └─ RVQ layer 8

Idea 2 · Two generative paradigms on top

Token-autoregressive. Flatten RVQ tokens into a sequence, run a decoder-only transformer. MusicGen uses "delayed parallel" to emit K codebook streams in parallel with per-stream offsets. VALL-E generates speech tokens from a text prompt + 3-second voice sample.

Latent diffusion. Pack codec tokens as continuous latents or model them with categorical diffusion. Stable Audio (Open and 2.x) runs a diffusion transformer on continuous VAE latents. AudioLDM 2 uses text-to-mel-to-audio diffusion.

The 2024-2026 trend: flow matching is winning for music (faster inference, cleaner samples) while token-AR still dominates speech because it is naturally causal and streams well.

The same idea as a formula

Residual vector quantisation. The encoder turns audio into one vector et​∈RD per frame. With K codebooks C1​,…,CK​ of size V:

r0​=et​,ik​=argjmin​∥rk−1​−Ck​[j]∥,rk​=rk−1​−Ck​[ik​],e^t​=k=1∑K​Ck​[ik​]

Each codebook encodes what the previous ones missed, so dropping the last codebooks lowers the bit rate gracefully.

Bit rate. f frames per second × K codebooks × log2​V bits.

Token generation. A frame has K tokens. Flattening gives the exact factorisation p=∏t​∏k​p(it,k​∣all earlier tokens) with TK steps. MusicGen's delay pattern instead emits codebook k of frame s−k at step s: all K codebooks in one step, T+K−1 steps in total, and each token still sees the lower codebooks of its own frame from earlier steps.

Diffusion on audio latents. A VAE gives continuous latents z∈RT′×C; the same loss as Lesson 8.6 or 8.13 is used, conditioned on text (T5 or CLAP embeddings).

Worked example
Codec arithmetic by hand
  • EnCodec 24 kHz, 8 codebooks: 75×8×log2​1024=75×8×10=6,000 bits per second, 6 kbps, 600 tokens per second. Raw 16-bit audio: 24,000×16=384 kbps, 64 times more.
  • The 1.5 kbps setting: 2 codebooks, 150 tokens per second.
  • MusicGen's codec: 32 kHz audio, 50 frames per second, 4 codebooks of 2,048: 50×4×11=2,200 bits per second.
  • A 30-second song in MusicGen: 30×50=1,500 frames, 6,000 tokens. Flattened: 6,000 decoder steps; with the delay pattern: 1,500+3=1,503 steps, four times fewer.
  • Streaming at 50 frames per second means one decoder step every 20 ms per listener.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "A good codec reproduces the waveform."
    ✓ It reproduces what we hear: magnitude spectra and their timing, not exact phase. EnCodec's waveform SNR is only 2–7 dB in the run under "Use it", yet the spectrograms match closely.
  • ✗ "More codebooks always help a lot."
    ✓ Each codebook models a smaller residual: the first explains about 80% of the encoder output, the last sixteen together less than 5%.
  • ✗ "Token models need one step per token."
    ✓ Delay patterns emit one token per codebook per step, so steps track frames, not tokens.

Step 1 · synthetic audio tokens

code/main.py simulates the core idea: train a tiny next-token transformer on synthetic "audio token" sequences generated from two distinct "styles" (alternating low and high tokens for style A, monotonic ramp for style B). Condition on style and sample.

def make_tokens(style, length, vocab_size, rng):
    if style == 0:  # "speech-like": alternating
        return [i % vocab_size for i in range(length)]
    # "music-like": ramp
    return [(i * 3) % vocab_size for i in range(length)]

Step 2 · train a tiny token predictor

A bigram-style predictor conditioned on style. The point is the pattern: codec tokens → cross-entropy training → autoregressive sampling.

Step 3 · sample conditionally

Given the style token and a starting token, sample the next token from the predicted distribution. Continue for 20-40 tokens.

Reading this output

The "training" is counting: for each style, a 16 × 16 table of how often token b follows token a. Despite the source's labels ("alternating" and "ramp"), both styles are ramps: style 0 climbs by 1 (sometimes 2) per step, style 1 by 3 (sometimes 4), wrapping at 16. The samples follow their style, and lower temperature makes them more regular: at 0.7 style 1 almost always steps by 3, at 1.0 the steps vary more. After token 5 in style 0, the model is unsure between 5, 6 and 7 (entropy 1.20 nats, top choice 0.48), the uncertainty sampling turns into variety. The prompt continuation is VALL-E's idea in miniature: the generator simply carries on from given tokens, which is how a three-second voice sample steers a cloned voice.

Pitfalls

  • Codec quality caps output quality. If the codec can't represent a sound faithfully, no amount of generator quality helps. DAC, EnCodec and Mimi are common open choices.
  • RVQ error accumulation. Each RVQ layer models the residual of the previous. Errors on layer 1 propagate. Sampling with temperature 0 on higher layers helps.
  • Musical structure. 30 seconds of tokens is 20k+ tokens at 75 Hz. Hard for transformers. MusicGen uses sliding window + prompt continuation; Stable Audio uses shorter clips + crossfading.
  • Artifacts at boundaries. Crossfading between generated clips needs careful overlap-add.
  • Clean-data appetite. Music generators need tens of thousands of hours of licensed music. The record labels' lawsuits against Suno and Udio (2024) brought this to the surface; in late 2025 several labels settled with both companies in exchange for licensed models.
  • Voice cloning ethics. A 3-second sample plus a text prompt is enough for VALL-E / XTTS / ElevenLabs to clone a voice. Every production model needs abuse detection + opt-out lists.

A real codec on real speech

facebook/encodec_24khz in transformers on 5 seconds of LibriSpeech: five bit rates with their codebooks, tokens per second, waveform SNR and log-mel distance; then the residual left after each codebook; spectrograms at 1.5 and 6 kbps.

import io, numpy as np, torch, soundfile as sf, librosa, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from datasets import load_dataset, Audio
from transformers import EncodecModel, AutoProcessor

torch.set_num_threads(2)
model = EncodecModel.from_pretrained("facebook/encodec_24khz").eval()
proc = AutoProcessor.from_pretrained("facebook/encodec_24khz")
ds = load_dataset("hf-internal-testing/librispeech_asr_dummy", "clean", split="validation").cast_column("audio", Audio(decode=False))
wav, sr = sf.read(io.BytesIO(ds[0]["audio"]["bytes"]))
wav = librosa.resample(wav.astype(np.float32), orig_sr=sr, target_sr=24000)[: 24000 * 5]      # 5 s of real speech
x = torch.tensor(wav)[None, None]
print(f"EnCodec 24 kHz: {sum(p.numel() for p in model.parameters()) / 1e6:.1f}M parameters; "
      f"{len(wav):,} samples ({len(wav) / 24000:.0f} s) of speech")

def logmel(a):
    return np.log(librosa.feature.melspectrogram(y=a, sr=24000, n_fft=1024, hop_length=256, n_mels=80) + 1e-5)
ref = logmel(wav); outs = {}
print(" kbps  codebooks  tokens/s   SNR (dB)   log-mel distance")
with torch.no_grad():
    for bw in (1.5, 3.0, 6.0, 12.0, 24.0):
        enc = model.encode(x, bandwidth=bw)
        codes = enc.audio_codes                      # (chunks, batch, codebooks, frames)
        y = model.decode(codes, enc.audio_scales)[0][0, 0, : len(wav)].numpy()
        k, frames = codes.shape[2], codes.shape[3]
        snr = 10 * np.log10((wav ** 2).sum() / ((wav - y) ** 2).sum())
        print(f"{bw:5.1f}  {k:9d}  {k * frames / (len(wav) / 24000):8.0f}   {snr:8.1f}   {np.abs(logmel(y) - ref).mean():16.3f}")
        outs[bw] = y

    # Residual vector quantisation, layer by layer: how much of the encoder's output is left unexplained.
    emb = model.encoder(x)                             # (1, 128, frames): one 128-number vector per 1/75 s
    residual = emb.clone(); left = []
    for layer in model.quantizer.layers:
        q = layer.decode(layer.encode(residual)); residual = residual - q
        left.append(float(residual.norm() / emb.norm()))
print(f"encoder output: {emb.shape[1]} numbers x {emb.shape[2]} frames ({emb.shape[2] / 5:.0f} per second); "
      f"codebook size {model.config.codebook_size}")
print("share of the encoder output left after 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32 codebooks:",
      " ".join(f"{left[i - 1]:.2f}" for i in (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32)))

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(9, 2.6), sharey=True)
for ax, (name, a) in zip(axes, (("original", wav), ("1.5 kbps (2 codebooks)", outs[1.5]), ("6 kbps (8 codebooks)", outs[6.0]))):
    ax.imshow(logmel(a), origin="lower", aspect="auto", cmap="magma"); ax.set_title(name, fontsize=8); ax.set_xticks([])
axes[0].set_ylabel("mel bin", fontsize=8)
plt.subplots_adjust(left=0.06, right=0.99, top=0.88, bottom=0.04, wspace=0.04)
plt.savefig("v2.png", dpi=60); plt.show()

Reading this output

Each codebook adds 75 tokens per second, 0.75 kbps. More codebooks steadily improve the log-mel distance, from 0.85 at 1.5 kbps to 0.41 at 24 kbps, while the waveform SNR stays between 2 and 7 dB, far below any "good" waveform figure: the codec does not try to reproduce the exact waveform, only what the ear hears, and its decoder is trained with discriminators (Lesson 8.3) to sound right rather than match sample by sample. The spectrograms show the same thing: at 6 kbps (8 codebooks) the harmonics and pauses of the speech match the original closely, while at 1.5 kbps the fine harmonic structure is blurred but the words' timing and shape survive. The residual line is RVQ at work: one codebook explains about 79% of the encoder's output, two leave 18%, eight leave 11%, and the last sixteen codebooks together remove only the final 4% of residual.

import time, numpy as np, torch, librosa, matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from transformers import AutoProcessor, MusicgenForConditionalGeneration

torch.set_num_threads(2); torch.manual_seed(0)
proc = AutoProcessor.from_pretrained("facebook/musicgen-small")
model = MusicgenForConditionalGeneration.from_pretrained("facebook/musicgen-small").eval()
n = lambda m: sum(p.numel() for p in m.parameters()) / 1e6
sr, K = model.config.audio_encoder.sampling_rate, model.config.decoder.num_codebooks
print(f"MusicGen-small: T5 text encoder {n(model.text_encoder):.0f}M | decoder {n(model.decoder):.0f}M | EnCodec {n(model.audio_encoder):.0f}M; "
      f"{K} codebooks at {model.config.audio_encoder.frame_rate} frames per second, {sr // 1000} kHz audio")

inputs = proc(text=["calm lo-fi piano with soft drums"], padding=True, return_tensors="pt")
steps = 200
t0 = time.perf_counter()
with torch.no_grad():
    audio = model.generate(**inputs, max_new_tokens=steps, do_sample=True, guidance_scale=3.0)[0, 0].numpy()
dt = time.perf_counter() - t0
frames = len(audio) // (sr // model.config.audio_encoder.frame_rate)
print(f"{steps} decoder steps -> {frames} frames x {K} codebooks = {frames * K} tokens -> {len(audio) / sr:.2f} s of audio; "

with torch.no_grad():                         # re-encode the audio with MusicGen's EnCodec to see (approximately) its tokens
    codes = model.audio_encoder.encode(torch.tensor(audio)[None, None]).audio_codes[0, 0]   # (codebooks, frames)
print("the delay pattern: at decoder step s, codebook k emits the token for frame s - k ('...' = not started yet):")
for k in range(K):
    row = ["  ..."] * k + [f"{int(v):5d}" for v in codes[k, : 7 - k]]

Break it on purpose

Replace MusicGen's delay pattern with "parallel" decoding: at step s, emit all four codebooks of frame s at once, each predicted from the earlier frames only. Generation gets three steps shorter. What does each codebook now not know when it is chosen, and how would that show up in the sound? (Solution B1.)

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Codec first: encoder → RVQ tokens (a few codebooks at 50–75 frames per second) → decoder.
  • Bit rate = frames/s × codebooks × bits per code; each extra codebook refines a smaller residual.
  • Generators: token-AR (streams, speech) or diffusion on continuous latents (fixed-length clips, music).
  • Delay patterns make decoder steps scale with frames, not tokens.
  • Judge audio by spectra and listening, not waveform SNR; streaming needs real-time factor above 1.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Far from it

Far from it: even at 24 kbps, 32 codebooks, EnCodec's waveform SNR is about 7 dB, and at the usual 6 kbps about 5 dB. Yet the log-mel distance falls steadily and the spectrograms match: the reconstructions sound right. Neural codecs are trained with spectral and adversarial losses, so they reproduce what the ear is sensitive to (energy in each frequency band over time) and are free to change the waveform's fine phase, which the ear barely notices. That is why codecs, TTS and music models are evaluated with perceptual measures (mel or spectral distances, listening tests, MOS) rather than SNR.

SolutionB1 · Codebooks that cannot coordinate

With the delay pattern, codebook 1 of a frame is chosen one step after codebook 0 of the same frame, so it is conditioned on it; codebook 2 sees codebooks 0 and 1, and so on, which matters because each codebook encodes the residual of the ones before it. In parallel decoding, all four codebooks of a frame are sampled at once, each only from earlier frames, so the fine codebooks are guessed without knowing what coarse sound they are supposed to correct. Combinations that never occur in real audio become possible, heard as noise, buzz and smeared transients. The MusicGen paper compared these patterns: flattening (exact but four times as many steps) scored best, the delay pattern was close at a quarter of the steps, and parallel decoding was clearly worse.

Production note: audio is a streaming problem

Audio is the one output modality users expect to arrive as it is generated, not all-at-once. In production terms this means TPOT matters (Time Per Output Token) because the user's listening speed is the target throughput — not their reading speed. For EnCodec at 75 frames per second, the server must run at least 75 decoder steps per second per listener (each step emitting one token per codebook, thanks to the delay pattern) to keep playback smooth; MusicGen's 32 kHz codec needs 50.

Two architectural consequences:

  • Flow-matching audio models cannot stream trivially. Diffusion and flow-matching models such as Stable Audio render a fixed clip length in one sampling run. To stream, you chunk the clip and overlap boundaries — think sliding-window diffusion — adding 100-300ms of latency overhead vs a codec AR model.

If the product is "live voice chat" or "real-time music continuation", pick the codec AR path. If it is "render a 30-second clip on submit", flow-matching wins on quality and total latency.
