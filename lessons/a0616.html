In plain words first
The idea in one line

Voice cloning shipped faster than defenses. 2026 production voice systems need two things: a detector (AASIST, RawNet2) that classifies real vs fake speech, and a watermark (AudioSeal) that survives compression and editing. Ship both or do not ship voice cloning.

The problem

In plain words first

Lesson 6.8 showed that a few seconds of someone's voice is enough to clone it. Two defences answer two different questions. Detection asks "does this recording sound synthetic?" and must work on audio from anyone, including attackers who do nothing to help. Watermarking asks "did our system make this?": the generator hides a faint, inaudible pattern in every output, and a matching detector finds it later, even after the file has been compressed or re-recorded. A third layer, provenance, attaches signed metadata to the file. This lesson runs Meta's open AudioSeal watermark against ten everyday and hostile edits, and the standard detection baseline, AASIST, against fake speech it was never trained on. Nothing is trained.

Three related defenses:

  1. Anti-spoofing / deepfake detection. Given an audio clip, is it synthetic or real? ASVspoof benchmarks (ASVspoof 2019 → 2021 → 5) are the gold standard.
  2. Audio watermarking. Embed an imperceptible signal in generated audio that a detector can extract later. AudioSeal (Meta) and WavMark are the open options.
  3. Authenticated provenance. Cryptographic signing of audio files + metadata. C2PA / Content Authenticity Initiative.

Detection handles adversaries who don't cooperate. Watermarking handles compliance — AI-generated audio should be identifiable as such. Both are required in 2026.

The idea

The same idea as a formula

Detection as a score. A countermeasure maps audio x to a score s(x), higher meaning more likely bona fide. With threshold θ, false acceptance FAR(θ)=P(s≥θ∣fake) and false rejection FRR(θ)=P(s<θ∣real). The equal error rate is the value where they meet, EER=FAR(θ∗)=FRR(θ∗); 50% is chance.

Additive watermark. A generator G produces a signal from the audio and a message m∈{0,1}16:

x~=x+G(x,m),imperceptibility: SNR=10log10​∥G(x,m)∥2∥x∥2​ dB

A detector D outputs, for every sample n, a probability pn​ that the watermark is present and 16 bit probabilities m^. Training adds random edits A (noise, filtering, compression) between G and D and minimises detection and message losses on D(A(x~)), so the pattern survives the edits that were in training.

Bit accuracy. BA=161​∑j​[m^j​=mj​]; random guessing gives 50%, and with 16 bits the chance of decoding the whole payload by luck is 2−16≈1.5×10−5.

Worked example
EER and false alarms by hand

EER from four scores. Real scores {2.1,1.4,0.3,−0.2}, fake scores {0.9,−0.5,−1.0,−1.8}. At θ=0.3: one fake of four is accepted (0.9), FAR = 25%; one real of four is rejected (−0.2), FRR = 25%. They are equal, so EER = 25%.

Why per-sample scores matter. AudioSeal scores every sample. Averaging over a 2 s span of 32,000 samples with a 0.5 threshold, a watermark spliced into part of a long recording is located to within a fraction of a second, so an edit that pastes a generated phrase into a real recording can be found.

Payload collisions. A 16-bit payload names 65,536 values: enough for a model version and a coarse date, not for a user ID across millions of users; production systems map the payload to a database record.

Idea 1 · ASVspoof 5 — the 2024-2025 benchmark

Biggest change from prior editions:

  • Crowdsourced data (not studio clean) — realistic conditions.
  • ~2000 speakers (vs ~100 before).
  • 32 attack algorithms. TTS + voice conversion + adversarial perturbation.
  • Two tracks. Countermeasure (CM) standalone detection; Spoofing-robust ASV (SASV) for biometric systems.

The gap between in-domain and real-world results is the main fact of this field. The official AASIST checkpoint, trained on ASVspoof 2019 LA, scores 0.83% EER there, but 12.4% on ASVspoof 2021 LA, 17.0% on 2021 DF, 35.5% on ASVspoof 5 and 43.0% on the In-the-Wild set of real-world deepfakes, according to the Speech Anti-Spoofing Benchmarks evaluation (50% is chance). The run under Step 3 shows the same on new fakes.

Idea 2 · AASIST and RawNet2 — detection model families

AASIST (Jung et al., ICASSP 2022). A sinc-filter front end over the raw waveform, a RawNet2-style encoder, and graph attention across spectral and temporal sub-graphs; 0.3 M parameters. ASVspoof 5's organisers supply it as a baseline, and most stronger systems keep its back end.

RawNet2. Convolutional front-end over raw waveform + TDNN backbone. Simpler baseline; still competitive with fine-tuning.

Self-supervised front ends. Replacing the hand-designed front end with a pretrained speech model (wav2vec 2.0, WavLM) plus an AASIST-style back end is the usual way to generalise better to unseen attacks; it is the pattern among stronger ASVspoof 5 submissions.

Idea 3 · AudioSeal — the 2024 watermark default

Meta's AudioSeal (Jan 2024, v0.2 Dec 2024). Key design:

  • Localized. Detects the watermark per-frame at 16 kHz sample resolution (1/16000 s).
  • Generator + detector jointly trained. Generator learns to embed inaudible signal; detector learns to find it through augmentations.
  • Robust. Survives MP3 / AAC compression, EQ, speed-shift ±10%, noise mix +10 dB SNR.
  • Fast. One forward pass detects the watermark, which the paper reports as up to two orders of magnitude faster than WavMark's brute-force synchronisation search.
  • Capacity. 16-bit payload (can encode model ID, generation timestamp, user ID) embeddable in each utterance.

Idea 4 · WavMark

The pre-AudioSeal open baseline. Invertible neural network, 32 bits/sec. Problems:

  • Synchronization brute-force is slow.
  • Can be removed by Gaussian noise or MP3 compression.
  • Not real-time friendly.

Idea 5 · WaveVerify (July 2025)

Addresses AudioSeal's weaknesses — specifically temporal manipulations (reversal, speed). Uses FiLM-based generator + Mixture-of-Experts detector. Competitive with AudioSeal on standard attacks; handles temporal edits.

Idea 6 · The gap adversaries exploit

AudioMarkBench (NeurIPS 2024) found that pitch shifting removes the watermarks it tested, and the run under Step 2 shows the same for AudioSeal: bit accuracy falls to chance. Pitch shift and speed change are the universal attacks. No 2026 watermark is fully robust to aggressive pitch modification. This is why you need detection (AASIST) alongside watermarking.

Idea 7 · C2PA / Content Authenticity Initiative

Not an ML technique — a manifest format. Audio files carry cryptographically signed metadata about creation tool, author, date. ElevenLabs, for example, attaches C2PA credentials to its audio alongside a watermark. Good for provenance; does nothing if a bad actor re-encodes and strips metadata.

Step 1 · a simple spectral-feature detector (toy)

def spectral_rolloff(spec, percentile=0.85):
    cum = 0
    total = sum(spec)
    if total == 0:
        return 0
    threshold = total * percentile
    for k, v in enumerate(spec):
        cum += v
        if cum >= threshold:
            return k
    return len(spec) - 1

def is_suspicious(audio):
    spec = magnitude_spectrum(audio)
    rolloff = spectral_rolloff(spec)
    return rolloff / len(spec) > 0.92

Synthetic speech often has unusually flat high-frequency energy. Production detectors use AASIST, not this. But the intuition holds.

import subprocess, tempfile, os, librosa
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def codec(x, args, ext):
    """Round-trip through ffmpeg (as a messaging app or phone network would)."""
    with tempfile.TemporaryDirectory() as d:
        sf.write(f"{d}/in.wav", x, 16000)
        subprocess.run(["ffmpeg", "-loglevel", "error", "-i", f"{d}/in.wav", *args, f"{d}/c.{ext}"], check=True)
        subprocess.run(["ffmpeg", "-loglevel", "error", "-i", f"{d}/c.{ext}", "-ar", "16000", "-ac", "1", f"{d}/out.wav"], check=True)
        y = sf.read(f"{d}/out.wav", dtype="float32")[0]
    return np.pad(y, (0, max(0, len(x) - len(y))))[: len(x)]
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def noise(x, snr_db):
    n = rng.standard_normal(len(x)).astype(np.float32)
    return x + n * np.sqrt((x ** 2).mean() / (n ** 2).mean() / 10 ** (snr_db / 10))
attacks = {
    "none": lambda x: x,
    "white noise, 20 dB SNR": lambda x: noise(x, 20),
    "white noise, 5 dB SNR": lambda x: noise(x, 5),
    "MP3, 64 kbps": lambda x: codec(x, ["-b:a", "64k"], "mp3"),
    "Opus, 16 kbps (VoIP)": lambda x: codec(x, ["-c:a", "libopus", "-b:a", "16k"], "ogg"),
    "phone band (8 kHz resample)": lambda x: librosa.resample(librosa.resample(x, orig_sr=16000, target_sr=8000), orig_sr=8000, target_sr=16000)[: len(x)],
    "volume x0.3": lambda x: 0.3 * x,
    "speed +10% (resample)": lambda x: librosa.resample(x, orig_sr=16000, target_sr=int(16000 / 1.1)),
    "pitch shift +2 semitones": lambda x: librosa.effects.pitch_shift(x, sr=16000, n_steps=2),
    "pitch shift -2 semitones": lambda x: librosa.effects.pitch_shift(x, sr=16000, n_steps=-2),
}

Step 3 · evaluation — EER

def eer(real_scores, fake_scores):
    thresholds = sorted(set(real_scores + fake_scores))
    best = (1.0, 0.0)
    for t in thresholds:
        far = sum(1 for s in fake_scores if s >= t) / len(fake_scores)
        frr = sum(1 for s in real_scores if s < t) / len(real_scores)
        if abs(far - frr) < best[0]:
            best = (abs(far - frr), (far + frr) / 2)
    return best[1]

AASIST against fakes it has never seen

Twelve real LibriSpeech clips against three kinds of fake made from the same sentences: two open TTS systems (SpeechT5 with a HiFi-GAN vocoder, and MMS-TTS, a VITS model) and real speech re-synthesised through the EnCodec codec of Lesson 6.13. Scored by the official AASIST checkpoint (ONNX), clean and after a 16 kbps Opus call channel, and by the toy roll-off feature from Step 1.

import onnxruntime as ort
from huggingface_hub import hf_hub_download
from transformers import (SpeechT5Processor, SpeechT5ForTextToSpeech, SpeechT5HifiGan, VitsModel, AutoTokenizer,
                          EncodecModel)

# Detector: AASIST (0.3M parameters), the official ASVspoof 2019 LA checkpoint exported to ONNX. Score = bona-fide logit.
aasist = ort.InferenceSession(hf_hub_download("SpeechAntiSpoofingBenchmarks/AASIST", "aasist.onnx"))
def aasist_score(x):
    x = np.tile(x, int(np.ceil(64600 / len(x))))[:64600]            # first ~4 s, repeat-padded as in the official code
    return float(aasist.run(None, {"wav": x[None].astype(np.float32)})[0][0, 1])

# Real speech, and three kinds of fake made from the same sentences.
ids = list(range(20, 32))
texts = [" ".join(libri[i]["text"].lower().split()[:14]) for i in ids]
real = [clip(i) for i in ids]
tproc = SpeechT5Processor.from_pretrained("microsoft/speecht5_tts")
st5 = SpeechT5ForTextToSpeech.from_pretrained("microsoft/speecht5_tts").eval()
hifigan = SpeechT5HifiGan.from_pretrained("microsoft/speecht5_hifigan").eval()

voices = [torch.tensor(xv[j]["xvector"])[None] for j in range(0, 7000, 600)][: len(ids)]

def encodec_resynth(x):
    y = torch.tensor(librosa.resample(x, orig_sr=16000, target_sr=24000))[None, None]
    e = enc.encode(y, bandwidth=6.0)
    return librosa.resample(enc.decode(e.audio_codes, e.audio_scales)[0][0, 0].numpy(), orig_sr=24000, target_sr=16000)

        torch.manual_seed(0); fakes["MMS-TTS / VITS (TTS)"].append(vits(**vtok(t, return_tensors="pt")).waveform[0].numpy())

def eer_from(real_s, fake_s):              # higher score = more real
    ts = np.sort(np.r_[real_s, fake_s]); best = (2, 0)
    for th in ts:
        frr = np.mean(np.array(real_s) < th); far = np.mean(np.array(fake_s) >= th)
        best = min(best, (abs(far - frr), (far + frr) / 2))
    return best[1]
def rolloff_score(x):                      # the toy detector's idea: a high roll-off = suspicious, so negate
    return -float(np.median(librosa.feature.spectral_rolloff(y=x, sr=16000, roll_percent=0.85)))

In clean conditions AASIST separates real speech from all three fakes almost perfectly (EER 8%, 0% and 8%, which is at most one clip in twelve; mean bona-fide score 3.24 for real against −1.15 and −1.94 for the two TTS systems). These are older open systems (SpeechT5 from 2022 and MMS-TTS from 2023, both with 16 kHz neural vocoders), close to the kinds of vocoder among AASIST's 2019 training attacks; EnCodec resynthesis of real speech is the hardest of the three. Passing both real and fake audio through a 16 kbps Opus call channel raises the EERs to 17%, 8% and 8%, because the codec erases some of the artefacts the detector relies on. With 12 clips per class each error moves the EER by about 8 points, so read these as indications. On newer generators and real-world deepfakes the same checkpoint does far worse in published evaluations (35.5% on ASVspoof 5, 43.0% on In-the-Wild). The roll-off feature scores 92%, worse than chance: the toy's assumption that fakes have more high-frequency energy is backwards for these 16 kHz vocoders, which have less. Flipped, it would score about 8%. A hand-picked feature captures one generator's quirk, not "fakeness".

Step 4 · the production integration

def safe_tts(text, voice, clone_reference=None):
    if clone_reference is not None:
        verify_consent(user_id, clone_reference)
    audio = tts_model.synthesize(text, voice)
    audio_with_wm = audioseal_embed(audio, payload=build_payload(user_id, model_id))
    manifest = c2pa_sign(audio_with_wm, user_id, timestamp=now())
    return audio_with_wm, manifest

Every generation ships: (1) watermark, (2) signed manifest, (3) retention-policy-compliant audit log.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Watermarks in shipping products. Google's SynthID marks audio from Lyria and NotebookLM's audio overviews; ElevenLabs added SynthID to its TTS output alongside C2PA credentials and a public audio detector; Resemble's open PerTh watermark (MIT) is embedded in every output of its Chatterbox TTS; Meta's AudioSeal (MIT) is the common open option.
  • Detection services (ElevenLabs' classifier, Google's SynthID Detector portal) check for their own marks; general deepfake detection for call centres and media is sold by specialist vendors such as Pindrop and Reality Defender, and should be validated on your own traffic, as the run above shows.
  • Regulation (Lesson 6.8): the EU AI Act's Article 50 requires machine-readable marking of AI-generated audio.

Sources: AudioSeal, AASIST, AASIST benchmarks, ASVspoof 5, SynthID, ElevenLabs and SynthID, Resemble PerTh, Pindrop, Reality Defender, C2PA.

Pitfalls

  • Watermark without detector ever running. Pointless. Ship the detector in your CI.
  • Detection without calibration. AASIST trained on ASVspoof LA overfits; real-world accuracy drops. Calibrate on your domain.
  • Pitch-shift gap. Aggressive pitch shift removes most watermarks. Have a detection fallback.
  • Metadata strip-and-rehost. C2PA is trivially bypassable by re-encoding. Always add cryptographic + perceptual (watermark) defense together.
  • Liveness as detection. Ask user to say a random phrase. Prevents replay attacks but not real-time cloning.
Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Detection works on anyone's audio; watermarking only on your own; provenance only while metadata survives. Use all three.
  • Measure detectors on fakes they have not seen: in-domain EERs say little about real-world performance.
  • AudioSeal survives codecs, resampling and volume changes; noise at low SNR, pitch shift and speed change remove it.
  • Per-sample watermark scores locate generated spans inside real recordings.
  • Every generation: watermark, signed manifest, audit log; run the detector in CI.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Codecs mild, pitch and speed fatal

From least to most damaging: MP3 at 64 kbps (detection 1.00, all bits), Opus at 16 kbps (0.99, all bits), then the 10% speed-up (0.01, 44% of bits) and the pitch shift (0.00, about 50%). Codecs keep the spectral regions the watermark uses, and compression is among AudioSeal's training augmentations; pitch and speed changes move every frequency and stretch time, so the detector no longer finds the pattern where it expects it.

SolutionB1 · The detector trusts the rate you give it

Both calls give the same result: a detection probability near 0.001 and about a third of the bits right. Since version 0.2, AudioSeal ignores sample_rate (it logs a deprecation warning) and always assumes 16 kHz. Read as 16 kHz, a 24 kHz file plays slower and lower, and Step 2 showed that the watermark does not survive pitch or speed changes. Resample to 16 kHz yourself before embedding and before detecting.
