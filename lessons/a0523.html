In plain words first
The idea in one line

Chunking configuration influences retrieval quality as much as the choice of embedding model (Vectara NAACL 2025). Get chunking wrong and no amount of reranking saves you.

The problem

In plain words first

Before a RAG system can retrieve anything, long documents have to be cut into pieces ("chunks"), each embedded and stored separately. How you cut decides what can be found. Cut too small and the answer is separated from the words that identify it. Cut too large and the answer is buried in a long chunk whose single vector mostly describes other things, which also costs more context in the prompt. Cut in the wrong place and a sentence is split in half. This lesson explains why one vector per chunk forces this trade-off. It compares the common strategies (fixed-size, recursive, sentence windows, semantic, parent-child) and the "contextual" tricks that prepend surrounding information to each chunk. Then it measures them on real Wikipedia articles with hundreds of questions whose answers are known, so you can see the recall-versus-context trade-off rather than take a blog post's word for it.

You put a 50-page contract into a RAG system. User asks: "What is the termination clause?" The retriever returns the cover page. Why? Because the model was trained on 512-token chunks and the termination clause sits 20 pages in, split across a page break, with no local keywords tying it to the query.

The fix is not "buy a better embedding model." The fix is chunking. How big? Overlap? Where to split? With surrounding context?

Published comparisons show surprising results:

  • Vectara's 2024 study (Qu, Bao and Tu, "Is Semantic Chunking Worth the Computational Cost?") found that semantic chunking did not consistently beat simple fixed-size chunking, and concluded fixed-size is the more efficient, reliable choice for most applications.
  • NVIDIA's 2025 comparison found page-level chunking the most accurate and most consistent default across its datasets, with about 15% overlap best among the few overlap settings it tried.
  • Results differ by dataset and query type in all of these studies, so your own evaluation decides.

The "obvious" answer (semantic chunking, 20% overlap, 1000 tokens) is often wrong. This lesson builds intuition for six strategies and tells you when to reach for which.

The idea

Fixed chunking. Split every N characters or tokens. Simplest baseline. Breaks mid-sentence. Good compression, bad coherence.

Recursive. LangChain's RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter. Try splitting on \n\n first, then \n, then ., then space. Falls back cleanly. The 2026 default.

Semantic. Embed each sentence. Compute cosine similarity between adjacent sentences. Split where similarity drops below a threshold. Preserves topic coherence. Slower; sometimes produces tiny 40-token fragments that hurt retrieval.

Sentence. Split on sentence boundaries. One sentence per chunk or a window of N sentences. Matches semantic chunking up to ~5k tokens at a fraction of the cost.

Parent-document. Store small child chunks for retrieval and the larger parent chunk for context. Retrieve by child; return parent. Degrades gracefully: bad child chunks still return reasonable parents.

Late chunking (2024). Embed the whole document at the token level first, then pool token embeddings into chunk embeddings. Preserves cross-chunk context. Works with long-context embedders (BGE-M3, Jina v3). Higher compute.

Contextual retrieval (Anthropic, 2024). Prepend each chunk with an LLM-generated summary of its position in the document ("This chunk is section 3.2 of the termination clauses..."). In Anthropic's benchmark it reduced the top-20 retrieval failure rate by 35% with embeddings alone, 49% when combined with BM25, and 67% with reranking added. Expensive to index, though prompt caching cuts the cost.

The same idea as a formula

Why chunk size is a trade-off. A chunk C of n sentences gets one vector, roughly an average of what its sentences say, e(C)≈n1​∑s∈C​e(s) (mean pooling in many encoders does almost exactly this). If one sentence s∗ holds the answer, its share of the chunk vector is about 1/n, so the query's similarity to the chunk is diluted as n grows, while chunks that are too small cut s∗ off from the nearby sentences that name its subject ("He was born in 1879" needs the sentence that says who "he" is).

Recall and cost. For a question with answer a, retrieval at k is a hit if any of the top k chunks contains a: Recall@k=∣Q∣1​∑q​1[∃C∈topk​(q):aq​⊂C]. The context cost is ∑C∈topk​​∣C∣ tokens per question, so larger chunks buy recall with tokens. The useful comparison is recall at equal context budget, not recall at equal k.

Overlap. With chunk size L and overlap o, the stride is L−o, so the index has about L−oN​ chunks instead of LN​; a 20% overlap means 25% more chunks to embed and store. It helps only when answers straddle boundaries.

Semantic chunking places a boundary between sentences i−1 and i when cos(e(si−1​),e(si​))<τ, subject to minimum and maximum lengths.

Late chunking (Günther et al., 2024) runs the long-context encoder over the whole document first, then pools the contextualised token vectors of each chunk, e(C)=∣C∣1​∑t∈C​ht​(whole document), so "he" in a chunk already carries information about who "he" is.

Worked example
Recall per token, by hand

A 6,000-token document and a question whose answer sits in one sentence. Two setups, each returning its top 3 chunks:

  • 256-token chunks: the answer's chunk ranks 2nd: a hit. Context sent to the model: 3×256=768 tokens.
  • 1,024-token chunks: the answer's chunk ranks 1st: a hit. Context: 3×1024=3072 tokens.

Both are hits at k=3, but the large chunks cost four times the context. Now suppose over 100 questions the small chunks hit 80 times and the large ones 88 times. Per 1,000 context tokens, the small chunks get 80/76.8=1.04 hits and the large ones 88/307.2=0.29: 8 more hits for 4 times the cost. Whether that is worth it depends on what a missed answer costs; you only know if you measure both numbers.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Bigger chunks are safer because the answer is more likely to be inside."
    ✓ At fixed k they raise recall, but they cost more context and dilute the vector; compare at equal context budget.
  • ✗ "Semantic chunking is always better than fixed-size."
    ✓ Published comparisons find it rarely beats simple recursive or fixed-size chunking by enough to pay for its cost.
  • ✗ "Each chunk should stand on its own."
    ✓ Chunks lose the document's title, section and pronoun referents; contextual headers, parent documents and late chunking exist to put that back.

Idea 1 · The rule that beats every default

Match the chunk size to the query type:

Query type Chunk size
Factoid ("what is the CEO's name?") 256-512 tokens
Analytical / multi-hop 512-1024 tokens
Whole-section comprehension 1024-2048 tokens

From NVIDIA's 2025 chunking comparison, which also found page-level chunks a strong default for both. The chunk should be big enough to contain the answer plus local context, small enough that the retriever's top-K returns focus on the answer rather than context noise.

Step 1 · fixed and recursive chunking

def chunk_fixed(text, size=512, overlap=0):
    step = size - overlap
    return [text[i:i + size] for i in range(0, len(text), step)]


def chunk_recursive(text, size=512, seps=("\n\n", "\n", ". ", " ")):
    if len(text) <= size:
        return [text]
    for sep in seps:
        if sep not in text:
            continue
        parts = text.split(sep)
        chunks = []
        buf = ""
        for p in parts:
            if len(p) > size:
                if buf:
                    chunks.append(buf)
                    buf = ""
                chunks.extend(chunk_recursive(p, size=size, seps=seps[1:] or (" ",)))
                continue
            candidate = buf + sep + p if buf else p
            if len(candidate) <= size:
                buf = candidate
            else:
                if buf:
                    chunks.append(buf)
                buf = p
        if buf:
            chunks.append(buf)
        return [c for c in chunks if c.strip()]
    return chunk_fixed(text, size)

Step 2 · semantic chunking

def chunk_semantic(text, encoder, threshold=0.6, min_chars=200, max_chars=2048):
    sentences = split_sentences(text)
    if not sentences:
        return []
    embs = encoder.encode(sentences, normalize_embeddings=True)
    chunks = [[sentences[0]]]
    for i in range(1, len(sentences)):
        sim = float(embs[i] @ embs[i - 1])
        current_len = sum(len(s) for s in chunks[-1])
        if sim < threshold and current_len >= min_chars:
            chunks.append([sentences[i]])
        else:
            chunks[-1].append(sentences[i])

    result = []
    for group in chunks:
        text_group = " ".join(group)
        if len(text_group) > max_chars:
            result.extend(chunk_recursive(text_group, size=max_chars))
        else:
            result.append(text_group)
    return result

Tune threshold on your domain. Too high → fragments. Too low → one giant chunk.

Step 3 · parent-document

def chunk_parent_child(text, parent_size=2048, child_size=256):
    parents = chunk_recursive(text, size=parent_size)
    mapping = []
    for p_idx, parent in enumerate(parents):
        children = chunk_recursive(parent, size=child_size)
        for child in children:
            mapping.append({"child": child, "parent_idx": p_idx, "parent": parent})
    return mapping


def retrieve_parent(child_query, mapping, encoder, top_k=3):
    child_embs = encoder.encode([m["child"] for m in mapping], normalize_embeddings=True)
    q_emb = encoder.encode([child_query], normalize_embeddings=True)[0]
    scores = child_embs @ q_emb
    top = np.argsort(-scores)[:top_k]
    seen, parents = set(), []
    for i in top:
        if mapping[i]["parent_idx"] not in seen:
            parents.append(mapping[i]["parent"])
            seen.add(mapping[i]["parent_idx"])
    return parents

Key insight: dedupe parents. Multiple children can map to the same parent; returning all would waste context.

Break it on purpose

In chunk_fixed, call it with overlap greater than or equal to size, for example chunk_fixed(text, size=512, overlap=512). What happens, and what does that say about validating chunking parameters? (Solution B1.)

Step 4 · contextual retrieval (Anthropic pattern)

def contextualize_chunks(document, chunks, llm):
    context_prompts = [
        f"""<document>{document}</document>
Here is the chunk to situate: <chunk>{c}</chunk>
Write 50-100 words placing this chunk in the document's context."""
        for c in chunks
    ]
    contexts = llm.batch(context_prompts)
    return [f"{ctx}\n\n{c}" for ctx, c in zip(contexts, chunks)]

Index the contextualized chunks. At query time, retrieval benefits from the extra surrounding signal.

Step 5 · evaluate

def recall_at_k(queries, corpus_chunks, encoder, k=5):
    chunk_embs = encoder.encode(corpus_chunks, normalize_embeddings=True)
    hits = 0
    for q_text, gold_idxs in queries:
        q_emb = encoder.encode([q_text], normalize_embeddings=True)[0]
        top = np.argsort(-(chunk_embs @ q_emb))[:k]
        if any(i in gold_idxs for i in top):
            hits += 1
    return hits / len(queries)

Always benchmark. The "best" strategy for your corpus may not match any blog post.

import time
import numpy as np
import pyarrow.parquet as pq
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from collections import defaultdict
from huggingface_hub import hf_hub_download
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer

sq = pq.read_table(hf_hub_download("rajpurkar/squad_v2", "squad_v2/validation-00000-of-00001.parquet", repo_type="dataset")).to_pylist()
articles = defaultdict(list); questions = defaultdict(list)
for r in sq:
    if r["context"] not in articles[r["title"]]:
        articles[r["title"]].append(r["context"])
    if r["answers"]["text"]:
        questions[r["title"]].append((r["question"], r["answers"]["text"][0]))
titles = [t for t in articles][:8]
docs = {t: "\n\n".join(articles[t]) for t in titles}
print(f"{len(titles)} articles, {sum(len(d) for d in docs.values()):,} characters, {sum(len(questions[t]) for t in titles)} questions")
enc = SentenceTransformer("sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2")

def sentence_windows(text, n=3):
    s = split_sentences(text)
    return [" ".join(s[i:i + n]) for i in range(0, len(s), n)]
strategies = {"fixed 512": lambda t: chunk_fixed(t, 512), "fixed 512, 20% overlap": lambda t: chunk_fixed(t, 512, overlap=102),
              "recursive 512": lambda t: chunk_recursive(t, 512), "3-sentence windows": sentence_windows,
              "semantic (0.4)": lambda t: chunk_semantic(t, enc, threshold=0.4, min_chars=200, max_chars=1500),
              "paragraphs": lambda t: t.split("\n\n")}
for size in (256, 1024, 2048):
    strategies[f"recursive {size}"] = (lambda s: lambda t: chunk_recursive(t, s))(size)

def evaluate(chunker, header=False, ks=(1, 3)):
    hits = {k: 0 for k in ks}; n = 0; ctx = 0; nchunks = 0
    for t in titles:
        chunks = chunker(docs[t]); nchunks += len(chunks)
        E = enc.encode([f"Article: {t.replace('_', ' ')}. " + c if header else c for c in chunks], normalize_embeddings=True, batch_size=64)
        Qe = enc.encode([q for q, _ in questions[t]], normalize_embeddings=True, batch_size=64)
        for (q, a), qv in zip(questions[t], Qe):
            order = np.argsort(-(E @ qv)); n += 1
            for k in ks:
                hits[k] += any(a in chunks[i] for i in order[:k])
            ctx += sum(len(chunks[i]) for i in order[:3])
    return {k: hits[k] / n for k in ks}, ctx / n / 4, nchunks

res = {}
for name, fn in strategies.items():

rec, ctx, _ = evaluate(strategies["recursive 512"], header=True)

print(f"({time.time() - start:.0f} s)")

Reading this output

Recursive splitting at 512 characters beats fixed 512-character slices clearly (recall@3 0.88 against 0.80) while sending less context, because it cuts at paragraph and sentence boundaries instead of mid-sentence. The article's own paragraphs, natural units written by people, give the best recall@1 (0.75) at about 500 tokens. Size behaves as the formula box predicts: recursive 256 is cheap but loses context (0.83), 1,024 is about as good as paragraphs, and 2,048 is worse than 512 (0.81) while costing four to five times the context, the dilution effect. Overlap buys a few points (0.80 to 0.84) for 25% more chunks. Semantic chunking is no better than plain three-sentence windows, in line with Vectara's finding. The title header changes nothing here, and that is instructive: every chunk in this test competes only with chunks of the same article, so a header shared by all of them cannot help tell them apart (Solution P1).

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Chunk with context, not just text. Anthropic's contextual retrieval (September 2024) prepends an LLM-written situating sentence to each chunk, and late chunking (Jina, 2024) embeds the whole document before pooling chunks; both put back the context a chunk loses when it is cut out, with a header specific to each chunk rather than one shared by the whole document.
  • Page-level and layout-aware chunking. For PDFs, NVIDIA's 2025 comparison found page-level chunks a robust default, and document-parsing tools now chunk by detected structure (headings, tables, figures) rather than character counts; vision-based retrieval over page images (ColPali, Lesson 5.22) skips text chunking entirely.
  • Long context changes the budget, not the logic. With large context windows, many systems retrieve fewer, larger chunks or whole sections, but cost still grows with every token and models still miss facts in long inputs, so recall per token remains the measure.

Sources: Contextual Retrieval, Is Semantic Chunking Worth the Computational Cost?, NVIDIA chunking study, Late Chunking.

Pitfalls

  • Chunking evaluated only on factoid queries. Multi-hop queries reveal very different winners. Use a query-type-stratified eval set.
  • Semantic chunking without a minimum size. Produces 40-token fragments that hurt retrieval. Always enforce min_tokens.
  • Overlap as cargo cult. Evidence on overlap is mixed: some studies find no benefit, NVIDIA's found a small one at 15%, and overlap always enlarges the index. Measure, do not assume.
  • No min/max enforcement. Chunks of 5 tokens or 5000 tokens both break retrieval. Clamp.
  • Cross-doc chunking. Never let a chunk span two documents. Always chunk per-doc, then merge.

The 2026 stack:

Situation Strategy
First build, unknown corpus Recursive, 512 tokens, no overlap

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • One vector per chunk forces a trade-off: small chunks lose context, large ones dilute the answer and cost tokens.
  • Compare strategies by recall at equal context budget, measured on your own questions.
  • Recursive splitting at natural boundaries is a strong default; semantic chunking rarely pays for its cost.
  • Overlap enlarges the index; use it only if measurements show answers straddling boundaries.
  • Put context back: titles and headings in chunks, parent documents, late chunking or contextual retrieval.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Not at all, in this test

Recall barely moves (0.70 to 0.69 at 1, 0.88 to 0.89 at 3). Each question here is searched only among chunks of its own article, and every one of those chunks gets the same header, so the header shifts all their vectors alike and cannot change which one ranks first. A title header helps when chunks from many documents compete in one index, where it tells the retriever which article a chunk belongs to. That is the setting of contextual retrieval, which also writes a different, chunk-specific header for each chunk ("this section describes the 1066 invasion"), which is what can separate chunks within a document.

SolutionB1 · An infinite loop or an error

The stride is size - overlap. With overlap equal to size the stride is 0, and range(0, len(text), 0) raises ValueError: range() arg 3 must not be zero; with overlap larger than size the stride is negative, the range is empty, and the function silently returns no chunks at all, so the document vanishes from the index without an error. Validate parameters (0 <= overlap < size) and assert that the chunks, joined back, cover the whole document.

Questions from real AI engineering interviews that this lesson prepares you for. Answer out loud first, then open the worked answer.

Weigh a trade-offA team is about to index a new document corpus and asks you to review the chunker. They picked semantic splitting, 1000 tokens per chunk and 20 percent overlap, because a blog post recommended it. What do you tell them?

Level 2 of 4 · about 10 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Start from the query type, not from a post. Factoid questions want 256 to 512 tokens, analytical or multi-hop questions 512 to 1024, and whole-section questions 1024 to 2048. Begin with recursive splitting at 512 tokens and no overlap, then measure recall at 5 on a 50-query set before you move any knob.

Why size follows the question.

A chunk must be large enough to hold the answer plus its local context, and small enough that the top k returns the answer and not noise. Too small, at 64 to 128 tokens, and a chunk says "it increased 15% last quarter" with no subject. Too large, at 2048 tokens and more, and one chunk is 10% about revenue and 90% about headcount, so its embedding points nowhere.

Query type Chunk size
Factoid 256 to 512 tokens
Analytical or multi-hop 512 to 1024 tokens
Whole-section comprehension 1024 to 2048 tokens

What the measurements say.

  • Recursive splitting at 512 tokens beat semantic splitting 69% to 54% in the chunking study.
  • Overlap gave zero measurable benefit in the sparse retrieval study, and every overlapped token is embedded and stored again, so the index cost grows with the overlap.
  • Response quality drops sharply around 2,500 tokens of context, so bigger chunks hit a cliff.
  • Semantic splitting without a minimum size produces 40-token fragments that hurt retrieval.

Pick a default without a benchmark.

Three properties decide the first chunker: the document type, the paragraph length, and whether the format carries structure. Prose with no structure gets recursive splitting. Markdown and API docs get the header splitter. Code gets a syntax-aware splitter. When in doubt, recursive splitting is the strongest single baseline.

Then measure.

Build a 50-query eval set, stratified by query type, and read recall at 5 for each candidate. Clamp every chunker with a minimum and a maximum size. Never let a chunk span two documents.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Semantic splitting is best because it follows meaning." It lost to recursive splitting at 512 tokens in the measured study, and it makes tiny fragments unless you set a floor.
  • "Overlap is a safety margin, keep it." The studies found that overlap often adds nothing, and it adds index cost in proportion to its size; justify any overlap with an ablation.
  • "Bigger chunks give the model more context." Quality drops around 2,500 tokens of context, and a chunk that covers many topics dilutes its own embedding.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why does the chunk size depend on the kind of question? Expected: the chunk has to contain the answer with its context, and still be small enough that the top k is about the answer and not about the surrounding noise.
  • (level 2) The corpus is markdown API documentation. What do you try first? Expected: the structural header splitter, because the format carries explicit structure; drop or merge header-only chunks.
  • (level 3) Which two guards does every chunker need, whatever the strategy? Expected: a minimum and a maximum size, because chunks of 5 tokens or 5000 tokens both break retrieval.
  • (level 3) You switch the embedding model later. What do you run again? Expected: the chunking eval, because the winner depends on the corpus and the model, and semantic splitting moves its boundaries when the model changes.

Where it is taught. The lesson chunking strategies gives the size table and the measurements, the lesson RAG explains the too-small and too-large failures, and the lesson chunking comparison picks a default from three properties.

Defend a designYour RAG eval reports high recall, high MRR, high faithfulness and high answer relevance, and the weekly drift dashboard is flat. Support still forwards a stream of wrong answers from one business unit. As the staff engineer on the system, what do you conclude, and what do you change in the eval program itself?

Level 4 of 4 · about 15 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Conclude that the eval set does not represent that unit's traffic: all four metrics can be high on queries that nobody asks. Add their real queries to the qrels, slice every metric by query class and document type, and check whether their documents are chunked by a splitter that fits their format. Then change the program: tier the qrels, review them on a schedule so they do not rot, and treat a per-slice drop as a release blocker.

The row in the table.

The eval lesson ends its diagnosis table with this exact pattern: all four metrics high, users still complain, likely cause an unrepresentative eval set, fix by expanding the qrels with real user queries. A dashboard that tracks the same set every week stays flat for the same reason.

Why averages hide it.

A 90 percent average recall can hide one query class that always misses. The lesson's rule is to slice the qrels by class, such as literal, paraphrased and multi-topic, and report each slice. The chunking lesson adds the other axis: a query-type-stratified set, because multi-hop queries reveal different winners than factoid queries.

The changes to the program.

  1. Expand the qrels with that unit's real queries, labeled by a domain expert with gold ids and answers, as the production capstone does for its golden set of 200 pairs with citations.
  2. Tag every qrel with query class and document type, and report every metric per slice.
  3. Persist the per-query trace per run, so that when a complaint arrives you can look up the matching qrel and see whether the eval would have caught it.
  4. Schedule a qrels review. Gold answers drift as the corpus changes; the lesson schedules a quarterly review.
  5. Lock the chunker in the qrels header, and check that the splitter in production fits that unit's document format.
  6. Tier the sets: 20 queries in CI, 200 nightly, 2000 weekly, and make a per-slice drop a release blocker.

What the dashboard should have shown.

The capstone's drift dashboard tracks retrieval quality weekly and alerts on a 5 percent drop. It can only alert on the traffic it contains. Once the unit's queries are in the set, the same alert starts to mean something for them.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The metrics are high, so the complaints are user error." The metrics grade the eval set, not the traffic; the complaints are the missing data.
  • "Raise the thresholds." Tighter thresholds on the wrong queries still measure the wrong queries.
  • "Replace the eval with a bigger public benchmark." A public set has nothing from that unit; the fix is their real queries, labeled.

  • (level 3) Why can every metric be high while the answers are wrong? Expected: the metrics are computed on the eval set, and that set lacks the queries and documents where the failures happen.
  • (level 4) What is the first concrete change to the qrels? Expected: add that unit's real queries with expert-labeled gold ids and answers, tagged with query class and document type for slicing.
  • (level 4) How do you keep the qrels honest over time? Expected: a scheduled review, quarterly in the lesson, because gold answers drift as the corpus changes, plus a check that the chunker in the qrels header is the one in production.
  • (level 4) Why was the drift dashboard flat while quality fell? Expected: it tracks the same unrepresentative set every week; an alert on that set says nothing about traffic it does not contain.

Weigh a trade-offYour chunker keeps a 100-token overlap between neighbor chunks. After the last corpus refresh the index size and the embedding bill both went up, and a teammate says the overlap must stay because it protects sentences that a boundary would cut in half. How do you settle it?

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Settle it with an ablation, not an opinion. Run the same recall at 5 eval with the overlap at zero and at the current value on a stratified query set, and keep the overlap only if the number moves. The recent studies found that overlap often adds no measurable benefit, while it adds index cost in proportion to its size.

Where overlap came from.

The fixed-window baseline cuts every N tokens with no regard for sentences. The RAG lesson shows the classic layout: 512-token chunks with a 50-token overlap, so chunk 1 holds tokens 0 to 511 and chunk 2 holds tokens 462 to 973. The repeated tail makes sure a sentence cut at an unlucky point appears whole somewhere.

Why it matters less now.

A recursive splitter tries the paragraph break first, then the line break, then the sentence end, then the space. Its boundaries fall at natural breaks, so the problem that overlap solved mostly disappears. The chunking lesson lists overlap under cargo cult: the 2026 studies found zero measurable benefit in the measured case, and every overlapped token is embedded and stored twice.

The test.

  1. Keep the chunker, the embedder and the query set fixed.
  2. Run recursive splitting at 512 tokens with overlap zero and with the current overlap.
  3. Compare recall at 5 per query slice, and compare chunk counts.
  4. Keep the overlap only where a slice shows a real gain, and record the ablation next to the setting.

The chunker skill in the lesson sets overlap to zero by default and refuses an overlap above 20 percent without an ablation that shows it helps.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Overlap is free, embeddings are cheap." Every overlapped token is embedded and stored twice, so the index cost grows in proportion to the overlap.
  • "A blog post says 20 percent overlap." The studies found no measurable benefit in the measured case; the answer depends on your corpus, so measure it there.
  • "Remove it everywhere, no test needed." The ablation is cheap and the result is corpus dependent; a slice with real boundary losses would show up in it.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why did the fixed-window splitter need overlap? Expected: it cuts at a fixed count whatever the text, so a boundary can split a sentence; the repeated tail puts that sentence whole into the next chunk.
  • (level 2) What does a recursive splitter do instead? Expected: it splits on the paragraph, the line and the sentence separators in turn, so boundaries land on natural breaks.
  • (level 3) What rule does the chunker skill apply to overlap? Expected: default zero, and refuse any overlap above 20 percent without an ablation.
  • (level 3) The ablation shows a gain on one document type only. What do you ship? Expected: overlap for that type alone, with the ablation recorded, and a re-run when the corpus or the embedder changes.

Where it is taught. The lesson chunking strategies reports the overlap studies and sets the skill rule, and the lesson RAG shows the fixed-window layout that overlap was built for.

Read the codeRead this retrieval function from the chunking lesson. What problem does the two-level layout solve, what does the seen set do, and how many parents can the function return? python def retrieve_parent(child_query, mapping, encoder, top_k=3): child_embs = encoder.encode([m["child"] for m in mapping], normalize_embeddings=True) q_emb = encoder.encode([child_query], normalize_embeddings=True)[0] scores = child_embs @ q_emb top = np.argsort(-scores)[:top_k] seen, parents = set(), [] for i in top: if mapping[i]["parent_idx"] not in seen: parents.append(mapping[i]["parent"]) seen.add(mapping[i]["parent_idx"]) return parents

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Small child chunks are indexed for a precise match and the larger parent is returned for context, which removes the trade-off between a precise hit and enough surrounding text. The seen set drops a parent that several top children map to, so the prompt does not receive the same text twice. For a mapping that is not empty and a positive top_k, the function returns between one and top_k parents, fewer when children share a parent; an empty mapping or a top_k of zero returns none.

The layout.

Standard chunking forces a choice: small chunks match precisely but lack context, large chunks carry context but cover many topics. The two-level layout indexes small children and stores the parent each child came from. The chunking lesson splits parents at 2048 and children at 256 by default, and the advanced RAG lesson draws a 512-token parent with four 128-token children. A query about one detail matches one child precisely, and the prompt receives the whole parent, including the lines around the detail.

Walk the function.

  1. Encode every child and the query with normalization on, so the matrix product equals the cosine.
  2. Sort children by score and keep the top k indices.
  3. For each top child, look up its parent index; if that parent was not seen, append the parent text and mark it.
  4. Return the parents in the order of their best child.

Why de-duplicate.

Several children of one parent often match the same query, because they sit in the same section. Without the set, the prompt would receive the same parent twice or three times and waste its context budget. The lesson calls this the key insight of the pattern.

What production changes.

The function re-encodes every child on each call. A real index stores the child vectors once and only embeds the query. The two-level layout also degrades gracefully: a poor child match still returns a reasonable parent.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Return the parent of every top child, duplicates included." Several children map to one parent, and the repeated text wastes the context budget.
  • "Make the child as large as the parent." Then there is no precision gain; the point is a small match unit and a large context unit.
  • "The function always returns exactly top_k parents." It returns fewer when children share a parent.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why index the child and not the parent? Expected: a small chunk matches a specific question precisely, while a large chunk covers many topics and its embedding points nowhere in particular.
  • (level 2) Why a plain matrix product and no cosine? Expected: the vectors are normalized at encode time, so the dot product equals the cosine.
  • (level 3) What does this function do on every call that production code must not? Expected: it re-encodes all the children; store the child vectors once at index time and embed only the query.
  • (level 3) Two parents each have one matching child, and a third parent has three matching children below the cut. What does the function miss? Expected: it ranks by the best child only; a parent with several good children gets no extra credit unless you aggregate child scores per parent.

Where it is taught. The lesson chunking strategies gives this function and the de-duplication insight, and the lesson advanced RAG draws the parent and child layout.

Take-homeTake-home. You receive a corpus of a few hundred internal documents and a retrieval pipeline with two chunkers and two retrievers behind flags. Build the evaluation that tells the team which configuration to ship, and write up what the numbers mean. You have one evening.

Level 3 of 4 · about 60 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Build a labeled query set first, stratified by query type, with gold document ids and gold answer text for each query. Run every configuration against the same set and report recall at 5 for each slice, plus MRR and the share of queries whose top 5 holds a chunk with the gold answer text, and read the spread between configurations. Label at document level so the same labels grade every chunker; if you label at chunk level, record the chunker in the qrels and relabel after any change of chunker.

The deliverable.

  1. A qrels file. One record per query with the query text, the gold document ids, a graded relevance map and the gold answer substring. Label at document level, not at chunk level, so the labels survive a chunker change.
  2. A stratified set. The chunking lesson uses a 50-query set split across factoid, analytical and multi-hop questions. Multi-hop queries reveal different winners than factoid queries, and one average hides the losing class.
  3. The run matrix. Two chunkers times two retrievers, every cell against the same qrels, same k values.
  4. The metrics. Recall at 5 as the primary number, precision at k beside it, MRR for the position of the first hit, nDCG if the labels are graded, and an answer hit at 5 that counts a query only when a top-five chunk contains the gold answer substring. Document-level recall cannot tell two chunkers apart when both return chunks of the gold document and only one of them returns the chunk with the answer. The answer hit measures exact-span coverage only: a multi-hop answer spread across several chunks can miss it while the top five still supports the answer, so read it beside recall and not as a full retrieval score.
  5. The report. One table with one row per configuration and one column per slice, then a recommendation and the reason, then what you would measure next.

What to expect.

The chunking comparison lesson shows the pattern: the structural header splitter wins on markdown, semantic clustering wins on plain prose, and recursive splitting holds its own on a mixed corpus. The hybrid retriever beats the lexical baseline on recall. Your corpus decides which of these holds.

Guards that graders look for.

  • The labeling level is recorded in the qrels header, and chunk-level gold ids name the chunker they were produced against.
  • Any training pairs for a reranker are disjoint from the eval queries.
  • The per-query results are persisted, not only the averages.
  • The write-up names what this eval cannot tell: answer quality, which needs faithfulness and relevance judged by a model from another family or by hand.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "I wrote ten questions myself and ran each configuration." Ten questions of one type cannot separate four configurations or expose a class that always misses.
  • "Here is the average recall for each configuration." One average hides per-slice winners and the class that fails.
  • "I changed the chunker and reused the chunk-level labels." Chunk ids no longer line up with the new chunks; label at document level, or relabel and record the chunker in the qrels header.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) Why split the queries by type? Expected: factoid and multi-hop queries reveal different winners, and one average hides the class that loses.
  • (level 3) The labels were produced with chunker A. Can you reuse them for chunker B? Expected: only if the labels are at document or span level; chunk ids change with the boundaries, so record the chunker in the qrels and relabel otherwise.
  • (level 4) How do you justify the query count in the write-up? Expected: a 50-query stratified set for the first decision, then tiers of 20 queries in CI, 200 nightly and 2000 weekly, with a note on what each tier can and cannot detect.
  • (level 4) What do you refuse to conclude after one evening? Expected: anything about answer quality, because the eval grades retrieval; faithfulness and relevance need a judge, and a judge from the generator's own family is biased.
