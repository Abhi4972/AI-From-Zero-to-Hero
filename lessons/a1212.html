In plain words first
The idea in one line

BAAI's Emu3 (Wang et al., September 2024) is the 2024 result that should have ended the diffusion-versus-autoregressive debate. A single Llama-style decoder-only transformer, trained only on the next-token-prediction objective, across a unified vocabulary of text + VQ image tokens + 3D VQ video tokens, matches or beats SDXL on most image-generation benchmarks and trades wins with LLaVA-1.6 on perception. No CLIP loss. No diffusion schedule. Classifier-free guidance is used at inference for quality, but the core training objective is next-token prediction with teacher forcin

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
Next-token prediction "NTP" Standard autoregressive loss: predict token[i+1] given token[0..i]; works for every modality when tokenized
SBER-MoVQGAN "The tokenizer base" A VQGAN-family image tokenizer; Emu3 extends it with 3D convolutions for video and a 32,768-entry codebook
3D VQ "Spatiotemporal quantizer" Codebook indexed by (time, row, col); one token covers 4 frames x 8x8 pixels
Classifier-free guidance "CFG" Mix conditional and unconditional logits with weight gamma; boosts image quality at inference
Unified vocabulary "Shared tokens" Text + image + video all draw from the same integer space; model predicts whichever modality comes next
GenEval "Image gen benchmark" Object-focused prompts checked by a detector (counts, colours, positions); Emu3 0.54, SDXL 0.55

The problem

In plain words first

Chameleon showed that images can be tokens in a language model's vocabulary. Emu3 (BAAI, September 2024) pushes the idea to its limit: one 8B transformer, trained with nothing but next-token prediction, that writes text, generates images and generates short videos. There is no diffusion model and no separate image encoder for understanding. Its quality comes from a stronger image tokenizer: 4,096 codes from a 32,768-entry codebook for a 512×512 image, and a 3D version for video. It also borrows one trick from diffusion: classifier-free guidance, which runs the model with and without the prompt and exaggerates the difference. This lesson covers the tokenizer, the single loss, guidance and the honest benchmark picture. It runs the real Emu3 tokenizer code (random weights) and classifier-free guidance on a small local model. No model is trained.

The conventional wisdom through 2024: image generation needs diffusion. The argument: discrete image tokens lose too much information to reconstruct detail, and autoregressive sampling accumulates error across thousands of tokens. Stable Diffusion, DALL-E 3, Imagen, Midjourney all use some form of diffusion. Chameleon (Lesson 12.11) partially disproved this at small scale but did not match SDXL on quality.

Emu3 attacked the argument head-on. The claim: better visual tokenizer + enough scale + next-token loss = diffusion-beating image generation in the same model that also does perception.

The bet was controversial when it published. Two years on, the open-source unified-generation family (Emu3, Show-o, Janus-Pro, Transfusion) is the default path for research; production frontier models appear to use some variant.

Idea 1 · The Emu3 tokenizer

The key ingredient is the visual tokenizer. Emu3 trains its own tokenizer on top of SBER-MoVQGAN (270M parameters), adding 3D convolutions for video. It compresses 8×8 in space and 4× in time: a 512x512 image becomes 64x64 = 4096 tokens from a codebook of 32,768.

This is larger than Chameleon's 1024 tokens per 512x512 at K=8192 on both axes: four times the tokens and a codebook four times as large. The extra capacity buys reconstruction quality and finer detail, at four times the sequence length.

For video: the same tokenizer encodes a spatiotemporal block (4 frames x 8x8 pixels) to one integer, so a 4×512×512 clip is also 4,096 tokens. A 4 s clip at 8 FPS has 32 frames; at 256x256 the token count is (256/8) * (256/8) * (32/4) = 32 * 32 * 8 = 8,192 tokens. Emu3 natively generates 5-second clips at 24 FPS, and its video training stage used a context of 131,072 tokens.

Tokenizer quality is the ceiling. Emu3's contribution is partly "we trained a very good tokenizer."

Idea 2 · Single-loss training

Emu3 uses one objective: next-token prediction on a shared vocabulary across text tokens, image tokens and video tokens. The loss function is identical for every token; the data mixture decides what the model learns to produce.

Train on a mix of: - Image gen: <text caption> <image> image_tokens </image> - Image perception: <image> image_tokens </image> <question> text_tokens - Video gen: <text caption> <video> video_tokens </video> - Video perception: analogous. - Text only: standard NTP.

The model learns when to emit image tokens vs text tokens from the data distribution. Generation emerges from the model predicting image tokens after the <image> tag.

Idea 3 · Classifier-free guidance and temperature

Autoregressive image generation gets much better with classifier-free guidance (CFG) at inference. Emu3 uses it: run the model with the prompt and with a negative or empty prompt, and push the logits away from the unconditional ones by a guidance weight. The paper evaluates with a scale of 5.0 to 5.5; the released Emu3-Gen example uses 3.0. This is the same CFG trick diffusion uses, borrowed to the autoregressive setting (measured below on a small text model).

Sampling matters: the released example samples with top-k 2048 rather than greedy decoding, since greedy image tokens tend to give flat, repetitive images.

The same idea as a formula

Tokens per image or clip with 8×8 spatial and 4× temporal compression:

n=⌈4F​⌉⋅8H​⋅8W​(F=1 for an image).

Classifier-free guidance on logits. With logits ℓc​ given the prompt and ℓu​ without it, and guidance weight w:

ℓw​=ℓu​+w(ℓc​−ℓu​),pw​=softmax(ℓw​).
w=0 ignores the prompt, w=1 is ordinary conditional sampling, and w>1 exaggerates whatever the prompt changes.

Cost. Each generated token needs both ℓc​ and ℓu​: two forward passes (or a batch of two) per token, so a 4,096-token image costs about 8,192 token evaluations.

Worked example
One 512×512 image from Emu3
  • Tokens: (512/8)2=4,096 image tokens, each one of 32,768 codes (log2​32768=15 bits): 61,440 bits per image, about 100× fewer than raw 24-bit pixels.
  • Decoding: 4,096 sequential steps. At 30 tokens per second on one GPU, about 2.3 minutes, against seconds for a few-step diffusion model.
  • With guidance, each step evaluates the model twice, so the batch is doubled; the wall-clock time stays similar if the GPU has spare capacity.
  • Five seconds of 512×512 video at 24 FPS: 120/4=30 temporal steps × 4,096 = 122,880 tokens, which is why the video stage needed a 131,072-token context.

Emu3's tokenizer, encode and decode

The transformers implementation of Emu3's vision tokenizer with its default configuration (the released tokenizer's shape: 271M parameters, 32,768 codes), random weights. One Imagenette photo, resized to 256 px to keep the CPU run short, is encoded to codes and decoded back to pixels. The run then counts tokens for images and videos at the real sizes. The codes are meaningless with random weights; shapes and costs are real. About a minute on CPU.

import time, numpy as np, torch
from datasets import load_dataset
from transformers import Emu3Config, logging
from transformers.models.emu3.modeling_emu3 import Emu3VQVAE
logging.set_verbosity_error()
torch.manual_seed(0)

# Emu3's vision tokenizer: the transformers implementation with its default configuration, the released tokenizer's
# shape (SBER-MoVQGAN-style, 8x8 spatial and 4x temporal compression, 32,768 codes). Random weights: real shapes
# and code path, meaningless codes. A 256-px image keeps the CPU run short (512 px would give 64x64 = 4,096 codes).
cfg = Emu3Config()
vq = Emu3VQVAE(cfg.vq_config).eval()
print(f"tokenizer: {sum(p.numel() for p in vq.parameters()) / 1e6:.0f}M parameters, codebook {cfg.vq_config.codebook_size:,}, "
      f"temporal compression {cfg.vq_config.temporal_downsample_factor}x")

photo = load_dataset("Multimodal-Fatima/Imagenette_validation", split="validation")[3]["image"].convert("RGB").resize((256, 256))
x = torch.from_numpy(np.asarray(photo, dtype=np.float32)).permute(2, 0, 1)[None] / 127.5 - 1   # pixels in [-1, 1]
with torch.no_grad():
    t = time.time()
    out = vq.encode(x, torch.tensor([[256, 256]]))
    codes = out.image_tokens[0]
    enc = time.time() - t
    t = time.time()
    rec = vq.decode(codes[None])
    dec = time.time() - t
print(f"256x256 photo -> {tuple(codes.shape)} grid = {codes.numel():,} codes in {enc:.0f} s; "
      f"decoded back to {tuple(rec.shape)} in {dec:.0f} s")
print(f"codes are integers in [0, {cfg.vq_config.codebook_size - 1:,}]: min {codes.min().item()}, max {codes.max().item()}; "
      f"{codes.unique().numel()} distinct")

for side, frames in [(512, 1), (512, 4), (720, 1)]:
    t_steps = max(1, frames // cfg.vq_config.temporal_downsample_factor)
    n = t_steps * (side // 8) ** 2
    print(f"{frames} frame(s) at {side}x{side} -> {t_steps} x {side // 8} x {side // 8} = {n:,} tokens")
print(f"5 s of 512x512 video at 24 fps: 120 frames -> {120 // 4} x 64 x 64 = {30 * 64 * 64:,} tokens")

Reading this output

The tokenizer is the size of a small model. 271M parameters, matching the paper's 270M SBER-MoVQGAN base. That is part of every generated image's cost: the decoder must run once to draw the pixels.

8 × 8 pixels per code. The 256 × 256 photo becomes a 32 × 32 grid of 1,024 codes. At 512 × 512 it would be 64 × 64 = 4,096, four times Chameleon's count. Every code is an integer below 32,768, the codebook size, and the decoder turns the grid back into a full-size image. With random weights the reconstruction is noise; the round trip itself is what is being checked.

It is slow on a CPU. Encoding took about 18 s and decoding about 40 s for one small image. On a GPU this is milliseconds, but it shows that the tokenizer is a real network, not a lookup.

Video shares the budget. Four frames compress into one temporal step, so one 512 × 512 image and a 4-frame 512 × 512 clip both cost 4,096 tokens. A 720 × 720 image costs 8,100. Five seconds of 512 × 512 video at 24 fps is 122,880 tokens, more than a long book. That is why Emu3's video stage needed a context of over 131,000 tokens.

Classifier-free guidance, measured

Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct (a text model, standing in for Emu3's decoder) predicts the next token after "the main colour of the car is", once with a caption and once with an empty caption. The logits are mixed with guidance weights from 0 to 10, and the run reports the probabilities of three colours, the entropy and the top tokens. About 30 seconds.

import torch
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer, logging
logging.set_verbosity_error()
# Classifier-free guidance, the trick Emu3 borrows from diffusion, on a small text model as a stand-in:
# run the model with and without the caption and push the logits away from the unconditional ones.
name = "Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct"
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(name)
lm = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(name, dtype=torch.float32).eval()
cond = "Caption: a small red sports car parked by the sea.\nPainting notes: the main colour of the car is"
uncond = "Caption:\nPainting notes: the main colour of the car is"
with torch.no_grad():
    lc = lm(tok(cond, return_tensors="pt").input_ids).logits[0, -1]
    lu = lm(tok(uncond, return_tensors="pt").input_ids).logits[0, -1]
colours = [" red", " blue", " white", " black", " yellow", " green"]
ids = [tok(c).input_ids[0] for c in colours]
print(f"{'guidance w':>11}{'P(red)':>9}{'P(blue)':>9}{'P(white)':>10}{'entropy (nats)':>16}   top 3 next tokens")
for w in [0.0, 1.0, 3.0, 5.0, 10.0]:
    logits = lu + w * (lc - lu)                         # w = 0: no caption, w = 1: plain conditional
    p = logits.softmax(-1)
    ent = -(p * p.clamp_min(1e-12).log()).sum()
    top = [tok.decode([i]) for i in p.topk(3).indices]
    print(f"{w:>11.1f}{p[ids[0]]:>9.3f}{p[ids[1]]:>9.3f}{p[ids[2]]:>10.3f}{ent:>16.2f}   {top}")
print("\ncost: every generated token needs two forward passes (with and without the caption), "
      "or one pass over a batch of two")

Reading this output

Without the caption (w = 0), the model guesses. "red" gets 5.5%, behind "a" and "black", and the distribution is wide (entropy 4.84 nats).

Plain conditioning (w = 1) helps but stays loose. "red" rises to 38% and becomes the top token, but much probability is still spread over continuations like "a" and "dark".

Moderate guidance (w = 3 to 5) makes the prompt decisive. "red" reaches 93% and then 95%, and the entropy falls below 0.5. This is why autoregressive image models use guidance: each image token sticks closely to what the caption implies, and the image follows the prompt.

Too much guidance distorts. At w = 10, "red" falls back to 83%, and "sea" and "blood" climb into the top three. Extreme guidance amplifies every difference between the two contexts, including irrelevant ones such as the word "sea" in the caption. In images this shows up as oversaturated colours and artefacts. The paper's scales of 5.0–5.5 and the release's 3.0 sit in the useful range for Emu3.

The cost is two passes. Every token needs the conditional and the unconditional logits.

Idea 4 · Three roles, one model

Emu3 is released as several checkpoints that share one architecture and one pretraining run:

  • Emu3-Stage1. The pretrained model: captions images and generates 512x512 images.
  • Emu3-Gen. Post-trained for image and video generation (quality fine-tuning and DPO).
  • Emu3-Chat. Post-trained for vision-language understanding.
  • Emu3-VisionTokenizer. Encodes and decodes images and video frames.

No task-specific heads: the architecture is the same, and only the post-training data differs.

Idea 5 · Benchmarks

From Emu3 paper (September 2024):

  • Image generation: GenEval overall 0.54 against SDXL's 0.55 (0.66 with a prompt rewriter); DPG-Bench 80.6 against 74.65 (81.6 after DPO); ahead of SDXL on T2I-CompBench colour, shape and texture when prompts are rewritten.
  • Image perception: trails LLaVA-1.6 on VQAv2 (75.1 vs 81.8) and MMMU (31.6 vs 35.1), but beats it on SEED-Bench, OCRBench, and ChartQA.
  • Video generation: 5-second clips at 24 FPS, compared in the paper against video diffusion models on VBench.

The numbers are not always winning — Emu3 trades a point here for a point there — but the claim "next-token prediction is all you need" is defensible across modalities.

Idea 6 · Compute cost

Emu3 is an 8B-parameter model. The paper does not publish a GPU-hour total; for scale, Llama-2-7B pretraining used about 184k A100 GPU-hours, roughly 21 GPU-years. Diffusion models like Stable Diffusion 3 train in similar budgets but need separate text encoders and more complex pipelines.

At inference, Emu3 is slower than SDXL per image: 4096 image tokens at 30 tok/s is ~2 minutes per 512x512 image, vs 2-5 seconds for SDXL. Speculative decoding and KV-cache optimization narrow the gap but do not close it. Autoregressive image gen is compute-heavy; this is the standing trade-off.

Idea 7 · Why it matters

Emu3's deep contribution is conceptual. If next-token prediction scales to match diffusion on image generation, the unified-model path (one loss, one backbone, any modality) is viable. Future models do not need separate text encoders, separate diffusion schedulers, separate VAEs. One transformer, one tokenizer per modality, scale.

Show-o, Janus-Pro, and InternVL-U all build on or challenge this thesis, several of them by adding diffusion back for image quality (Lessons 12.13–12.15).

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Autoregressive image generation keeps improving: Janus-Pro (Lesson 12.15), Lumina-mGPT, Infinity and next-scale methods (VAR) cut the token count or the number of sequential steps.
  • Emu3.5 (BAAI, 2025) extends the line to world modelling over long interleaved vision-language sequences, with a faster parallel decoding scheme for images.
  • Hybrids dominate quality leaderboards: transformers that predict text autoregressively and images with diffusion or flow matching (Transfusion, Lesson 12.13; BAGEL; InternVL-U).
  • Laptop reality: Emu3's 8B model needs about 16 GB in bf16 and minutes per image; on an 8 GB GPU it needs 4-bit quantization and is slow. Small diffusion models remain the practical local choice for image generation.

Sources: Emu3 (arXiv 2409.18869), Emu3 GitHub, Classifier-Free Diffusion Guidance (arXiv 2207.12598), Hugging Face Emu3 documentation.

Given a generation product spec (image or video, target resolution, quality tier, throughput requirement), compute token counts for Emu3-style next-token generation, estimate inference cost, and pick between Emu3-family and diffusion.

Produce:

1. Token count. Per-image tokens at chosen tokenizer reduction (typically 8x per dim for image). Per-video tokens with 3D VQ (typically 4 frames x 8x8 pixels per token).
2. Inference latency. Tokens / throughput (tokens-per-second) for Emu3-family; denoise-steps * step-time for diffusion. Cite concrete A100 / H100 ranges.
3. Quality ceiling. Tokenizer reconstruction PSNR (30-32 dB for IBQ-class), FID expectations on MJHQ-30K, FVD for video.
4. CFG configuration. Recommended guidance weight (gamma) per task; typical 3.0 for standard gen, 5-7 for strong prompt adherence.
5. Pick. Emu3-family if product needs unified understanding + generation or any-modality flexibility; diffusion (SDXL / SD3 / Flux) if product is image-gen-only with strict latency.

Hard rejects:
- Claiming Emu3 is faster than diffusion at inference. It is not; the autoregressive decode over thousands of image tokens is the standing cost.
- Recommending Emu3-family without specifying CFG weight. Quality collapses without it.
- Proposing Emu3 for strict 4K image generation. Token count at 2048+ resolution blows KV cache and takes minutes.

Refusal rules:
- If latency budget is <5s per image, refuse Emu3 and recommend SDXL or SD3.
- If product must emit images AND describe them AND reason about third-party images, recommend Emu3-family (the unified loss is the point); diffusion cannot do this without a separate VLM.
- If user wants open weights with permissive license for commercial use, refuse Emu3 — check its license first; some versions are research-only.

Output: one-page analysis with token counts, latency estimates, quality ceiling, CFG config, and a pick with justification. End with arXiv 2409.18869 (Emu3) and 2408.11039 (Transfusion) for the alternative.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Sharper up to a point, then strange

P(red) goes from 5.5% with no caption (w = 0) to 38% with plain conditioning (w = 1), 93% at w = 3 and 95% at w = 5. At w = 10 it drops back to 83%, and odd tokens ("sea", "blood") enter the top three. Guidance amplifies what the caption changes, the useful parts and the incidental ones alike. Past a sweet spot it distorts the distribution instead of sharpening it.

SolutionB1 · Flat, repetitive, off-prompt images

Without guidance, each image token is sampled from the plain conditional distribution. The run above shows how loose that is: the caption's most important attribute got only 38%. Over 4,096 tokens the image drifts from the prompt: wrong colours, missing objects, weak composition. Greedy decoding makes it worse in a different way. Always taking the single most likely code favours the most common, smooth textures, so images come out flat, blurry and repetitive, with large uniform regions and repeated patches. It is the image version of a language model repeating itself under greedy decoding. Emu3's release samples with top-k 2048 and uses a guidance scale of 3. To speed up generation, cut tokens (lower resolution) or use faster decoding methods instead of removing guidance and sampling.

Interview questions

Try answering out loud first, then open one answer at a time.

Questions from real AI engineering interviews that this lesson prepares you for. Answer out loud first, then open the worked answer.

Estimate itA unified next-token model generates a 512 by 512 image as a sequence of discrete codes. Estimate how long one image takes at a typical decode speed, compare it with a diffusion model, and say what a four-second video clip at low resolution costs in codes. Then name the levers that change the answer.

Level 2 of 4 · about 10 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. The reference model's tokenizer reduces 8 by 8 pixels to one code, so a 512 by 512 image is 4096 codes; at about 30 codes per second that is about 2 minutes per image, against 2 to 5 seconds for a diffusion model of the same quality tier. A 4-second clip at 8 frames per second is 32 frames; with the same 8 times spatial reduction and a 4-frame temporal reduction, a 256 by 256 clip is 32 times 32 times 8, which is 8,192 codes. The levers are the codes per image, the decode speed through speculative decoding or the KV cache, and a different decoding scheme such as masked parallel decoding, which needs about 16 passes instead of one pass per code.

The image.

512 / 8 = 64 codes per side
64 x 64 = 4096 codes per image
4096 codes at about 30 per second = about 2 minutes

A diffusion model samples a continuous latent in a few dozen steps and finishes in 2 to 5 seconds. Autoregressive image generation is compute-heavy by construction: one pass per code.

The video.

The video tokenizer is three-dimensional: one code covers 4 frames by 8 by 8 pixels.

frames: 4 s x 8 per second = 32
codes: (256 / 8) x (256 / 8) x (32 / 4) = 32 x 32 x 8 = 8,192

Why the model pays this price.

The same weights do image generation, image understanding and video with one next-token loss, no diffusion schedule and no separate text encoder. The tokenizer's quality is the ceiling: the reference reports a reconstruction of 30.5 dB against about 32 for a continuous latent space, with four times the codes and four times the codebook of the earlier early-fusion model.

The inference knobs.

  • Classifier-free guidance: generate with and without the caption and mix the logits with a weight, typically 3.0 to 7.0; it doubles the forward passes per code.
  • Temperature: 0.8 for image generation and 1.0 for understanding in the reference recipe.
  • Speculative decoding narrows the gap to diffusion, and so does the KV cache, but neither closes it.

A different decoding scheme.

Masked parallel decoding keeps discrete codes but predicts all masked positions at once and commits the most confident ones, in about 16 passes for an image, against about 20 for continuous diffusion and one pass per code for plain next-token generation.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "An image is a few hundred tokens, like a VLM input." Those are projected encoder tokens for understanding; generation needs discrete codes at the tokenizer's density, 4096 for this image size.
  • "Diffusion is slower because it iterates." Diffusion takes a few dozen passes over all pixels at once; next-token generation takes one pass per code, thousands per image.
  • "A larger codebook makes generation faster." It raises the reconstruction quality; the pass count is set by the number of codes, not by the codebook size.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why does guidance double the cost? Expected: each step needs a conditional and an unconditional forward pass to mix.
  • (level 2) What happens to the per-image time at 1024 by 1024? Expected: four times the codes at the same reduction, so about four times the decode time; the lesson's exercise asks for that count.
  • (level 3) Where does the quality ceiling of this model sit, and what moved it up from the first shared-vocabulary model? Expected: in the tokenizer's reconstruction; four times the codes per image and a four times larger codebook lifted it to 30.5 dB.
  • (level 3) When do you still pick this over a diffusion model? Expected: when one model must understand and generate across image, video and text with one loss, and the latency budget allows minutes per image or a batch pipeline.

Where it is taught. The Emu3 lesson counts the image and video codes and gives the decode cost, and the Show-o lesson compares the pass counts of the three decoding schemes.
