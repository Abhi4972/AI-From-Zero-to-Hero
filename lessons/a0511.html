The problem

In plain words first

Machine translation (MT) is the seq2seq-with-attention design of the last two lessons, trained on millions of sentence pairs. Today you rarely build the model yourself. You pick a pretrained one, decode with beam search, and spend your effort on two harder questions. The first is how to tell whether a translation is good. Automatic scores such as BLEU and chrF count how many word or character sequences the output shares with a human reference translation; newer metrics use neural networks trained on human judgements. The second is how translations fail in ways a score will not show, such as a sentence that reads perfectly but says something the source never said. This lesson derives BLEU by hand, checks it against the standard library, runs a pretrained English-to-French model on a public news test set, and probes its typical failure modes.

A model reads a sentence in one language and produces a sentence in another. Length varies. Word order varies. Some source words map to multiple target words and vice versa. Idioms refuse one-to-one mapping. "I miss you" in French is "tu me manques" — literally "you are lacking to me." No word-level alignment survives that.

Machine translation is the task that forced NLP to invent encoder-decoders, attention, transformers, and eventually the whole LLM paradigm. Every step forward arrived because translation quality was measurable and the gap between human and machine was stubborn.

This lesson skips the history lesson and teaches the working pipeline of 2026: pretrained multilingual encoder-decoder (NLLB-200 or mBART), subword tokenization, beam search, BLEU and chrF evaluation, and the handful of failure modes that still ship to production uncaught.

The idea

Modern MT is a transformer encoder-decoder trained on parallel text. The encoder reads the source in its language's tokenization. The decoder generates the target, one subword at a time, using the encoder's output via cross-attention (lesson 10). Decoding uses beam search to avoid the greedy-decoding trap. The output is detokenized, detruecased, and scored against a reference.

Three operational choices drive real-world MT quality.

  • Tokenizer. SentencePiece BPE trained on a mixed-language corpus. Shared vocabulary across languages is what enables zero-shot pairs in NLLB.
  • Model size. NLLB-200 distilled 600M fits on a laptop. NLLB-200 3.3B is the largest dense checkpoint. The 54.5B mixture-of-experts model is the research ceiling.
  • Decoding. Beam width 4-5 for general content. Length penalty to avoid too-short output. Constrained decoding when you need terminology consistency.

Step 1 · a pretrained MT call

from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForSeq2SeqLM

model_id = "facebook/nllb-200-distilled-600M"
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_id, src_lang="eng_Latn")
model = AutoModelForSeq2SeqLM.from_pretrained(model_id)

src = "The cats are running."
inputs = tok(src, return_tensors="pt")

out = model.generate(
    **inputs,
    forced_bos_token_id=tok.convert_tokens_to_ids("fra_Latn"),
    num_beams=5,
    length_penalty=1.0,
    max_new_tokens=64,
)
print(tok.batch_decode(out, skip_special_tokens=True)[0])

Step 2 · BLEU and chrF

BLEU measures n-gram overlap between output and reference. Four reference n-gram sizes (1-4), geometric mean of precisions, brevity penalty for too-short output. The score is in [0, 100]. Commonly used. Frustrating to interpret: rough folklore says 30 is "usable", 40 "good" and 50 "exceptional", but scores depend heavily on the language pair, the test set and how many references there are, so compare systems only on the same test set, and treat differences under about 1 BLEU as noise.

chrF measures character-level F-score. More sensitive to morphologically rich languages where BLEU undercounts matches. Often reported alongside BLEU.

import sacrebleu

hypotheses = ["Les chats courent."]
references = [["Les chats courent."]]

bleu = sacrebleu.corpus_bleu(hypotheses, references)
chrf = sacrebleu.corpus_chrf(hypotheses, references)
print(f"BLEU: {bleu.score:.1f}  chrF: {chrf.score:.1f}")
What you should see
BLEU: 100.0  chrF: 100.0

Always use sacrebleu. It normalizes tokenization so scores are comparable across papers. Rolling your own BLEU computation is how misleading benchmarks happen.

The same idea as a formula

BLEU (Papineni et al., 2002). For n-gram sizes n=1,…,4, the clipped precision counts each hypothesis n-gram only as many times as it appears in the reference:

pn​=∑g∈n-grams(y^​)​county^​​(g)∑g∈n-grams(y^​)​min(county^​​(g), county​(g))​

Precision alone would reward a one-word output, so a brevity penalty compares the hypothesis length c with the reference length r:

BP={1e1−r/c​c>rc≤r​,BLEU=BP⋅exp(41​n=1∑4​logpn​)

Corpus BLEU sums the clipped counts and lengths over all sentences before dividing, so it is not the average of sentence BLEUs.

chrF (Popović, 2015) works on character n-grams (up to 6) with precision P and recall R averaged over n, combined with β=2, which weighs recall twice as much as precision:

chrFβ​=(1+β2)β2P+RPR​

Because it matches pieces of words, "chats" and "chat" still share most of their character n-grams, which is why chrF is fairer to languages with rich morphology.

Worked example
BLEU by hand

Reference "the cat sat on the mat" (6 words), hypothesis "the cat sat on mat" (5 words).

  • Unigrams: all 5 hypothesis words are in the reference, p1​=5/5=1.
  • Bigrams: "the cat", "cat sat", "sat on" match; "on mat" does not. p2​=3/4.
  • Trigrams: "the cat sat", "cat sat on" match; "sat on mat" does not. p3​=2/3.
  • 4-grams: "the cat sat on" matches; "cat sat on mat" does not. p4​=1/2.
  • Geometric mean: (1×0.75×0.667×0.5)1/4=0.251/4=0.707.
  • Brevity penalty: c=5≤r=6, so BP=e1−6/5=e−0.2=0.819.
  • BLEU =0.819×0.707=0.579, reported as 57.9.

Dropping one small word cost 42 points, because it broke three of the higher-order n-grams. The first experiment under "Use it" checks this number against sacrebleu.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "BLEU measures whether the translation is correct."
    ✓ It measures n-gram overlap with one particular reference. A correct paraphrase can score low, and a fluent wrong translation that reuses the reference's words can score high.
  • ✗ "Corpus BLEU is the average of sentence BLEU."
    ✓ Counts are pooled first, then divided; the two numbers differ, and sentence BLEU is very noisy.
  • ✗ "A higher BLEU always means a better system."
    ✓ Only on the same test set, references and tokenization, and only for differences beyond the noise; learned metrics and human evaluation decide close calls.

Build · The three-tier evaluation hierarchy (2026)

Modern MT evaluation uses three complementary metric families. Ship with at least two.

  • Heuristic (BLEU, chrF). Fast, reference-based, interpretable, insensitive to paraphrase. Use for legacy comparison and regression detection.
  • Learned (COMET, BLEURT, MetricX; and the untrained BERTScore). COMET, BLEURT and MetricX are neural models fine-tuned to predict human quality judgements from the source, the translation and the reference; BERTScore instead compares contextual embeddings with no training on judgements. In the WMT metrics tasks, learned metrics agree with human judgements far better than BLEU, and COMET is the most widely reported.
  • LLM-as-judge (reference-free). Prompt a large model to score translations on fluency, adequacy, tone, cultural appropriateness. GEMBA (Kocmi and Federmann, 2023) showed that GPT-4, prompted to score translations, ranked WMT22 systems in close agreement with human rankings; at the level of single segments it is much less reliable. Use for open-ended content where no reference exists.

Practical 2026 stack: sacrebleu for BLEU and chrF, unbabel-comet for COMET, and a prompted LLM for the final human-facing signal. Calibrate every metric against 50-100 human-labeled examples before trusting it on production data.

Reference-free metrics (COMET-QE, BLEURT-QE, LLM-as-judge) let you evaluate translations without a reference, which matters for long-tail language pairs where reference translations do not exist.

Step 3 · what breaks in production

The working pipeline above translates fluently most of the time and fails silently on a minority of inputs, and the failures are concentrated in exactly the content that matters (names, numbers, domain terms, rare languages). Named failure modes:

  • Hallucination. Model invents content that was not in the source. Common in unfamiliar domain vocabulary. Symptom: output is fluent but claims facts the source did not state. Mitigation: constrained decoding on domain terms, human review on regulated content, monitoring for output much longer than input.
  • Off-target generation. Model translates into the wrong language. NLLB is surprisingly prone to this on rare language pairs. Mitigation: verify forced_bos_token_id and always decode with a language-ID model check on output.
  • Terminology drift. "Sign up" becomes "s'inscrire" in doc 1 and "créer un compte" in doc 2. For UI text and user-facing strings, consistency matters more than raw quality. Mitigation: glossary-constrained decoding or post-edit dictionary.
  • Formality mismatch. French "tu" vs "vous", Japanese politeness levels. The model picks whichever form was more common in training. For customer-facing content this is usually wrong. Mitigation: prompt prefix with a formality token if the model supports it, or fine-tune a small model on formal-only corpora.
  • Degenerate output on short or unusual input. Single words, UI labels and fragments are rare in sentence-level training data, and models sometimes answer them with an over-long or repetitive output. Mitigation: a hard maximum output length proportional to the source length, and a glossary for UI strings.

Step 4 · fine-tuning for a domain

Pretrained models are generalists. Legal, medical, or game-dialog translation benefits measurably from fine-tuning on domain parallel data. The recipe is not exotic:

from transformers import Trainer, TrainingArguments
from datasets import Dataset

pairs = [
    {"src": "The defendant pleaded guilty.", "tgt": "L'accusé a plaidé coupable."},
]

ds = Dataset.from_list(pairs)


def preprocess(ex):
    return tok(
        ex["src"],
        text_target=ex["tgt"],
        truncation=True,
        max_length=128,
        padding="max_length",
    )


ds = ds.map(preprocess, remove_columns=["src", "tgt"])

args = TrainingArguments(output_dir="out", per_device_train_batch_size=4, num_train_epochs=3, learning_rate=3e-5)
Trainer(model=model, args=args, train_dataset=ds).train()

A few thousand high-quality parallel examples beats a few hundred thousand noisy web-scraped ones. Quality of training data is the single largest production lever.

Reading this output

sacrebleu gives 57.9 for the worked example, with the same precisions (100, 75, 66.7, 50) and brevity penalty (0.819). The three-hypothesis test shows the metrics' blind spots. The paraphrase is a perfectly good description of the scene and gets only 23 BLEU, because it shares few 3- and 4-grams with the one reference. The shuffled words are nonsense but still collect 13 BLEU and 33 chrF from matching words and pieces of words. Both metrics rank the three in the right order here, but the gap between "correct but differently worded" and "word salad" is small, which is why learned metrics and human checks exist.

A pretrained model on a real news test set

Helsinki-NLP's opus-mt-en-fr (a Marian transformer, 75M parameters, trained on the OPUS parallel corpora) translates the first 100 sentences of the WMT14 English-French news test set with greedy and beam-5 decoding, scored with corpus BLEU and chrF. Then the same model meets inputs chosen to trigger the failure modes of Step 3.

import time
import torch
import pyarrow.parquet as pq
from huggingface_hub import hf_hub_download
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForSeq2SeqLM
torch.set_num_threads(2)

wmt = pq.read_table(hf_hub_download("wmt/wmt14", "fr-en/test-00000-of-00001.parquet", repo_type="dataset")).to_pylist()
src = [r["translation"]["en"] for r in wmt[:100]]; refs = [r["translation"]["fr"] for r in wmt[:100]]
mt_tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("Helsinki-NLP/opus-mt-en-fr")
mt = AutoModelForSeq2SeqLM.from_pretrained("Helsinki-NLP/opus-mt-en-fr").eval()

def translate(texts, beams, batch=16):
    out = []
    for i in range(0, len(texts), batch):
        x = mt_tok(texts[i:i + batch], return_tensors="pt", padding=True, truncation=True)
        with torch.no_grad():
            ids = mt.generate(**x, num_beams=beams, max_new_tokens=200)
        out += mt_tok.batch_decode(ids, skip_special_tokens=True)
    return out

results = {}
for beams in (1, 5):
    start = time.time(); hyp = translate(src, beams)
    results[beams] = hyp
    print(f"beams={beams}: BLEU {sacrebleu.corpus_bleu(hyp, [refs]).score:.1f}   chrF {sacrebleu.corpus_chrf(hyp, [refs]).score:.1f}"
          f"   ({time.time() - start:.0f} s for 100 sentences)")
diff = [i for i in range(100) if results[1][i] != results[5][i]]
print(f"beam search changed {len(diff)} of 100 outputs; e.g.\n   source: {src[diff[0]]}\n   greedy: {results[1][diff[0]]}\n   beam 5: {results[5][diff[0]]}\n   ref:    {refs[diff[0]]}")

print("\nfailure-mode probes:")
probes = ["I miss you.", "Can you help me with my order?", "Save", "Click Sign up, then sign up again with the same email.",
          "The patient received 5 mg of warfarin on 3 March 2024.", "It's raining cats and dogs."]
for s, t in zip(probes, translate(probes, 5)):
    print(f"   {s:58s} -> {t}")

Reading this output

On news text the 75M-parameter Marian model scores about 40 BLEU and 63 chrF. Beam search adds about one BLEU point at almost three times the decoding time, and changes 62 of the 100 outputs, mostly small choices: in the printed example the beam picks the past tense "il a survolé", which fits the story, where greedy used the present. Compare that sentence with the human reference, which restructures it freely and adds an explanation of "wingsuit": both machine outputs are good French and still share little with the reference, which is why BLEU stays far from 100. A caution: OPUS contains public news text, so the model may have seen material like this, and its score here may be slightly optimistic. The probes are mixed. "I miss you" correctly becomes "Tu me manques", word order inverted. The customer request uses formal "vous", the model's habit, with no way to ask for "tu". "Save" becomes the verb "Enregistrer", right for a button, but only by luck, since there is no context. The medical sentence keeps dose, drug and date intact. The idiom fails: "Il pleut des chats et des chiens" is a word-for-word rendering that no French speaker would say (the idiom is "il pleut des cordes"), and it is fluent enough that a reader who does not know French would never notice.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • LLMs run commercial translation. DeepL moved its service to its own translation LLM (2024), Google Translate uses Gemini models, including live speech-to-speech translation, and general LLM APIs are a common choice for document translation with style and terminology instructions.
  • Dedicated models remain. For high volume, on-device or offline translation, compact encoder-decoders (Marian, distilled NLLB) still win on cost and latency, and they are the default for the long tail of languages that big LLMs cover poorly.
  • Quality estimation in production. Learned metrics such as COMET and MetricX score translations without a reference, so pipelines route low-scoring output to human post-editors.

Sources: DeepL next-generation LLM, Gemini in Google Translate, NLLB, COMET, MetricX.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Modern MT is a pretrained transformer encoder-decoder with a shared subword vocabulary, decoded with beam search and a length penalty.
  • BLEU = brevity penalty times the geometric mean of clipped 1- to 4-gram precisions; chrF is a recall-weighted character n-gram F-score.
  • Surface metrics punish valid paraphrases; use learned metrics (COMET, MetricX) and human checks for close calls, on the same test set only.
  • Production failures are silent: hallucination, wrong language, inconsistent terms, wrong formality, damaged numbers and names.
  • LLMs now lead quality benchmarks; dedicated models still win on cost, latency and coverage of rare languages.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Same order, uncomfortably small gaps

Both metrics give the copy 100 and rank the paraphrase above the shuffled words: BLEU about 23 against 13, chrF about 50 against 33. The ranking is right, but look at the scale: a correct sentence in other words gets less than a quarter of BLEU's maximum, and meaningless word salad gets more than half of the paraphrase's BLEU and two-thirds of its chrF. Overlap metrics see form, not meaning, and with one reference they cannot tell a good paraphrase from a partly scrambled output with any confidence.

SolutionB1 · A one-word "translation" scores 25

For "the": p1​=1/1 and p2​=p3​=p4​=0 (it has no bigrams at all), so the arithmetic mean is 0.25, which scores 25 BLEU for a one-word output, with no penalty for being five words short. The real formula blocks both exploits. The geometric mean makes any zero precision give a zero score, so an output must get every n-gram order partly right. The brevity penalty, here e1−6/1=e−5=0.007, punishes outputs much shorter than the reference, which precision alone never sees.
