In plain words first
The idea in one line

Five 2025-2026 benchmarks cover the multi-agent evaluation space. MultiAgentBench / MARBLE (ACL 2025, arXiv:2503.01935) evaluates star/chain/tree/graph topologies with milestone KPIs; graph is best for research, cognitive planning adds ~3% milestone achievement. COMMA evaluates multimodal asymmetric-information coordination; GPT-4o is far below humans, and chain-of-thought reasoning models struggle to beat a random baseline in agent-agent collaboration. MedAgentBoard (arXiv:2505.12371, NeurIPS 2025) covers four medical task categories and finds multi-agent does not consistently beat strong single LLMs, and often trails conventional methods. AgentArch (arXiv:2509.10769) benchmarks 18 enterprise agent architectures (orchestration, agent style, memory, thinking tools) across six models; the best scored 35.3% on the complex task. SWE-bench Pro (arXiv:2509.16941) has 1865 problems across 41 repos spanning business apps, B2B services, and developer tools; frontier models scored about 23% on Pro at its release in September 2025 against 70%+ on Verified — a reality check on contamination and difficulty. Anthropic's Claude Opus 4.7 announcement (April 16, 2026) reports 64.3% on Pro; Verdent (agent scaffold) hit 76.1% pass@1 on Verified in November 2025 (Verdent technical report). The AAAI 2026 Bridge Program on Advancing LLM-Based Multi-Agent Collaboration (https://multiagents.org/2026/, January 2026) is one community focal point. This lesson builds on MARBLE's metrics, runs a topology-vs-metric sweep, and pins the "just passing SWE-bench Verified is not evidence of generalization" rule.

Problem

In plain words first

A benchmark is a fixed set of tasks with a scoring rule, so different systems can be compared. For agent teams the hard questions are what to count (final success, or partial milestones), what to compare against (one model alone, or a random policy), and whether the model has already seen the test (contamination). Evaluating single agents end to end is covered in Zero to Agents 12.12 (linked above). This lesson re-runs the lesson's scorecard many times to see how much one run can be trusted, then measures the gap between two identical small-model systems.

When a paper claims "our multi-agent system is better," the question is: better than what, on what, measured how? The 2023-2024 era of multi-agent evaluation was chaos — everyone picked their own metrics, their own baselines, and their own task sets. The 2025-2026 benchmarks imposed structure.

Without shared benchmarks, you cannot compare two multi-agent systems meaningfully. Worse, without hold-out benchmarks, frontier models can contaminate. In February 2026 OpenAI stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified: every frontier model it tested could reproduce gold patches or problem details for some tasks, and in an audit of 138 hard problems 59.4% had flawed tests or descriptions. It recommends SWE-bench Pro, which was built to resist contamination.

This lesson enumerates the five canonical 2026 benchmarks, names what each measures, and teaches you to read benchmark claims skeptically.

Concept

MultiAgentBench (MARBLE) — ACL 2025

arXiv:2503.01935. Evaluates four coordination topologies (star, chain, tree, graph) in its research scenario, alongside scenarios such as coding, Minecraft, databases and games. Milestone-based KPIs track partial progress rather than only final success.

Measured results:

  • Graph topology best for research scenarios; supports any-to-any critique.
  • Star scored about the same as graph on task score.
  • Tree did worst, with the highest token use and the lowest task and coordination scores. The paper does not single out chain.
  • More agents, lower overall score. Going from 1 to 3 agents sharply improved coordination; beyond that the overall KPI fell. This lesson calls it the coordination tax.
  • Cognitive planning adds ~3% milestone achievement across topologies.

Use when: you want to compare coordination topologies apples-to-apples. The MARBLE repo (https://github.com/ulab-uiuc/MARBLE) provides the evaluator.

COMMA — multimodal asymmetric information

COMMA (arXiv:2410.07553) is a puzzle benchmark where agents hold different information, often visual, and must solve the task together by talking. The result is uncomfortable: GPT-4o reached about 42% partial success against 69% for humans and 19% for a random baseline, and chain-of-thought reasoning models struggled to beat the random baseline in agent-agent collaboration. The signal is that multimodal, asymmetric-information coordination is under-trained and under-evaluated.

Use when: your system has multimodal or asymmetric-information coordination. The null result from COMMA is a warning to measure before claiming.

MedAgentBoard — domain stress test

arXiv:2505.12371 (NeurIPS 2025). Four medical task categories: medical (visual) question answering, lay summary generation, structured EHR predictive modeling and clinical workflow automation. Compares multi-agent collaboration with single LLMs and with conventional specialised methods.

Finding: multi-agent collaboration does not consistently beat advanced single LLMs, and it generally trails specialised conventional methods on tasks such as medical VQA and EHR-based prediction. It helped in places, for example task completeness in clinical workflow automation. The authors' conclusion: weigh the added complexity and overhead against the measured gain.

Use when: your domain has clear-cut single-LLM baselines. If MedAgentBoard's lesson generalizes, many proposed multi-agent systems are over-engineered.

AgentArch — enterprise architectures

arXiv:2509.10769. Two enterprise workflows (requesting time off, and routing customer requests) under 18 architecture configurations across six models, varying orchestration (single or multi-agent), agent style (ReAct or function calling), memory (complete or summarised) and thinking tools. The best model scored 70.8% on the simpler task and 35.3% on the complex one; no architecture won everywhere, multi-agent ReAct was consistently weak, and pass^k reliability peaked around 6%.

Use when: you are designing an enterprise agent stack and need to justify each layer. AgentArch helps avoid buying features you cannot measure the value of.

SWE-bench Pro — the reality check

arXiv:2509.16941 (Scale AI). 1,865 problems across 41 repositories: a 731-problem public set, an 858-problem held-out set and a 276-problem commercial set from private startup codebases. The public and held-out sets use only copyleft repositories, to keep them out of commercial training data. At release top models scored about 23% on the public set, against 70%+ on Verified; Pro's problems are also harder (larger, multi-file patches), so the gap is part contamination, part difficulty.

April 2026 scores: - Claude Opus 4.7 on Pro: 64.3%, from Anthropic's release announcement (April 16, 2026), up from 53.4% for Opus 4.6; Claude Mythos Preview was reported at 77.8%. - Verdent (agent scaffold) on Verified: 76.1% pass@1 and 81.2% pass@3, November 2025 (technical report). - At Pro's release, the best models scored about 23% on the public set; an updated version of the paper reports up to 43.6% (Claude Sonnet 4.5) (SWE-bench Pro paper).

The takeaway: "we beat SWE-bench Verified" is no longer evidence of capability. Pro is the current gating test. The rise from about 23% to 64% on Pro in seven months comes mostly from newer models and their agent harnesses; it is not by itself evidence that multi-agent teams beat single agents.

AAAI 2026 WMAC

AAAI 2026 Bridge Program on Advancing LLM-Based Multi-Agent Collaboration (https://multiagents.org/2026/), held on January 20, 2026 in Singapore. It is one meeting point for multi-agent AI research; workshop acceptance is a light review, so still check claims against the checklist below.

Read benchmark claims skeptically — the 2026 checklist

When someone claims a multi-agent result:

  1. Which benchmark, which split? SWE-bench Verified vs Pro matters a lot. A number reported on the wrong split is worthless.
  2. Contamination check. Was the benchmark released after the model's training cutoff? If not, treat with caution.
  3. Baseline comparison. Vs single-LLM baseline, vs random, vs prior multi-agent work. Not "vs untuned version of the same system."
  4. Statistical significance. N trials, p-value, confidence interval. Frontier models are high-variance; single runs mislead.
  5. Task diversity. One task or many? Generalization matters for production.
  6. Cost disclosure. Tokens per task, wall-clock. A 90% solution at 20x cost is a business decision, not a capability claim.

What none of the benchmarks measure well

  • Long-horizon coordination. Days of wall-clock interaction. All current benchmarks run short.
  • Adversarial resilience. What happens when one agent is malicious or compromised?
  • Drift under deployment. Benchmarks are static; production distributions shift.
  • Cost-normalized performance. Most benchmarks report raw accuracy, not accuracy-per-dollar.

Building your own internal benchmark for the axis you actually care about is often the right move.

The same idea as a formula

Uncertainty of an accuracy. On n tasks with true success rate p, measured accuracy has standard error p(1−p)/n​. For the difference of two independent systems,

SE(a^−b^)=na​a(1−a)​+nb​b(1−b)​​,
and a 95% interval is about ±1.96SE. With 160 tasks each and accuracies near 0.7, that is about ±0.10.

Contamination check. Comparing seen and held-out accuracy, Δ=a^seen​−a^held​, has standard error p(1−p)(1/nseen​+1/nheld​)​. With 40 seen tasks that is about 0.08 at p=0.6, so a fixed threshold of 0.10 fires by chance about one time in eight.

Worked example
One scorecard row, with its error bar
  • In the lesson's run, system A scored 0.731 on 160 held-out tasks and system B 0.681.
  • The difference is 0.050, with standard error 0.731×0.269/160+0.681×0.319/160​≈0.051.
  • The 95% interval runs from about −0.050 to +0.150, so one run cannot say A is better.
  • Over 200 runs A won 195 times, so it is better; the single scorecard just could not show it.

# "contamination flag" fires on systems that are not contaminated, whether the held-out ranking of A and B
# survives a confidence interval, and where the random baseline comes from. Runs in about a second.
import random, inspect
from main import SYSTEMS, run_bench, random_baseline

print("1. the contamination flag (seen - held > 0.10) over 200 benchmark runs")
for s in SYSTEMS:
    flags = sum(run_bench(s, 40, 160, seed=k)["accuracy_seen"] - run_bench(s, 40, 160, seed=k)["accuracy_held"] > 0.1
                for k in range(200))
    truth = "contaminated (+0.20 on seen tasks)" if s.training_contamination else "not contaminated"
    print(f"   {s.name}: {truth:35s} flagged in {flags:3d}/200 runs")

a = run_bench(SYSTEMS[0], 40, 160, seed=17)["accuracy_held"]; b = run_bench(SYSTEMS[1], 40, 160, seed=17)["accuracy_held"]

wins = sum(run_bench(SYSTEMS[0], 40, 160, seed=k)["accuracy_held"] > run_bench(SYSTEMS[1], 40, 160, seed=k)["accuracy_held"]
           for k in range(200))
print(f"   over 200 runs A beat B on held-out tasks {wins} times; B won or tied {200 - wins}")

print("\n3. the random baseline")
print(f"   random_baseline() returns {random_baseline(random.Random(1))} whatever its input:")
print("   " + inspect.getsource(random_baseline).strip().splitlines()[-1].strip())
print("   it is a constant typed into the code, not a measured run of a random policy")

Reading this output

The contamination flag is noisy. It caught the contaminated system A in 188 of 200 runs, but it also fired for the clean systems B and C in 24 and 32 runs. With only 40 seen tasks, a difference of 0.10 is about one standard error, so chance alone crosses it often. In the lesson's own run, B's difference was −0.13, which is noise in the other direction.

One run cannot rank A above B. In the lesson's run A led by 0.050 with a 95% interval from −0.050 to +0.150. Over 200 runs A won 195 times, so A is better, but a single scorecard is too small to show it.

The random baseline is a constant. random_baseline() returns 0.15 whatever it is given; nothing random is ever run. A baseline you type in cannot catch a task that is easier than you think.

Milestones on failures are random. Successes always get 4 of 4; failures average 0.94 of 4, drawn at random. On failed tasks the milestone metric measures the draw, not partial progress.

# This block continues from the one above. Two "systems" that are in fact identical: the same model
# (Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct, CPU), the same prompt and the same sampling settings (temperature 0.7), differing only in
# the random seed. Each answers the same 20 two-digit multiplications in 4 rounds. How big an A/B gap do
# identical systems show? About 1-2 minutes.
import os, re, random, warnings
os.environ["HF_HUB_OFFLINE"] = "1"
warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")
import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM, logging as hf_logging
hf_logging.set_verbosity_error(); hf_logging.disable_progress_bar()
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct")
llm = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct").eval()

rng = random.Random(21)
QS = [(rng.randint(12, 49), rng.randint(12, 49)) for _ in range(20)]
def score(seed):
    right = 0
    for i, (a, b) in enumerate(QS):
        ids = tok.apply_chat_template([{"role": "user", "content": f"What is {a} times {b}? Reply with only the number."}],
                                      add_generation_prompt=True, return_tensors="pt", return_dict=True)["input_ids"]
        torch.manual_seed(seed * 1000 + i)
        with torch.no_grad():
            out = llm.generate(ids, max_new_tokens=8, do_sample=True, temperature=0.7, top_k=0, top_p=1.0)
        m = re.search(r"\d+", tok.decode(out[0, ids.shape[1]:], skip_special_tokens=True).replace(",", ""))
        right += bool(m) and int(m.group()) == a * b
    return right / len(QS)

gaps = []; all_scores = []
for rnd in range(4):
    sa, sb = score(2 * rnd + 1), score(2 * rnd + 2)
    gaps.append(sa - sb); all_scores += [sa, sb]
    print(f"round {rnd + 1}: system A {sa:.2f}, system B {sb:.2f}, gap {sa - sb:+.2f}")
print(f"\nlargest gap between identical systems: {max(abs(g) for g in gaps):.2f}")
p = sum(all_scores) / len(all_scores); se = (2 * p * (1 - p) / 20) ** 0.5
print(f"standard error of a difference of two 20-question accuracies near {p:.2f}: {se:.2f}, so a 95% interval is about +/-{1.96 * se:.2f}")

Reading this output

Identical systems disagreed by up to 10 points. Across four rounds the gap between A and B ranged from −0.05 to +0.10, though nothing differs but the seed. Accuracies sat between 0.40 and 0.50.

The arithmetic predicts it. With 20 questions and accuracy near 0.45, the standard error of a difference is 0.16, so a 95% interval is about ±0.31. Any A/B gap smaller than that on 20 questions says nothing.

What to do. Use more tasks, repeat runs with different seeds, and report the interval. A difference that survives repetition and a proper interval is worth acting on; a single 10-point gap is not.

Build it

code/main.py is a non-interactive walk-through:

  • Simulates 3 multi-agent systems on a toy task.
  • Computes MARBLE-style milestone metrics for each.
  • Runs a contamination check by withholding tasks from a "training" set.

  • Prints a benchmark-claims scorecard.

Part 1 of 3 · SystemSim, TaskResult, run_task, random_baseline

"""Multi-agent benchmark scorecard generator, stdlib only.

Simulates 3 multi-agent systems on a toy task set. Computes MARBLE-style
milestone metrics, random baseline delta, cost-per-milestone, and a
contamination check by splitting seen/unseen tasks.
"""
from __future__ import annotations

import random
from dataclasses import dataclass, field


@dataclass
class SystemSim:
    name: str
    base_accuracy: float
    cost_per_task: float
    milestone_completion_rate: float
    training_contamination: float = 0.0  # extra accuracy on seen tasks
    variance: float = 0.1


@dataclass
class TaskResult:
    task_id: str
    seen_in_training: bool
    accuracy: float
    milestones: int
    cost: float


SYSTEMS = [
    SystemSim("system-A", base_accuracy=0.70, cost_per_task=0.30,
              milestone_completion_rate=0.80, training_contamination=0.20),
    SystemSim("system-B", base_accuracy=0.64, cost_per_task=0.12,
              milestone_completion_rate=0.55, training_contamination=0.0),
    SystemSim("system-C", base_accuracy=0.55, cost_per_task=0.25,
              milestone_completion_rate=0.70, training_contamination=0.0),
]


def run_task(system: SystemSim, task_id: str, seen: bool, rng: random.Random) -> TaskResult:
    base = system.base_accuracy
    if seen:
        base += system.training_contamination
    base = max(0.0, min(1.0, base + rng.uniform(-system.variance, system.variance)))
    success = rng.random() < base
    milestones = 4 if success else int(4 * system.milestone_completion_rate * rng.random())
    return TaskResult(
        task_id=task_id,
        seen_in_training=seen,
        accuracy=1.0 if success else 0.0,
        milestones=milestones,
        cost=system.cost_per_task,
    )


def random_baseline(rng: random.Random) -> float:
    return 0.15  # random routing accuracy on this task family

Part 2 of 3 · run_bench, format_scorecard, print_claim_scorecard

def run_bench(system: SystemSim, n_seen: int, n_held: int, seed: int = 0) -> dict:
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    results_seen: list[TaskResult] = []
    results_held: list[TaskResult] = []
    for i in range(n_seen):
        results_seen.append(run_task(system, f"seen-{i}", True, rng))
    for i in range(n_held):
        results_held.append(run_task(system, f"held-{i}", False, rng))
    return {
        "name": system.name,
        "accuracy_seen": sum(r.accuracy for r in results_seen) / len(results_seen),
        "accuracy_held": sum(r.accuracy for r in results_held) / len(results_held),
        "milestone_rate_seen": sum(r.milestones for r in results_seen) / (len(results_seen) * 4),
        "milestone_rate_held": sum(r.milestones for r in results_held) / (len(results_held) * 4),
        "cost_per_task": system.cost_per_task,
        "cost_per_milestone_held":
            system.cost_per_task / max(0.01, sum(r.milestones for r in results_held) / len(results_held) / 4),
    }


def format_scorecard() -> None:
    print("=" * 78)
    print("BENCHMARK SCORECARD: MARBLE-style milestone + contamination check")
    print("  contamination check: accuracy_seen - accuracy_held (delta > 0.1 = suspect)")
    print("=" * 78)
    print(f"{'system':10s} {'acc(seen)':>10s} {'acc(held)':>10s} {'Δ':>6s} "
          f"{'mile(held)':>12s} {'cost/t':>8s} {'cost/mil':>10s} {'vs random':>12s}")

    rng = random.Random(0)
    rand_baseline = random_baseline(rng)
    for sys in SYSTEMS:
        r = run_bench(sys, n_seen=40, n_held=160, seed=17)
        delta = r["accuracy_seen"] - r["accuracy_held"]
        contam_flag = "*" if delta > 0.1 else " "
        vs_random = r["accuracy_held"] - rand_baseline
        print(f"{r['name']:10s} {r['accuracy_seen']:>10.3f} {r['accuracy_held']:>10.3f} "
              f"{delta:>5.2f}{contam_flag} {r['milestone_rate_held']:>12.3f} "
              f"${r['cost_per_task']:>7.2f} ${r['cost_per_milestone_held']:>9.3f} "
              f"+{vs_random:>10.3f}")

    print("\n  * = contamination flag; held-set accuracy is the canonical number")
    print(f"  random baseline accuracy: {rand_baseline:.3f}")


def print_claim_scorecard() -> None:
    print("\n" + "=" * 78)
    print("CLAIM CHECKLIST: read this before accepting any multi-agent result")
    print("=" * 78)
    checklist = [
        "Which benchmark + split? Pro vs Verified is a 40-point gap for frontier models.",
        "Contamination check: is the benchmark post-training-cutoff?",
        "Baseline comparison: vs single-LLM, vs random, vs prior multi-agent?",
        "Statistical significance: N trials, p-value, confidence interval?",
        "Task diversity: single task or many? Generalization beyond one domain?",
        "Cost disclosure: tokens per task, wall-clock per task?",
    ]
    for i, item in enumerate(checklist, 1):
        print(f"  [{i}] {item}")


def main() -> None:
    format_scorecard()
    print_claim_scorecard()
    print("\nTakeaways:")
    print("  system-A scores highest on seen tasks but has contamination signal (large delta).")
    print("  system-B is cheapest per milestone; lowest raw accuracy but transparent.")
    print("  system-C sits in the middle but without contamination flag, trustworthy.")
    print("  the ranking by 'raw accuracy' vs 'cost per milestone (held)' can differ sharply.")

  system-A scores highest on seen tasks but has contamination signal (large delta).
  system-B is cheapest per milestone; lowest raw accuracy but transparent.
  system-C sits in the middle but without contamination flag, trustworthy.
  the ranking by 'raw accuracy' vs 'cost per milestone (held)' can differ sharply.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

outputs/skill-benchmark-reader.md reads any multi-agent benchmark claim and applies the scrutiny checklist. Output: a grade and caveats.

Break it on purpose

A vendor's slide says its five-agent coding team "beats single agents by 12 points on SWE-bench Verified", measured on 50 tasks, one run each, against a single-agent baseline that used an older model. It also reports "milestone completion 88%". Using this lesson's checklist, what is wrong with the claim, and what would you ask for? (Solution B1.)

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • SWE-bench Pro replaces Verified at the top. OpenAI stopped reporting Verified in February 2026 over contamination and flawed tests; Pro's public set is the common reference, and Anthropic reported 64.3% for Claude Opus 4.7 on it.
  • Agent benchmarks with partial credit. MultiAgentBench's milestones, COMMA's partial success and AgentArch's pass^k reliability all go beyond a single success rate.
  • Multi-agent is not a free win. MedAgentBoard and AgentArch both find that multi-agent designs often fail to beat a strong single model.
  • On your laptop: run every comparison several times, report intervals, and include a measured random or trivial baseline; with 20 questions, gaps up to about 0.3 can be noise.

Sources: OpenAI, Why we no longer evaluate SWE-bench Verified, SWE-bench Pro (arXiv:2509.16941), Anthropic, Introducing Claude Opus 4.7, COMMA (arXiv:2410.07553), MedAgentBoard (arXiv:2505.12371), AgentArch (arXiv:2509.10769), MultiAgentBench (arXiv:2503.01935).

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Every benchmark number needs its split, its baseline, its cost and its uncertainty.
  • Contamination and difficulty both widen the gap between old and new benchmarks.
  • A fixed threshold on noisy differences raises false alarms; scale it to the standard error.
  • Measure the random baseline; do not type it in.
  • Identical systems differ by chance; run several times before believing a gap.

Ship it

Production evaluation discipline:

  • Build an internal benchmark that reflects your actual production distribution. Public benchmarks inform but do not substitute.
  • Include a random baseline in every comparison. If you cannot beat random by a large margin on a coordination task, the task may be ill-posed.
  • Report cost alongside accuracy. Token cost and wall-clock. Ops teams need both.
  • Rebuild the benchmark quarterly. Production distribution shifts; stale benchmarks mislead.
  • Avoid published-benchmark overfitting. If your team is optimizing specifically for SWE-bench Pro numbers, you will regress on production.

Given a published or internal claim of multi-agent benchmark performance, grade the claim and surface caveats.

Produce:

1. **Benchmark + split identification.** Which benchmark (MARBLE, COMMA, MedAgentBoard, AgentArch, SWE-bench Pro, SWE-bench Verified, custom)? Which split (full, held-out, contamination-cleaned)? Unknown splits are disqualifying.
2. **Contamination status.** Is the benchmark post-training-cutoff for the model under test? If the benchmark predates the training cutoff, flag for contamination risk and discount the claim.
3. **Baseline quality.** Vs single-LLM, vs random, vs prior multi-agent work. Vs untuned-same-system does not count; it is an ablation, not a baseline.
4. **Statistical significance.** N trials, confidence interval or standard error, p-value or equivalent. Claims without statistics on N < 50 trials are under-supported.
5. **Task diversity.** One task, one domain, or many? Single-task claims do not imply generalization.
6. **Cost disclosure.** Tokens per task, wall-clock per task, dollar cost per task. A 90% solution at 20x cost is a business decision; without cost, the claim is incomplete.
7. **Letter grade + one-sentence verdict.**

   - **A:** All six checks pass; the claim is likely robust.
   - **B:** One weakness; the claim is plausible with noted caveats.
   - **C:** Two weaknesses; the claim is suggestive but needs replication.
   - **D:** Three or more weaknesses; the claim is not evidence.
   - **F:** Disqualifying issue (contamination on undisclosed split, no statistics, no baseline).

Hard rejects:

- Claims citing "SWE-bench" without specifying Verified vs Pro. The 40+ point gap makes this ambiguous reporting unacceptable.
- Claims without baseline comparison. "Our system does X%" is a number, not a result.
- Claims based on fewer than 20 trials for multi-agent systems. Variance is too high.
- Cost-unreported claims for multi-agent systems. The coordination tax is material.

Refusal rules:

- If the benchmark is not publicly available and the user has no internal audit trail, grade cannot be assigned. Recommend releasing evaluation artifacts.
- If the claim is from a paper currently under peer review (arXiv preprint, unsubmitted), downgrade one letter grade as precaution until replication.
- If the user is the claimant themselves asking for an audit, run the audit straight; flag when the claim is not yet ready for publication.

Output: a one-page grade card. Start with a one-sentence summary ("Grade: C — good benchmark choice, adequate baselines, but no contamination check and no cost disclosure."), then the seven sections above. End with a prioritized list of "what to fix to raise the grade."

Solutions

SolutionP1 · About one run in eight

About one run in eight: 24 of 200. System B's seen and held-out accuracy have the same expected value, but with 40 seen tasks the difference has a standard error of about 0.08, so it exceeds 0.10 by chance about 12% of the time. System C was flagged in 32 of 200 runs. Scale the threshold to the standard error, or use more seen tasks.

SolutionB1 · Wrong split, no error bars, unfair baseline

What is wrong: - Wrong split: SWE-bench Verified is contaminated and has flawed tests; OpenAI stopped reporting it in February 2026. Ask for SWE-bench Pro, or a held-out set. - No error bars: 50 tasks and one run each give a standard error of about 0.1 for the difference; a 12-point gap is within noise. - Unfair baseline: a single agent on an older model compares models, not architectures. The baseline must use the same model and budget. - Unexplained milestones: "88% milestone completion" means nothing without the milestone definition and the success rate beside it. - No cost: five agents use several times the tokens; ask for cost per resolved task.

What to ask for: the same model in both arms, Pro or a private set, at least several runs with seeds, intervals or a significance test, a trivial or random baseline, and tokens and wall-clock per task.

Interview questions

Try answering out loud first, then open one answer at a time.

Questions from real AI engineering interviews that this lesson prepares you for. Answer out loud first, then open the worked answer.

Explain the mechanismA paper reports that its multi-agent coding system beats single agents by a wide margin on a well-known software engineering benchmark. Your team wants to adopt the architecture. What do you ask before you believe the number?

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Ask which benchmark and which split, whether the benchmark was released after the model's training cutoff, what the comparison baseline was, how many trials were run, how many task types were covered, and what the result cost in tokens and wall-clock. The split matters most: at its release, frontier models scored about 23 percent on the contamination-resistant benchmark against more than 70 percent on the older one, so a number without its split says nothing.

The checklist.

Question Why it matters
Which benchmark, which split The newer split was built to resist contamination, and scores differ by a factor of three between splits
Contamination A benchmark released before the model's cutoff can have leaked into training, so the score can be inflated
Baseline Against a single-agent baseline, random, and prior multi-agent work, not against an untuned version of the same system
Significance Number of trials and a confidence interval, because frontier models are high-variance and single runs mislead
Task diversity One task or many, because production needs generalization
Cost disclosure Tokens per task and wall-clock, because a strong result at 20 times the cost is a business decision

What the other benchmarks teach.

The lesson surveys five benchmark families, and two of them are warnings. On a multimodal coordination benchmark with asymmetric information, frontier models struggle to beat a random baseline. On a medical benchmark with four task categories, multi-agent systems did not dominate a single model on most categories; decomposition helped where subtasks were separable and hurt where coordination cost exceeded the specialization gain. A topology benchmark found graph best for research, chain for stepwise coding, star for fast factual work, and a coordination tax past about 4 agents; cognitive planning added about 3 percent milestone achievement.

What the number in the prompt may mean.

If it is on the uncontaminated split, agent-team scaffolding has produced a measurable delta there, in the range of 30 to 40 points, and that is one of the stronger empirical arguments for coordination. If it is on the older split, the result is no longer evidence of capability.

What no benchmark measures well.

Long-horizon coordination over days, resilience to a malicious agent, drift under deployment, and cost-normalized performance. For the axis you care about, build an internal benchmark from your production distribution, include a random baseline in every comparison, report cost beside accuracy, and rebuild it quarterly.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "A wide margin on a known benchmark is enough to adopt." Without the split, the baseline, the trials and the cost, the margin is a headline, not evidence.
  • "Multi-agent always helps on coding tasks." The medical and multimodal results show domains where it did not, and the coding gain depends on the split and the scaffolding.
  • "Our internal benchmark is less rigorous than a public one." A public benchmark informs, and only an internal one reflects your production distribution and your cost constraints.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why compare a coordination system against random guessing? Expected: if the system cannot beat random by a wide margin, the task may be ill-posed, as the multimodal coordination results showed.
  • (level 2) What does milestone achievement measure that final success does not? Expected: partial progress, so a system that gets most of the way is distinguished from one that fails at the start.
  • (level 3) How do you check contamination yourself? Expected: compare the benchmark's release date with the model's training cutoff, and withhold a slice of tasks from any tuning set in your own evaluations.
  • (level 3) The paper's score is reported without a primary source from the model's vendor. How do you treat it? Expected: as preliminary, and you do not make a production decision on it until an independent run reproduces it.
