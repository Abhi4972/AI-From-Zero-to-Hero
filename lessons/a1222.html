In plain words first
The idea in one line

Documents are not photos. A PDF, scientific paper, invoice, or handwritten form has layout, tables, diagrams, footnotes, headers, and semantic structure that plain image understanding cannot capture. The pre-VLM stack was a pipeline: Tesseract OCR + LayoutLMv3 + table-extraction heuristics. The VLM wave replaced that with OCR-free models — Donut (2022) and Nougat (2023) — that emit structured markup directly, while layout-aware models such as LayoutLMv3 and DocLLM kept the OCR step and added the page geometry. By 2026 the common approach is to feed the page image to a VLM at high resolution (Claude 4.7 and later accept up to 2,576 pixels on the long edge) and ask for structured markup, or to run a small dedicated document-parsing VLM

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
OCR pipeline "Tesseract-style" Stage-wise stack: detect -> OCR -> layout -> rules; deterministic, fragile
OCR-free "Donut-style" Image-to-output transformer that skips explicit OCR; single model
Layout-aware "LayoutLM" Input includes per-token bounding boxes; LayoutLMv3 also reads image patches, masks text and patches, and aligns words with patches
VLM-native "Frontier VLM" Feed page image directly to Claude/GPT/Qwen VLM at high resolution; no pipeline
DocVQA "Doc benchmark" Questions about scanned document images, scored with ANLS (a string-similarity accuracy); the most-cited document score
Markup output "LaTeX / MD" Structured output format instead of free-form text; enables downstream automation

The problem

In plain words first

A document is more than its words. On an invoice, "$1,022.40" means "the total" because of where it sits: on the row that says Total, at the bottom right. Document AI is about reading text together with that layout, plus tables, formulas and handwriting. It went through three eras. First came pipelines: OCR finds the words and their boxes, then rules or layout-aware models such as LayoutLMv3 pick out the fields. Then came OCR-free models such as Donut and Nougat, which turn the page image straight into structured text. Now vision-language models read the page image at high resolution and answer or convert it directly. This lesson runs a real OCR engine on a rendered invoice, builds LayoutLMv3's three inputs with its real processor, and counts what one page costs each kind of model. No model is trained, and no model weights are downloaded.

"Understand this PDF" is deceptively hard. The information sits in:

  • Text content (most of the signal).
  • Layout (headers, footnotes, sidebars, two-column format).
  • Tables (rows, columns, merged cells).
  • Figures and diagrams.
  • Handwritten annotations.
  • Fonts and typography (title vs body).

Raw OCR dumps the text and loses the rest. A system that cares about invoices needs to know "Total: $1,245" came from the bottom-right, not from a footnote.

Idea 1 · Era 1 — OCR pipeline (pre-2021)

The classic stack:

  1. PDF → image per page.
  2. Tesseract (or commercial OCR) extracts text with per-word bounding boxes.
  3. Layout analyzer identifies blocks (header, table, paragraph).
  4. Table structure recognizer parses tables.
  5. Domain rules + regex extract fields.

Works for clean printed text. Breaks on handwriting, skewed scans, complex tables, non-English scripts. Every failure mode requires a custom exception path.

Idea 2 · TrOCR (2021)

TrOCR (Li et al., arXiv:2109.10282) replaced the usual CNN-plus-recurrent text recognizer with a pure transformer: an image encoder initialised from BEiT or DeiT reads a cropped text line, and a text decoder initialised from RoBERTa or MiniLM writes it out. It was pretrained on about 684M text lines cut from digital PDFs, then on synthetic and task data; TrOCR-Large reached a 2.89% character error rate on IAM handwriting and 96.6 F1 on SROIE receipts. Clean win on handwritten and printed text. Still a pipeline (a detector finds the lines, TrOCR reads them, layout comes after), but the OCR step improved dramatically.

Idea 3 · Era 2 — OCR-free (2022-2023)

The first OCR-free models said: skip detection entirely, map image pixels to structured output directly.

Donut (Kim et al., arXiv:2111.15664): - Encoder-decoder transformer: a Swin-B encoder reading the page at up to 2560×1920 and a 4-layer BART decoder, about 143M parameters. - Output is a token sequence that converts to JSON: fields for forms and receipts, a class for document classification, an answer for DocVQA (67.5 ANLS, against 78.1 for LayoutLMv2 with OCR). - Pretraining is "pseudo-OCR": read all the text in order, on 11M scanned pages (IIT-CDIP) plus about 2M synthetic pages in four languages (SynthDoG). - No OCR, no layout, no detection.

Nougat (Blecher et al., arXiv:2308.13418): - Trained specifically on scientific papers: about 8.2M pages, over 90% of them from arXiv. - Output is Markdown with LaTeX math (Mathpix Markdown), about 1,400 tokens per page. - Handles equations, multi-column layout, figures. - Swin encoder at 896×672 and a 10-layer mBART decoder, 350M parameters. On about 1.5% of test pages it falls into a repetition loop, more often out of domain.

These are specialists, not generalists. Donut on a scientific paper fails; Nougat on an invoice fails.

Idea 4 · LayoutLMv3 (2022)

A different track. LayoutLMv3 (Huang et al., arXiv:2204.08387) keeps OCR but adds layout understanding:

  • Three input streams: OCR text tokens, per-token 2D bounding boxes, image patches.
  • Pretraining: masked language modelling on the text, masked image modelling on the patches (targets from an image tokenizer), and word-patch alignment (is this word's image patch masked?).
  • Downstream: classification, entity extraction, table QA.

LayoutLMv3 is the peak of OCR-based document understanding. Strong on forms and invoices: FUNSD form F1 92.1 (large). Requires OCR upstream: with Microsoft's Read OCR it reached DocVQA ANLS 78.8 (base, 133M) and 83.4 (large, 368M).

The same idea as a formula

Layout as numbers. LayoutLM-style models put each word's box on a 0–1000 grid, whatever the page size W×H:

x~=⌊1000⋅Wx​⌋,y~​=⌊1000⋅Hy​⌋.
Every subword token of a word gets that word's box. LayoutLMv3's image stream is a 224×224 page cut into 16×16 patches: (224/16)2=196 patch tokens.

What a page costs a VLM. With p×p pixels per visual token, a page of W×H pixels costs

n=⌈pW​⌉⋅⌈pH​⌉,p=28 for Qwen2.5-VL and Claude,p=32 for a Swin encoder’s last stage.

Can it still read? For q-point text on a page L inches tall, shown to the model at h pixels tall, the x-height (height of a lowercase "x", about half the point size) is

hx​≈0.5⋅72q​⋅Lh​ pixels.

ANLS, the DocVQA metric: for each question, compare the output o with each accepted answer a by normalised edit distance NL(a,o)=Lev(a,o)/max(∣a∣,∣o∣), and average the best score

s(a,o)={1−NL(a,o)0​if NL(a,o)<0.5otherwise.​

Worked example
An A4 invoice through three kinds of model
  • At 150 dpi an A4 page is 1240×1754 pixels. Qwen2.5-VL rounds it to 1232×1764: 44⋅63=2,772 visual tokens.
  • 10-point text at 150 dpi has an x-height of 0.5⋅7210​⋅150≈10.4 pixels: easy to read.
  • Nougat squeezes the page to 896 pixels tall: 0.5⋅7210​⋅11.69896​≈5.3 pixels. Still readable, just.
  • LayoutLMv3's image is 224 pixels tall: about 1.3 pixels. Its image stream sees layout, not letters; the words must come from OCR.
  • ANLS for the answer "$1,022.40": the output "$1,022.4" is one edit away, NL=1/9, score 0.89. The wrong total "$1,245.00" is 4 edits away, NL=0.44<0.5, so it still scores 0.56. "$189.00" is 5 edits away and scores 0. ANLS forgives typos, and sometimes wrong numbers.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "OCR-free means the model ignores text."
    ✓ It reads the text itself from pixels; it just has no separate OCR engine.
  • ✗ "LayoutLMv3 reads the words from its image patches."
    ✓ At 224 pixels the letters are a pixel tall; it takes words and boxes from OCR, and the image adds layout and style.
  • ✗ "Higher resolution is always better for a VLM."
    ✓ Tokens grow with the square of the resolution; past the point where text is legible, you pay for nothing.
  • ✗ "A high DocVQA score means the extracted numbers are right."
    ✓ ANLS gives partial credit for near-misses, including wrong numbers that share digits.
Predict before you run

A rendered invoice goes through Tesseract, a classic OCR engine. Will a regular expression for the first dollar amount in the OCR text find the invoice total? And how much skew, blur, noise or lost resolution can the OCR take before it stops reading? (Solution P1.)

See it drawn
An Era-1 pipeline on a rendered invoice

A two-column invoice is drawn with Pillow at about 150 dpi and read by Tesseract 5, an open-source OCR engine (via pytesseract; install the tesseract program first). The output shows the words with their boxes, then the total found two ways: a regex on the text alone, and a rule that uses the boxes. Last, it measures how many of the words survive skew, blur, noise and lower resolution. About 5 seconds.

import re
import numpy as np
import pytesseract
from PIL import Image, ImageDraw, ImageFont, ImageFilter

F = "/usr/share/fonts/truetype/dejavu/DejaVuSans.ttf"
font = lambda size, bold=False: ImageFont.truetype(F.replace(".ttf", "-Bold.ttf") if bold else F, size)

# A two-column invoice page, rendered at about 150 dpi (A5-sized to keep it fast).
img = Image.new("L", (1240, 870), 255)
d = ImageDraw.Draw(img)
d.text((60, 40), "ACME Tools Ltd", font=font(40, True))
d.text((60, 95), "12 Harbour Road, Leeds", font=font(22))
d.text((760, 40), "INVOICE INV-2026-0042", font=font(28, True))
d.text((760, 85), "Date: 14 March 2026", font=font(22))
d.text((760, 115), "Due: 13 April 2026", font=font(22))
d.text((60, 170), "Bill to: Northwind Garden Supplies", font=font(22))
y = 250
for col, x in (("Item", 60), ("Qty", 640), ("Unit price", 780), ("Amount", 1010)):
    d.text((x, y), col, font=font(22, True))
d.line((60, y + 34, 1180, y + 34), fill=0, width=2)
rows = [("Cordless drill", 3, 189.00), ("Drill bit set", 5, 32.00), ("Safety gloves", 20, 6.25)]
for i, (item, q, p) in enumerate(rows):
    yy = y + 50 + 40 * i
    for txt, x in ((item, 60), (str(q), 640), (f"${p:,.2f}", 780), (f"${q*p:,.2f}", 1010)):
        d.text((x, yy), txt, font=font(22))
sub = sum(q * p for _, q, p in rows); vat = 0.2 * sub; total = sub + vat
for i, (k, v) in enumerate((("Subtotal", sub), ("VAT 20%", vat), ("Total", total))):
    d.text((780, 470 + 40 * i), k, font=font(22, k == "Total"))
    d.text((1010, 470 + 40 * i), f"${v:,.2f}", font=font(22, k == "Total"))
d.text((60, 780), "Late payment fee: $50.00 per month. Questions: accounts@acme.example", font=font(16))
print(f"page {img.size[0]}x{img.size[1]} px; true total ${total:,.2f}")

def ocr(im):
    t = pytesseract.image_to_data(im, output_type=pytesseract.Output.DICT)
    return [(w, (l, tp, l + wd, tp + h), c) for w, l, tp, wd, h, c in
            zip(t["text"], t["left"], t["top"], t["width"], t["height"], t["conf"]) if w.strip()]

words = ocr(img)
text = pytesseract.image_to_string(img)
print(f"\nEra 1, step 2 - OCR: {len(words)} words with boxes; first five:")
for w, box, c in words[:5]:
    print(f"  {w!r:<12} box={box} conf={c:.0f}")

# Step 5 - field extraction. A regex on the text alone vs a rule that uses the boxes.
first_money = re.search(r"\$[\d,]+\.\d\d", text).group()
key = next(b for w, b, _ in words if w.lower().startswith("total"))
same_row = [(b[0], w) for w, b, _ in words
            if abs((b[1] + b[3]) / 2 - (key[1] + key[3]) / 2) < 15 and b[0] > key[2]]
print(f"\nregex 'first $ amount' on the text : {first_money}")
print(f"layout rule 'right of Total, same row': {min(same_row)[1]}")

# Robustness: the same page as a skewed, blurred, noisy scan.
truth = [w for w, _, _ in words]
def word_recall(im):
    got = [w for w, _, _ in ocr(im)]
    return sum(min(got.count(w), truth.count(w)) for w in set(truth)) / len(truth)
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def scan(angle, blur, noise):
    im = img.rotate(angle, expand=True, fillcolor=255).filter(ImageFilter.GaussianBlur(blur))
    a = np.asarray(im, float) + rng.normal(0, noise, (im.size[1], im.size[0]))
    return Image.fromarray(np.clip(a, 0, 255).astype(np.uint8))
print("\nword recall against the clean OCR:")
for name, im in (("clean page", img), ("rotated 3 degrees", scan(3, 0, 0)),
                 ("blur 1.5 px", scan(0, 1.5, 0)), ("rotated 3, blur 1, noise sd 25", scan(3, 1, 25)),
                 ("rotated 3, blur 1, noise sd 40", scan(3, 1, 40)),
                 ("half resolution (75 dpi)", img.resize((620, 435))),
                 ("third resolution (50 dpi)", img.resize((413, 290)))):
    print(f"  {name:<30} {word_recall(im):6.0%}")

Reading this output

On a clean page, OCR is nearly free and exact. Tesseract finds 58 words, each with a pixel box and a confidence of 90 or more, and its text matches what was drawn. This is step 2 of the Era-1 pipeline, and on clean printed pages it is hard to beat for cost.

Text alone loses the meaning. A regex for the first dollar amount returns $189.00, the unit price of the drill. The total is the amount on the same row as "Total", to the right of it: one comparison of box coordinates finds $1,022.40. Real pipelines are many such rules, one per field and layout, which is why they are fragile.

OCR degrades, then falls off a cliff. A 3-degree skew costs 5% of the words, a 1.5-pixel blur 2%, and skew, blur and moderate noise together 7%. With stronger noise (standard deviation 40 instead of 25), recall drops to 0%: the page no longer segments into text lines, so nothing is read. Resolution behaves the same way: at 75 dpi (body-text x-height about 5 pixels) 93% survives; at 50 dpi (about 4 pixels) only 7%. Each failure needs its own fix (deskewing, denoising, upscaling), the "custom exception paths" of Era 1.

LayoutLMv3's three streams, and what a page costs

Tesseract reads a small form; LayoutLMv3's real processor (microsoft/layoutlmv3-base, tokenizer and image settings only) turns the words, boxes and image into its three inputs, and the model is built on PyTorch's meta device to count its parameters. Then one A4 page is sized for each kind of model: visual tokens and how tall 10-point text ends up in pixels. Qwen2.5-VL uses its real resizing function; the Claude row approximates its documented limits. A few seconds.

import math, torch
import pytesseract
from PIL import Image, ImageDraw, ImageFont
from transformers import AutoConfig, AutoProcessor, LayoutLMv3Model
from transformers.models.qwen2_vl.image_processing_qwen2_vl import smart_resize

# A small form: OCR words and boxes, as an Era-1 pipeline would hand them over.
img = Image.new("RGB", (900, 260), "white")
d = ImageDraw.Draw(img)
f = ImageFont.truetype("/usr/share/fonts/truetype/dejavu/DejaVuSans.ttf", 24)
d.text((40, 40), "Invoice number: INV-2026-0042", font=f, fill="black")
d.text((40, 100), "Customer: Northwind Garden Supplies", font=f, fill="black")
d.text((560, 180), "Total: $1,022.40", font=f, fill="black")
t = pytesseract.image_to_data(img, output_type=pytesseract.Output.DICT)
W, H = img.size
words, boxes = [], []
for w, l, tp, wd, h in zip(t["text"], t["left"], t["top"], t["width"], t["height"]):
    if w.strip():
        words.append(w)                                  # LayoutLMv3 wants boxes on a 0-1000 grid
        boxes.append([int(1000 * l / W), int(1000 * tp / H), int(1000 * (l + wd) / W), int(1000 * (tp + h) / H)])

proc = AutoProcessor.from_pretrained("microsoft/layoutlmv3-base", apply_ocr=False)
enc = proc(img, words, boxes=boxes, return_tensors="pt")
ids, bb, px = enc["input_ids"][0], enc["bbox"][0], enc["pixel_values"]
print(f"stream 1, text : {len(ids)} tokens for {len(words)} OCR words")
print(f"stream 2, boxes: {tuple(bb.shape)}, one 0-1000 box per token")
print(f"stream 3, image: {tuple(px.shape)} -> (224/16)^2 = {(224 // 16) ** 2} patches")
print("\n  token        box (x0, y0, x1, y1)")
for tok, b in list(zip(proc.tokenizer.convert_ids_to_tokens(ids), bb.tolist()))[:14]:
    print(f"  {tok:<12} {b}")

cfg = AutoConfig.from_pretrained("microsoft/layoutlmv3-base")
with torch.device("meta"):
    n = sum(p.numel() for p in LayoutLMv3Model(cfg).parameters())
print(f"\nLayoutLMv3-base: {n/1e6:.0f}M parameters (built on the meta device, no weights)")

# What one A4 page costs each family, and how big 10-pt text ends up in pixels.
PAGE_IN = (8.27, 11.69)                                  # A4, inches (width, height)
def xheight(px_tall):                                    # 10-pt text, x-height about half the em
    return 0.5 * (10 / 72) * px_tall / PAGE_IN[1]
def claude(w, h):   # approximates the documented limits: 28-px patches, 2,576 px and 4,784 tokens max
    s = min(1, 2576 / max(w, h))
    while math.ceil(w * s / 28) * math.ceil(h * s / 28) > 4784:
        s *= 0.99
    return math.ceil(w * s / 28) * math.ceil(h * s / 28), h * s
print(f"\n{'model input for one A4 page':<44}{'visual tokens':>14}{'10-pt x-height':>16}")
rows = [("LayoutLMv3: 224x224 (+ up to 512 OCR tokens)", 196, 224),
        ("Nougat: 896x672, Swin /32", (896 // 32) * (672 // 32), 896),
        ("Donut: 2560x1920, Swin /32", (2560 // 32) * (1920 // 32), 2560),
        ("DeepSeek-OCR Small: 640x640", 100, 640),
        ("DeepSeek-OCR Base: 1024x1024", 256, 1024)]
for dpi in (100, 150, 300):
    w, h = round(PAGE_IN[0] * dpi), round(PAGE_IN[1] * dpi)
    rh, rw = smart_resize(h, w, factor=28, min_pixels=56 * 56, max_pixels=12845056)
    rows.append((f"Qwen2.5-VL, page at {dpi} dpi ({w}x{h})", (rh // 28) * (rw // 28), rh))
    n_c, ch = claude(w, h)
    rows.append((f"Claude 4.7+, page at {dpi} dpi", n_c, ch))
for name, n, tall in rows:
    print(f"{name:<44}{n:>14,}{xheight(tall):>14.1f}px")

Reading this output

Three streams, aligned by position. 9 OCR words become 28 text tokens, because the RoBERTa-style tokenizer splits "Invoice" into "ĠInv" + "oice" and the invoice number into 7 pieces. Every piece carries its word's box on the 0–1000 grid, so the model knows that all of "INV-2026-0042" sits at the same place. The image adds 196 patches from a 224×224 copy of the page. The base model has 125M parameters as built here (the paper's 133M counts slightly differently).

LayoutLMv3's own image cannot read. Shrunk to 224 pixels, 10-point text is 1.3 pixels tall. The patches tell the model where blocks, lines and tables are; the words themselves must come from OCR. That is why it is an Era-1 model with a better back end, not an OCR-free one.

OCR-free models pay for legibility in tokens. Nougat's 896-pixel page leaves an x-height of 5.3 pixels for 588 tokens, enough for clean printed papers. Donut's 2560-pixel page gives 15.2 pixels but 4,800 tokens. DeepSeek-OCR's Small mode (100 tokens) leaves 3.8 pixels: below what Tesseract could read in the last run, so its compact modes rely on a decoder trained to read very small text.

A VLM's cost follows the resolution you send. Qwen2.5-VL takes the page as given: 1,260 tokens at 100 dpi, 2,772 at 150 and 11,125 at 300, four times the tokens for twice the resolution. Claude's limits cap the cost at about 4,800 tokens; a 300-dpi page is scaled down to an x-height of about 14 pixels. Around 150 dpi (10 pixels) is usually plenty for body text; small print and dense tables are the reason to go higher.

Idea 5 · DocLLM (2023)

DocLLM (Wang et al., JPMorgan AI Research, arXiv:2401.00908, submitted 31 December 2023, so the ID carries a January 2024 prefix) is LayoutLM's generative sibling. It has no image encoder at all: it reads OCR text plus bounding boxes. The boxes get their own embeddings, and attention gets four terms (text-text, text-box, box-text, box-box). It is pretrained by filling in masked text blocks and built on Falcon-1B and Llama 2 7B. Better for QA on documents; still depends on OCR input.

Idea 6 · Era 3 — VLM-native (2024+)

2024 VLMs became good enough to replace the pipeline entirely. Feed the full page image at high resolution to a VLM, ask the question, get an answer.

  • LLaVA-NeXT 336-tile AnyRes works for small documents.
  • Qwen2.5-VL reads the page at native resolution, one token per 28×28 pixels (Lesson 12.9).
  • Claude 4.7 and later accept images up to 2,576 pixels on the long edge (at most about 4,784 visual tokens).
  • PaliGemma 2 (December 2024) added transfer results on text detection and recognition, table structure, molecular structures and music scores, at up to 896 pixels.

The gap between VLM-native and OCR-pipeline closed rapidly. By 2026, VLM-native wins on:

  • Scene text (hand-written + printed, mixed scripts).
  • Complex tables with merged cells.
  • Math equations embedded in text.
  • Figures with text annotations.

OCR pipelines still win on:

  • Pure-scan workloads at massive scale where per-page latency matters.
  • Pipeline reliability (deterministic failures vs VLM hallucinations).
  • Regulated environments requiring auditable OCR output.

Idea 7 · The Claude 4.7 / GPT-5 frontier

At high resolution, frontier VLMs read documents very well, near the top of the standard benchmarks. Vendors publish their current scores on their model pages. Open reference points:

  • DocVQA (ANLS): Qwen2.5-VL-72B 96.4 (validation set), LayoutLMv3-Large with OCR 83.4, Donut 67.5.
  • ChartQA: Qwen2.5-VL-72B 89.5.
  • OmniDocBench v1.5 (whole-page parsing into text, tables and formulas), as reported by PaddleOCR-VL's authors: PaddleOCR-VL (0.9B) 92.6, MinerU2.5 (1.2B) 90.7, Gemini 2.5 Pro 88.0, GPT-4o 75.0.

DocVQA is close to saturated. For whole-page conversion, small specialised open models now match or beat general frontier models, because they are trained on exactly that output format.

Idea 8 · Math equations and LaTeX output

Scientific papers need exact LaTeX output for equations. Nougat was trained on this, and its successors among document parsers (MinerU, PaddleOCR-VL, olmOCR and others) produce usable LaTeX for formulas. Without explicit LaTeX training, VLMs produce readable but imprecise transcriptions.

A common scientific-paper pipeline in 2026: a document parser for the whole PDF, then a larger VLM on the pages it flags as hard (dense formulas, complex figures).

Idea 9 · Handwriting

Still the hardest sub-task. Mixed printed + handwritten (doctors' notes, filled forms) is where OCR pipelines with a handwriting recognizer can still win on cost. General VLMs have improved fast on handwriting; test on your own documents, since public benchmarks rarely match a specific hand or form.

Idea 10 · 2026 recipe

For a new document-AI project:

  • Pure-printed invoices at scale: LayoutLMv3 + rules, cost-efficient.
  • Mixed documents (scientific + handwritten + forms): VLM-native, either a document parser (PaddleOCR-VL, MinerU, Docling) or a general VLM (Qwen2.5-VL, Qwen3-VL).
  • Full arXiv ingestion: a parser with LaTeX output for text and math, a VLM for figures.
  • Regulatory: OCR pipeline + VLM validator for cross-check.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Small open document parsers convert pages to Markdown, JSON or HTML with tables and formulas: PaddleOCR-VL (0.9B, October 2025, 109 languages; a layout model then a VLM per element), MinerU2.5 (1.2B), IBM's Granite-Docling (258M, September 2025, used inside the Docling toolkit, Apache 2.0) and olmOCR 2 (a Qwen2.5-VL-7B fine-tune from AI2, trained with unit-test rewards). The sub-1B ones run on a laptop GPU or a CPU; a 7B model needs 4-bit on an 8 GB card.
  • Fewer tokens per page: DeepSeek-OCR (October 2025) encodes a page in 64 to 400 visual tokens and reports about 97% decoding precision at up to 10× compression, about 60% at 20×.
  • General VLMs on page images: Qwen2.5-VL and Qwen3-VL (2B to 8B sizes fit a laptop in 4-bit) and hosted models from Anthropic, OpenAI and Google, which accept PDFs or page images directly. Check each vendor's page for its current document scores.
  • Classic OCR still runs at scale: Tesseract, PaddleOCR and the cloud OCR services (Azure Document Intelligence, Google Document AI, Amazon Textract) remain the cheap, auditable first stage for high-volume printed documents.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Documents carry meaning in layout, tables and formulas, not just words.
  • Era 1: OCR gives words and boxes; rules or LayoutLMv3 use the boxes to find fields.
  • Era 2: Donut and Nougat map pixels straight to structured text, as specialists.
  • Era 3: VLMs read the page image at high resolution; small parsers convert whole pages.
  • Resolution decides legibility, and tokens grow with its square: size pages on purpose.
  • ANLS rewards near-misses; check extracted numbers exactly.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · The regex finds the wrong amount; OCR fails at cliffs

No. The first dollar amount in the text is $189.00, the drill's unit price. Plain OCR text has no notion of "the number next to Total"; you need the boxes (or a model that sees the layout). A one-line rule on the coordinates finds $1,022.40.

OCR held up well to each problem on its own: 95% of words after a 3-degree skew, 98% after a 1.5-pixel blur, 93% at 75 dpi. But it failed abruptly: skew, blur and heavier noise together dropped recall to 0%, and 50 dpi dropped it to 7%. The lesson for production is to measure on degraded samples of your real documents, because the failure is a cliff, not a slope.

SolutionB1 · The small print disappears first

At 640 pixels on the long side, an A4 page is about 55 pixels per inch. Body text at 10 points then has an x-height of about 4 pixels, and 7-point footnotes and small table figures under 3: about where OCR stopped reading at 50 dpi in the first run. Large-print headings survive, so a clean test set with big totals looks fine. On real invoices the model cannot read small figures, and a VLM, unlike OCR, does not return nothing: it produces a plausible-looking number. Wrong totals with high confidence are worse than a visible failure.

How to catch it before production:

  • Compute the legibility first: smallest font on your documents, page size, and the resolution you send give the x-height in pixels (the formula above). Under about 5 pixels, expect errors.
  • Test on real, varied pages, including small print, scans and photos, and score extracted fields exactly (or with arithmetic checks such as subtotal + tax = total), not with ANLS.
  • Spend tokens where they matter: send the page at a readable resolution, or crop the region with the totals at full resolution, or use a parser that keeps resolution per element.
