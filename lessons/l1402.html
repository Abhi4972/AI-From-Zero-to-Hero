Why this came about

Everything before this lesson is about building an agent that works. Most agents that work are still never used, or are used and then switched off, and the reason is rarely the model. Nobody chose the use case on purpose, nobody wrote down what it had to earn, or nobody owned it after launch. This lesson is the part of the work that happens around the engineering.

In plain words first
A shop decides whether to hire

Think of an agent as a new member of staff. Before hiring, a sensible manager asks four questions. Which job needs doing most? What will the hire cost, and what will the work be worth? Should we hire, or pay an agency? Who will manage this person? An AI system gets the same four questions, and this lesson takes them in that order.

The problem: a good demo is not a business case

ShopKart's support assistant from the capstone answers order questions well. A director now asks three things the demo cannot answer: Why this use case first? What is it worth, and when does it pay back? Should we have bought one? Each has an honest answer made of a few numbers, and each answer is a range, because the numbers that matter are not known until the system runs.

Step 1 · Choose the use case

List the candidates and score each on three things. Value is the work it could take over, in money per month. Feasibility asks whether the data exists and whether an answer can be checked: an answer you cannot check cannot be evaluated, and a system you cannot evaluate cannot be improved. Harm is how bad a wrong answer is.

# Step 1 · Score candidate use cases on value, feasibility and risk
CANDIDATES = [
    # name,                          volume/month, minutes saved each, data ready (0-1), checkable (0-1), harm if wrong (1-5)
    ("order-status answers",              120_000,  3,  0.9, 0.9, 1),
    ("refund decisions under INR 2,000",   18_000,  6,  0.8, 0.8, 3),
    ("supplier contract review",              400, 90,  0.5, 0.3, 4),
    ("marketing copy drafts",               2_500, 20,  0.7, 0.4, 2),
    ("credit-limit changes",                3_000, 10,  0.6, 0.6, 5),
]
COST_PER_HOUR = 350          # INR, loaded cost of the people who do this work today

rows = []
for name, volume, minutes, data, checkable, harm in CANDIDATES:
    value = volume * minutes / 60 * COST_PER_HOUR            # INR a month of work it could take over
    feasibility = data * checkable                            # is the data there, and can an answer be checked?
    score = value * feasibility / harm                        # harm divides: risky work needs more value to go first
    rows.append((score, name, value, feasibility, harm))

print(f"{'use case':<34}{'value/month':>13}{'feasible':>10}{'harm':>6}{'score':>12}")
for score, name, value, feasibility, harm in sorted(rows, reverse=True):
    print(f"{name:<34}{value:>13,.0f}{feasibility:>10.2f}{harm:>6}{score:>12,.0f}")

Reading this output
  • The first use case is the boring one. Order status wins by a factor of twelve: the volume is high, the answer is in a database, it can be checked exactly, and a wrong answer costs little. The exciting candidate, contract review, comes last, because few contracts arrive and nobody can cheaply check a review.
  • The score is a way to argue, not a verdict. The inputs are estimates. Its use is that a team must write down volume, checkability and harm for every candidate, and those three numbers are where the disagreements are.
  • Harm sets the level of autonomy, not only the order. Credit-limit changes can still be a good second project as a draft for a person to approve (Lesson 14.148). High harm rules out acting alone. It does not rule out helping.

Step 2 · Write the business case as a range

A business case has a one-off cost to build and a monthly result. The monthly result depends mostly on the automation rate: the share of cases the system resolves with no person involved. It is the number you know least before launch, so compute the case for several values of it.

# Step 2 · The business case for one use case, with a range and not a single number
def monthly_case(volume, automation_rate, human_cost, ai_cost, error_rate, cost_per_error, run_cost):
    """Net saving a month. automation_rate: share of cases the system resolves without a person."""
    resolved = volume * automation_rate
    saved = resolved * (human_cost - ai_cost)                 # work the system took over
    escalated = (volume - resolved) * ai_cost                 # cases that still went to a person: the AI cost is extra
    errors = resolved * error_rate * cost_per_error           # wrong resolutions have to be found and put right
    return saved - escalated - errors - run_cost

BASE = dict(volume=120_000, human_cost=17.5, ai_cost=1.2, error_rate=0.01, cost_per_error=250, run_cost=450_000)
BUILD_COST = 6_000_000                                        # INR, one-off: build, evals, integration, launch

print(f"{'automation':>10}{'net per month':>16}{'payback':>12}")
for rate in (0.30, 0.45, 0.60, 0.75):
    net = monthly_case(automation_rate=rate, **BASE)
    payback = f"{BUILD_COST / net:.1f} months" if net > 0 else "never"
    print(f"{rate:>10.0%}{net:>16,.0f}{payback:>12}")

print()
print("the same case at 60% automation, as the error rate moves:")
for err in (0.005, 0.01, 0.03, 0.06):
    net = monthly_case(automation_rate=0.60, **{**BASE, "error_rate": err})
    print(f"  {err:.1%} wrong resolutions -> net {net:>10,.0f} a month")

Reading this output
  • The same system loses money or pays back in eight months. At 30% automation the running cost is larger than the saving. At 75% the build is paid for in under a year. Nothing about the model changed between those rows. Present the range, and say which automation rate the pilot must show before the next stage is funded.
  • Escalated cases cost extra. A case the system tried and then handed over was paid for twice: once for the model, once for the person. This is why a low automation rate is worse than it looks.
  • Wrong answers can cancel the saving. At 60% automation, moving from 1% to 6% wrong resolutions turns a profit of 486,000 into a loss. Each wrong resolution costs INR 250 to find and put right, against a saving of about INR 16 on a good one. The eval set and the release gate of the Production engineering and AI system design section are what protect the business case.
  • Every input is a measurement you will have. Volume and human cost come from today's operation. Automation rate, error rate and AI cost per case come from the pilot, in the form of Lesson 14.142's cost per resolved outcome.
Watch out
The automation rate is not the goal

A system can raise its automation rate by refusing to hand over, and customers pay for it. Report the automation rate together with a quality measure such as wrong resolutions, repeat contacts or satisfaction, and an escape route to a person that works (Lesson 14.148). Klarna's public account of its assistant, in Lesson 14.66, is the standard case study.

Step 3 · Build, buy or both

There are three routes to the same job. A product is quick to start and is priced per resolution. Building on a cloud agent platform (the Cloud platforms, compared section) costs more up front and less per resolution. Hosting the model yourself (Lesson 14.137) costs the most to set up and run, and the least per resolution.

# Step 3 · Build or buy: three-year cost of each route for the same job
def three_year_cost(one_off, per_month, per_resolution, volume, years=3):
    return one_off + years * 12 * (per_month + per_resolution * volume)

VOLUME = 72_000                    # resolutions a month (120,000 conversations at 60% automation)
ROUTES = {
    "buy a support-agent product":   dict(one_off=800_000,   per_month=150_000, per_resolution=9.0),
    "build on a cloud agent platform": dict(one_off=6_000_000, per_month=450_000, per_resolution=1.2),
    "build and self-host the model": dict(one_off=9_000_000, per_month=900_000, per_resolution=0.3),
}
for name, r in ROUTES.items():
    print(f"{name:<34} INR {three_year_cost(volume=VOLUME, **r) / 1e6:6.1f} million over 3 years")

print()
buy, build = ROUTES["buy a support-agent product"], ROUTES["build on a cloud agent platform"]
for volume in (5_000, 20_000, 72_000):
    cheaper = "buy" if three_year_cost(volume=volume, **buy) < three_year_cost(volume=volume, **build) else "build"
    print(f"at {volume:>6,} resolutions a month: {cheaper} is cheaper")

build and self-host the model      INR   42.2 million over 3 years

at  5,000 resolutions a month: buy is cheaper

Reading this output
  • Volume decides between buying and building. A price per resolution is cheap at low volume and expensive at high volume. At ShopKart's 72,000 resolutions a month, building on a platform is cheaper over three years. At 20,000, buying is.
  • Self-hosting loses here on cost. Its low price per resolution does not repay the fixed cost at this volume. Teams usually self-host for other reasons: data that may not leave, latency, or a fine-tuned small model (Lesson 3.25).

Cost is one of four questions. The others often decide:

Question Points toward buying Points toward building
Is this work what makes your company different? No: it is the same job everywhere, such as password resets Yes: your policies, data and tools are the product
How much must it connect to your own systems? A little, through connectors the product already has Deeply: your order system, your approvals, your permissions
Who must be able to change it, and how fast? A business team, through settings Engineers, in code, with your own evals and release gates
What must you be able to prove? The vendor's certifications are enough You need your own traces, audit log and data controls

Many organisations do both: they buy for common work, and build where their own data and rules are the point. Before you sign for a product, ask the questions of Lesson 14.151's design review: where the data goes and whether it is used for training, what happens when the service is down, how you get your traces and your data out, and what a wrong answer costs under the contract.

Step 4 · The operating model: who owns what

One team with one agent needs no operating model. The second and third team make the same decisions again, differently, unless someone owns the shared parts. The arrangement that most organisations reach has three layers:

Layer Owns Does not own
A central platform team (often called a centre of excellence at the start) The paved path of Lesson 14.149: gateway, identity, runtime, tracing, eval tooling, the agent registry, shared guardrails The use cases
Product teams Their agent end to end: the use case, the eval set, the release gate, on-call, the outcome metric The platform
Risk, legal and security The tiering rules of Lesson 15.26 and the review for high-risk tiers Day-to-day releases of low-risk agents

Two rules keep this working. Each agent has one named owner who is on call for it, and the registry refuses agents without one. Low-risk agents on the paved path ship without a committee, so that the review time goes to the few systems that need it.

Step 5 · Adoption: the part nobody budgets for

A system that works and is not used earns nothing. Treat adoption as something you measure, in the same way as quality:

  • Measure use, not availability. Count the share of eligible work that goes through the system each week, and the share of users who come back. A flat line after launch is a finding.
  • Put it where the work already happens. An assistant inside the support console is used. The same assistant on a separate page is forgotten.
  • Show what it did. People trust a system whose work they can see, correct and stop (Lesson 14.148). Hidden automation is switched off after its first visible mistake.
  • Give the people affected a say. Support agents know which answers are wrong and why. Their corrections are your best eval cases (Lesson 14.131), and a team that helped shape a tool uses it.
  • Retire what is not used. Every agent left running costs money and attention. The registry of Lesson 14.149 should show usage, and unused agents should be removed.

A maturity ladder

Use this to say where an organisation is and what the next step is. Each step adds one thing.

Stage What exists What to add next
1 · Experiments Demos by individuals, no shared tooling One use case chosen on purpose, with a metric (Step 1)
2 · One system in production An eval set, a release gate, an owner on call A business case measured against the pilot (Step 2)
3 · Several systems Each team built its own stack A shared platform and a registry (Step 4, Lesson 14.149)
4 · A platform Paved path, tiered review, cost per outcome by team Adoption and outcome metrics reviewed like revenue (Step 5)
5 · Managed like a product line Use cases are added and retired on evidence Keep the evidence honest
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Start with the most impressive use case."
    ✓ Start with the one that has high volume, an answer you can check, and a low cost when it is wrong.
  • ✗ "A business case is one number."
    ✓ It is a range that depends on the automation rate and the error cost, and the pilot's job is to narrow it.
  • ✗ "Buying is always cheaper than building."
    ✓ It is cheaper at low volume. A price per resolution grows with success.
  • ✗ "Once it works, people will use it."

Exercises

Predict before you run

In Step 2, the AI cost per case doubles from INR 1.2 to INR 2.4. At 60% automation, does the net result fall by more or by less than a doubling of the error rate from 1% to 2%? Work it out, then check with the function. (Solution P1.)

Break it on purpose

Give "supplier contract review" a checkability of 0.9 in Step 1 and run the ranking again. Where does it land, and what would have to be true in the real world for that number to be honest? (Solution B1.)

Debugging exercise

A team reports an automation rate of 82% and a strong business case. Three months later, repeat contacts are up by a third. What was measured wrongly, and what would you add to the report? (Solution D1.)

Make it your own

Replace the candidates in Step 1 with five from your own organisation, using real volumes. Then fill in Step 2 for the top one and write down the automation rate at which it pays back within twelve months. (Solution M1.)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · AI cost against error rate

Doubling the AI cost adds INR 1.2 for every one of the 120,000 cases, resolved or not: 144,000 a month. Doubling the error rate adds 72,000 resolved cases x 1% x INR 250 = 180,000 a month. The error rate hurts more, although the AI cost is the number people watch. With these inputs the net falls from 486,000 to 342,000 in the first case and to 306,000 in the second.

SolutionB1 · An honest checkability

With checkability 0.9 its feasibility becomes 0.45 and its score 23,625, which moves it above credit-limit changes and still far below order status, because only 400 contracts arrive a month. For 0.9 to be honest, you would need a set of contracts already reviewed by lawyers, with the issues marked, so that a review can be graded against them. Building that set is the real first project.

SolutionD1 · Automation that customers paid for

The automation rate counted every conversation that ended without a handover as resolved, including the ones where the customer gave up and wrote in again. Report resolution confirmed by an outcome (the order was found, the refund was issued, no repeat contact within some days), and show the automation rate next to repeat contacts and wrong resolutions. Then check whether the handover to a person works.

SolutionM1 · Your own case

There is no single answer. A good one names where each input came from (a report, a sample, an estimate), gives the payback at three automation rates, and states which rate the pilot must reach. If the top candidate has high harm, the answer should also say what the person in the loop approves.

Check your understanding

Explain the mechanismHow would you choose the first AI use case for a team?

Worked answer. List the candidates and write down, for each, the monthly volume, the time each case takes today, whether the data exists, whether an answer can be checked, and what a wrong answer costs. Start with high volume, a checkable answer and low harm, because that system can be evaluated and improved, and its mistakes are cheap while the team learns. Keep the high-harm candidates as drafts for a person to approve.

A plausible wrong answer. "Pick the one the leadership is most excited about." Excitement tracks novelty. The exciting cases tend to have low volume and answers nobody can check.

Follow-ups. What would make you choose a lower-scoring use case first? How do you find volume if nobody measures it?

Debug a failureThe pilot worked and the rollout is losing money. Where do you look?

Worked answer. Compare the business case's inputs with what production shows: the automation rate, the cost per case including escalated ones, the rate and cost of wrong resolutions, and the running cost of the team. The usual finding is that the pilot ran on easy cases, so the automation rate fell on real traffic, or that wrong resolutions were not counted at all.

A plausible wrong answer. "Switch to a cheaper model." The model's price is rarely the largest term. A cheaper model that resolves fewer cases makes the result worse.

Follow-ups. Which of these numbers can you measure in the first week? What would you show a finance director?

Defend a designDefend building over buying for ShopKart's support assistant.

Worked answer. At 72,000 resolutions a month the platform route is cheaper over three years than a product priced per resolution, and the gap widens as volume grows. The assistant must call ShopKart's own order and refund systems under ShopKart's permissions and approval rules, engineers need to change it weekly behind their own release gate, and the company wants its own traces and audit log. Each of those points toward building. I would still buy for work that is the same everywhere.

A plausible wrong answer. "We should own all our AI." Ownership has a running cost: an on-call rota, evals, upgrades. It is worth paying where the work is specific to the company.

Follow-ups. At what volume would you reverse the decision? What would you ask a vendor before signing?
