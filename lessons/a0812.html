In plain words first
The idea in one line

3D is the modality where 2D-to-3D leverage is strongest. The 2023 breakthrough was 3D Gaussian Splatting. The 2024-2026 generative push layers multi-view diffusion + 3D reconstruction on top to produce objects and scenes from a single prompt or photo.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
3D Gaussian Splatting "3DGS" Scene as a cloud of 3D Gaussians; differentiable alpha-composite render.
NeRF "Neural radiance field" MLP that outputs color + density at a 3D point; render by ray integration.
Triplane "Three 2-D planes" Factor 3D into three 2-D axis-aligned feature grids; cheaper than volumetric.
SDS "Score distillation sampling" Train 3D model by using 2D-diffusion score as pseudo-gradient.

PBR "Physically-based rendering" Material with albedo, roughness, metallic, normal channels.
Densification "Grow splats" 3DGS training heuristic: split / clone splats in high-gradient regions.

The problem

In plain words first

A 3D object or scene has to look right from every direction, and there is far less 3D training data than 2D images. Most 3D generators therefore lean on 2D: a diffusion model imagines the object from several viewpoints, and a reconstruction step turns those views into 3D, or a 3D model is compressed into a latent space and diffused there directly, as images are. A popular 3D format, Gaussian splatting, describes a scene as a cloud of soft, coloured, see-through blobs that can be drawn very fast from any camera. This lesson renders a few 3D Gaussians the way splatting does (projection, depth sorting, see-through blending) and shows why the lesson's simple "add them up" rendering gets occlusion wrong, then lifts a real photo to 3D with a depth-estimation model and turns the camera: the holes that appear are exactly what single-image 3D generators must invent. Nothing is trained.

3D content is painful:

  • Representation. Meshes, point clouds, voxel grids, signed distance fields (SDFs), neural radiance fields (NeRFs), 3D Gaussians. Each has trade-offs.
  • Data scarcity. ImageNet has 14M images. The largest clean 3D dataset (Objaverse-XL, 2023) has ~10M objects, most low quality.
  • Memory. A 512³ voxel grid is 128M voxels; a useful scene NeRF needs 1M samples/ray. Generation is harder than reconstruction.
  • Supervision. For a 2D image you have the pixels. For 3D you usually have a handful of 2D views and have to lift to 3D.

Two recipes dominate. One separates the problems: first generate 2D multi-view images with a diffusion model, then fit or predict a 3D representation (often Gaussian splatting) from them. The other, used by Hunyuan3D 2 and TRELLIS, trains a 3D VAE and runs diffusion directly in its 3D latent space, then decodes a mesh or Gaussians.

The idea

Idea 1 · Representation: 3D Gaussian Splatting (Kerbl et al., 2023)

Represent a scene as a cloud of ~1M 3D Gaussians. Each has 59 parameters: position (3), covariance (6, or quaternion 4 + scale 3), opacity (1), spherical-harmonics color (48 at degree 3, 3 at degree 0).

Rendering = projection + alpha-compositing. Fast (~100 fps at 1080p on a 4090). Differentiable. Fit by gradient descent against ground-truth photos. A scene fits in 5-30 minutes on a consumer GPU.

Two 2023-2024 innovations on top: - Generative Gaussian splats. Models like LGM, LRM, InstantMesh predict a Gaussian cloud directly from one or a few images. - 4D Gaussian Splatting. Gaussians with per-frame offsets for dynamic scenes.

Idea 2 · Multi-view diffusion

Fine-tune a pretrained image diffusion model to generate multiple consistent views of the same object from a text prompt or single image. Zero123 (Liu et al., 2023), MVDream (Shi et al., 2023), SV3D (Stability, 2024), CAT3D (Google, 2024). Usually output 4-16 views around the object, lifted to 3D via Gaussian splatting or NeRF.

Idea 4 · NeRF (for context)

Neural Radiance Field (Mildenhall et al., 2020). A tiny MLP takes (x, y, z, view direction) and outputs (color, density). Render by integrating along rays. Beats mesh-based novel-view synthesis in quality but is 100-1000x slower to render. Superseded by Gaussian splatting for most real-time use but still dominant in research.

The same idea as a formula

A 3D Gaussian has mean μ∈R3 and covariance Σ=RSS⊤R⊤ (R from a unit quaternion, S diagonal scales), so it stays a valid, positive-definite ellipsoid during optimisation. Its density is G(x)=exp(−21​(x−μ)⊤Σ−1(x−μ)).

Projection (EWA splatting). With world-to-camera rotation W and J the Jacobian of the perspective projection at the Gaussian's centre,

Σ′=JWΣW⊤J⊤

is the 2×2 covariance of its footprint on the screen.

Alpha compositing. Sort the Gaussians by depth; for a pixel with front-to-back footprints αi​=oi​Gi′​(pixel) and colours ci​:

C=i∑​ci​αi​j<i∏​(1−αj​)

The product is the light that survives everything in front. Summing ∑i​ci​αi​, as the lesson's toy does, ignores occlusion.

NeRF is the continuous version: C=∫T(t)σ(r(t))c(r(t),d)dt with T(t)=exp(−∫0t​σ), evaluated by sampling points along each ray through an MLP.

Score distillation (SDS). Optimise 3D parameters θ so that renders x=g(θ) look likely to a 2D diffusion model: ∇θ​L=Et,ε​[w(t)(εϕ​(xt​;y,t)−ε)∂x/∂θ].

Worked example
Compositing and size by hand

One pixel, two splats. Front splat red with α=0.8, back splat blue with α=0.8:

  • Composited: 0.8⋅red+(1−0.8)⋅0.8⋅blue=0.8red+0.16blue: mostly red, as an opaque object in front should be.
  • Summed: 0.8red+0.8blue: a magenta mix brighter than either, with no notion of in front.

Parameters. Position 3 + rotation quaternion 4 + scales 3 + opacity 1 + colour as degree-3 spherical harmonics 16×3=48: 59 numbers per Gaussian. A one-million-Gaussian scene: 59×106×4 bytes ≈225 MB in fp32.

Why the screen covariance changes with distance. For a Gaussian at depth z with focal length f, J≈f/z in the image directions: twice as far away, its footprint is half as wide.

Step 1 · 2D Gaussian splat

code/main.py implements a toy 2D "Gaussian splatting" fit: represent a synthetic target image (a smooth gradient) as a sum of 2D Gaussian splats. Optimize positions, colors, and covariances by gradient descent to match the target. You see the two core operations: forward render (splat + alpha-composite) and fit by gradient descent.

def gaussian_at(x, y, gaussian):
    px, py = gaussian["pos"]
    sigma = gaussian["sigma"]
    d2 = (x - px) ** 2 + (y - py) ** 2
    return math.exp(-d2 / (2 * sigma * sigma))

Step 2 · render by summing splats

def render(image_size, gaussians):
    img = [[0.0] * image_size for _ in range(image_size)]
    for g in gaussians:
        for y in range(image_size):
            for x in range(image_size):
                img[y][x] += g["color"] * gaussian_at(x, y, g)
    return img

Real 3D Gaussian splatting sorts Gaussians by depth and alpha-composites in order. Our 2D toy just sums.

Step 3 · fit by gradient descent

for step in range(steps):
    pred = render(size, gaussians)
    loss = mse(pred, target)
    gradients = compute_grads(pred, target, gaussians)
    update(gaussians, gradients, lr)

import numpy as np, matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Three 3D Gaussians: a big red one in front, a green one behind it, a blue flat one further back.
mu = np.array([[0.0, 0.0, 3.0], [0.35, 0.1, 4.0], [-0.3, -0.2, 5.0]])
scales = np.array([[0.25, 0.25, 0.25], [0.3, 0.3, 0.3], [0.6, 0.4, 0.05]])
color = np.array([[0.9, 0.15, 0.1], [0.1, 0.8, 0.2], [0.15, 0.3, 0.95]])
opacity = np.array([0.9, 0.9, 0.9])
Sigma = np.array([np.diag(s ** 2) for s in scales])          # covariance = R S S^T R^T (no rotation here)

def render(yaw_deg, composite=True, size=96, f=110.0):
    """EWA splatting: project each 3D Gaussian to a 2D Gaussian, sort by depth, alpha-composite front to back."""
    a = np.radians(yaw_deg)
    R = np.array([[np.cos(a), 0, np.sin(a)], [0, 1, 0], [-np.sin(a), 0, np.cos(a)]])   # camera turned around the y axis
    centre = np.array([0, 0, 4.0])                                                     # orbit around the scene's middle
    pts = (mu - centre) @ R.T + centre
    order = np.argsort(pts[:, 2])                                                      # nearest first
    ys, xs = np.mgrid[0:size, 0:size] - size / 2 + 0.5
    img = np.zeros((size, size, 3)); T = np.ones((size, size))                         # T = light still passing through
    for i in order:
        x, y, z = pts[i]
        J = np.array([[f / z, 0, -f * x / z ** 2], [0, f / z, -f * y / z ** 2]])       # Jacobian of the projection
        S2 = J @ R @ Sigma[i] @ R.T @ J.T + 0.3 * np.eye(2)                           # 2D covariance on the screen
        u, v = f * x / z, f * y / z
        d = np.stack([xs - u, ys - v], -1)
        g = np.exp(-0.5 * np.einsum("...i,ij,...j->...", d, np.linalg.inv(S2), d))
        alpha = np.clip(opacity[i] * g, 0, 0.99)
        if composite:
            img += (T * alpha)[..., None] * color[i]; T *= 1 - alpha
        else:
            img += alpha[..., None] * color[i]                                         # the lesson's toy: just add
    return np.clip(img, 0, 1), order

views = {}
for name, yaw, comp in (("front, composited", 0, True), ("front, summed", 0, False), ("turned 40°, composited", 40, True)):
    views[name], order = render(yaw, comp)
    print(f"{name:<24} depth order (nearest first): {[('red', 'green', 'blue')[k] for k in order]}")
print(f"parameters per Gaussian: 3 position + 4 rotation + 3 scale + 1 opacity + 48 colour (degree-3 harmonics) = {3 + 4 + 3 + 1 + 48}")
print(f"a 1M-Gaussian scene: {59e6 * 4 / 2**20:.0f} MB in fp32")

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 3, figsize=(8, 2.9))
for ax, (name, im) in zip(axes, views.items()):
    ax.imshow(im); ax.set_title(name, fontsize=8); ax.axis("off")
plt.subplots_adjust(left=0.01, right=0.99, top=0.88, bottom=0.01, wspace=0.04)
plt.savefig("v1.png", dpi=60); plt.show()

Reading this output

From the front, the red Gaussian is nearest, and composited front to back it covers the centre: the green and blue ones show only where they extend beyond it, dimmed by the light the red one already absorbed. The lesson's summed rendering adds the three colours wherever they overlap, giving a bright, whitish magenta-green centre that no real scene would produce; it has no notion of in front. Turning the camera by 40° moves the Gaussians apart on screen, and their projected shapes change because the formula box's Jacobian depends on each Gaussian's position. The depth order is recomputed for every view (here it stays red, green, blue). Each Gaussian carries 59 numbers, so a typical one-million-Gaussian scene is about 225 MB in fp32, small enough to stream to a browser.

Pitfalls

  • View inconsistency. If you generate 4 views independently and they disagree about object structure, the 3D fit is blurry. Fix: multi-view diffusion with shared attention.
  • Back-side hallucination. Single-image → 3D has to invent the unseen side. Quality varies wildly.
  • Gaussian splat explosion. Unconstrained training grows to 10M splats and overfits. Densification + pruning heuristics (from 3D-GS original paper) are essential.
  • Topology issues. Meshes from implicit fields (SDFs) often have holes or self-intersections. Run a remesher (e.g. blender's voxel remesh) before shipping.
  • License of training data. Objaverse has mixed licenses; commercial use varies per model.

From one photo to 3D, and its limits

Depth Anything V2 Small (an open monocular depth model, widely used for 3D photos and depth ControlNets) estimates depth for a photo; the pixels are lifted to a coloured point cloud and re-rendered from cameras turned by 8° and 20°.

import io, numpy as np, torch, matplotlib.pyplot as plt, pyarrow.parquet as pq
from PIL import Image
from huggingface_hub import hf_hub_download
from transformers import pipeline

torch.set_num_threads(2)
depth = pipeline("depth-estimation", model="depth-anything/Depth-Anything-V2-Small-hf")
table = pq.read_table(hf_hub_download("Multimodal-Fatima/Imagenette_validation",
                      "data/validation-00000-of-00001-b518380f1876c09a.parquet", repo_type="dataset"), columns=["image"])
im = Image.open(io.BytesIO(table.column("image")[1200].as_py()["bytes"])).convert("RGB")
s = min(im.size); im = im.crop(((im.width - s) // 2, (im.height - s) // 2, (im.width + s) // 2, (im.height + s) // 2)).resize((256, 256))
pred = np.asarray(depth(im)["predicted_depth"].squeeze(), dtype=np.float32)          # relative inverse depth (bigger = nearer)
pred = np.asarray(Image.fromarray(pred).resize((256, 256)))
print(f"Depth Anything V2 Small: {sum(p.numel() for p in depth.model.parameters()) / 1e6:.0f}M parameters; "
      f"output range {pred.min():.2f} to {pred.max():.2f} (relative, larger = nearer)")

# Lift to 3D: turn relative inverse depth into a plausible metric depth (2 m to 20 m), back-project with a 60° camera.
inv = (pred - pred.min()) / (pred.max() - pred.min())
Z = 1 / (1 / 20 + inv * (1 / 2 - 1 / 20))
f = 128 / np.tan(np.radians(30))
v, u = np.mgrid[0:256, 0:256] - 127.5
P = np.stack([u * Z / f, v * Z / f, Z], -1).reshape(-1, 3); C = np.asarray(im).reshape(-1, 3)
print(f"point cloud: {len(P):,} points, depth {Z.min():.1f} to {Z.max():.1f} m")

def novel_view(yaw_deg, n=128):
    """Re-render the coloured points from a turned camera at half resolution (so neighbouring points leave no gaps)."""
    a = np.radians(yaw_deg); pivot = np.array([0, 0, np.median(Z)])
    R = np.array([[np.cos(a), 0, np.sin(a)], [0, 1, 0], [-np.sin(a), 0, np.cos(a)]])
    Q = (P - pivot) @ R.T + pivot
    fn = f * n / 256
    x = np.round(fn * Q[:, 0] / Q[:, 2] + n / 2 - 0.5).astype(int); y = np.round(fn * Q[:, 1] / Q[:, 2] + n / 2 - 0.5).astype(int)
    ok = np.flatnonzero((Q[:, 2] > 0.1) & (x >= 0) & (x < n) & (y >= 0) & (y < n))
    img = np.zeros((n, n, 3), np.uint8); seen = np.zeros((n, n), bool)
    for i in ok[np.argsort(-Q[ok, 2])]:                                   # far to near: nearer points overwrite (z-buffer)
        img[y[i], x[i]] = C[i]; seen[y[i], x[i]] = True
    return img, 1 - seen.mean()
views = {}
for yaw in (0, 8, 20):
    views[yaw], holes = novel_view(yaw)
    print(f"camera turned {yaw:>2}°: {holes:.0%} of the new view has no point" + ("" if yaw else " (the original viewpoint)"))

fig, axes = plt.subplots(1, 4, figsize=(9, 2.5))
for ax, (title, img) in zip(axes, (("photo", im), ("estimated depth (bright = near)", pred), ("point cloud, turned 8°", views[8]),
                                   ("turned 20°", views[20]))):
    ax.imshow(img, cmap="inferno" if img is pred else None); ax.set_title(title, fontsize=8); ax.axis("off")
plt.subplots_adjust(left=0.01, right=0.99, top=0.88, bottom=0.01, wspace=0.04)
plt.savefig("v2.png", dpi=60); plt.show()

Break it on purpose

In a splatting renderer, sort the Gaussians far-to-near instead of near-to-far but keep the front-to-back formula C=∑i​ci​αi​∏j<i​(1−αj​). What does the picture show? (Solution B1.)

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Gaussian splatting in production capture. Scaniverse, Polycam, Luma and Postshot capture scenes as splats; gsplat and Nerfstudio are the open training libraries, and engines and viewers increasingly load splat files directly.
  • Image-to-3D generators. Hunyuan3D 2.x and TRELLIS (open weights) and hosted services such as Meshy, Tripo and Rodin produce textured meshes from an image or text in seconds to minutes.
  • Depth and normals as building blocks. Depth Anything V2 and similar monocular models supply depth for 3D photos, ControlNet conditioning and quick reconstructions.

Sources: 3D Gaussian Splatting, gsplat, Hunyuan3D 2.1, TRELLIS, Depth Anything V2.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • 3D Gaussians: position, rotation, scales, opacity and colour, 59 numbers each; rendered by projecting and compositing front to back.
  • Screen footprint Σ′=JWΣW⊤J⊤; occlusion comes from the ∏(1−αj​) term.
  • Two generation recipes: multi-view diffusion + reconstruction, or diffusion in a 3D latent space.
  • A single image fixes only visible surfaces; new viewpoints expose regions a generator must invent.
  • Serving splits into fast generation and slower reconstruction or optimisation.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · About 10–25%

About 10–25%: 13% of the view turned by 8° and 24% of the view turned by 20° have no point. Two things cause the holes: parts of the scene outside the original frame come into view at the edge, and regions hidden behind near objects (the gravestones and the cross) are uncovered, "disocclusion". Both grow quickly with the angle, and for a full turn around an object everything on the far side is unknown. Depth estimation gives the shape of what the camera saw, nothing more.

SolutionB1 · Inside-out occlusion

The formula assumes that each term's ∏j<i​(1−αj​) is the light passing everything in front of Gaussian i. Sorted far-to-near, it becomes the light passing everything behind it, so the farthest Gaussian is drawn as if it were nearest and hides everything else: in the run after Step 3, the flat blue Gaussian at the back would cover the red one in front wherever they overlap. Objects look inside-out, and as the camera moves, the wrong things pop in front. Real renderers either sort near-to-far with the front-to-back formula or far-to-near with the "over" operator, C←αi​ci​+(1−αi​)C, never a mix.

Production note: 3D has no shared substrate yet

Unlike image (latent diffusion + DiT) and video (spatiotemporal DiT), 3D has no single dominant runtime in 2026. The production decision tree forks on the representation:

  • NeRF / triplane. Inference is ray-marching + an MLP forward per sample. A 512² render requires millions of MLP forwards. Batch the ray samples aggressively; SDPA/xformers applies.
  • Multi-view diffusion + LRM reconstruction. Two-stage pipeline. Stage 1 (multi-view DiT) is a diffusion server just like Lesson 07. Stage 2 (LRM transformer) is a one-shot forward pass over the views. The overall latency profile is "diffusion + one-shot" — pick per-stage serving primitives accordingly.
  • SDS / DreamFusion. Per-asset optimization, not inference. Build jobs, not request handlers.

For most 2026 products, the right answer is "run a multi-view diffusion model on request, reconstruct to 3DGS asynchronously, serve the 3DGS for real-time viewing". This splits the workload cleanly between a GPU-inference server (fast) and an offline optimizer (slow).
