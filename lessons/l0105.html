Why this came about

In 2020 there was essentially one capable model family; by 2025 there were dozens, from tiny models on phones to frontier models, plus reasoning models that think before answering and open-weight models anyone can run. Choosing a model went from a non-question to a core engineering decision, made per task.

In plain words first
Different tools for different jobs

"The model" is really a family of choices. Frontier models are the largest and most capable, and the most expensive. Reasoning models think before answering, spending extra tokens to do better on hard problems. Small models are cheaper and faster and often good enough for narrow steps such as classifying or extracting. Open-weight models can be run on your own machines for control, cost or data reasons. Real systems often mix several, each where it fits.

Kinds of models

Kind What it is Typical agent use
Frontier model The largest hosted models from Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and others Planning, hard tool choices, writing
Reasoning model Trained to think in hidden tokens before answering, with an effort or budget setting Multi-step problems, complex tool uselater · 14.56. Most frontier models are now hybrids with reasoning on or off.
Small language model (SLM) Compact models (a few billion parameters) Routing, extraction, classification at low cost
Open-weight model Weights you can download and host (Qwen, DeepSeek, Mistral, Gemma, Llama) Data residency, cost at scale, fine-tuning
Multimodal model Reads (and sometimes writes) images, audio, video Documents, screenshots, voice
Embedding model Turns text into a vector of numbers that captures meaning (Lesson 7.22) Search and RAG (the Retrieval (RAG) section)
Reranker Reads a question and a passage together and scores how relevant the passage is The second stage of retrieval (Lesson 5.38)

Many large open models use a mixture-of-experts (MoE) design: only a fraction of the parameters run for each token, so a "400B" model can be cheaper to run than its size suggests. Lesson 7.24 shows how the routing works.

How to read a model name. A name such as qwen3-32b-instruct-q4 packs four facts: the family and generation (Qwen 3), the size in parameters (32 billion; more parameters usually means more capable, slower and more memory), the training stage (instruct or chat means it follows instructions; base means it only continues text) and sometimes the number format (q4 means quantised to 4 bits to save memory, Lesson 3.20). Hosted model names follow the same idea with tiers instead of sizes: each provider sells a large, a medium and a small model per generation, and often dated snapshots such as -2026-08-01 that you pin so behaviour does not change under you.

The model landscape, late 2026

As of October 2026, the closed frontier is led by Anthropic's Claude 5.5 family (Opus, Sonnet, Haiku), OpenAI's GPT-5.5 and 5.6 families, and Google's Gemini 3.5 models. Strong open-weight families include Qwen 3.x, DeepSeek V4, Mistral (Small 4, Medium 3.5) and Google's Gemma 4. New versions ship almost monthly, so treat any ranking, including this one, as stale within a quarter. What lasts is the method below.

How a model becomes a chat model

How this connects
A four-line recap of the From language model to assistant section

Lessons 10.35 to 10.37 cover these four stages in full, with code. They are repeated here in four lines because the two consequences below them matter for every design decision in the rest of the course. Later, the Models: training, evaluation and fine-tuning section returns to training in depth, and its workshop (Lessons 7.25 to 3.25) has you fine-tune a model yourself.

  1. Pretraining: predict the next token over a huge text corpus. The result knows a lot but does not follow instructions.
  2. Supervised fine-tuning (SFT): train on example conversations so it answers like an assistant.
  3. Preference tuning: reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) or AI feedback (RLAIF) teaches it which answers people prefer.
  4. Reinforcement learning from verifiable rewards (RLVR): train on tasks with checkable answers (math, code, tool use). This is what produced today's reasoning models and much of their agent skill.

Two consequences for engineers: the model's knowledge stops at a knowledge cutoff, so anything current must come from tools or retrieval. And models are trained on particular tool-calling formats, so the provider's native tool calling usually works better than an improvised JSON format in the prompt.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "A reasoning model is better at everything."
    ✓ It is better at hard multi-step problems and slower and costlier everywhere; simple steps rarely need it.
  • ✗ "Open-weight means free."
    ✓ The weights are free; the GPUs, serving and operations are not.
  • ✗ "A system should use one model."
    ✓ Routing simple steps to small models and hard ones to strong models is common and cheaper.

Exercises

Predict before you run

For classifying 1 million support tickets overnight, would you pick a frontier model, a reasoning model or a small model first? (Solution P1.)

Break it on purpose

Use a reasoning model with maximum effort for a simple FAQ bot. What happens to cost and latency? (Solution B1.)

Debugging exercise

An open-weight model performs well in tests but its license blocks your use case. What should have been checked first? (Solution D1.)

Make it your own

Write a model portfolio for ShopKart: which kind of model for chat, classification, extraction, coding and offline batch, with reasons. (Solution M1.)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Fit the model to the task

A small model (or a batch API with a mid-size model), validated on an eval set: the task is simple and high-volume, so cost and throughput dominate. Escalate hard cases to a larger model.

SolutionB1 · Reasoning has a price

Thinking tokens add cost and seconds before the answer, with little quality gain for easy questions. Use low effort or a non-reasoning model.

SolutionD1 · Licenses are requirements

The license terms (commercial use, user limits, acceptable use) before evaluation. Open weights do not mean unrestricted use.

SolutionM1 · A portfolio

A strong model for chat and agents, small models for classification, structured-output models for extraction, a coding model for internal tools, batch APIslater · 14.142 for offline work, all behind a gateway.

Check your understanding

Explain the mechanismWhat kinds of models exist, and how is a chat model made from a base model?

Worked answer. Frontier models (largest, most capable), reasoning models (trained to think before answering, with adjustable effort), small models (fast and cheap), and open-weight models (downloadable, self-hostable, with licenses); mixture-of-experts architectures activate part of the network per token. A base model from pretraining becomes a chat model through supervised fine-tuning and preference or reinforcement training.

A plausible wrong answer. "Bigger is always better." Fit to the task and constraints wins.

Follow-ups. When would you choose open-weight models?

Debug a failureA small model works for English tickets and fails for Hindi ones. What do you check?

Worked answer. Language coverage in its training and tokenizer, the eval segment for Hindi, and whether a multilingual model or translation step helps.

A plausible wrong answer. "Small models cannot do Hindi." Some can; measure.

Follow-ups. How would you route by language?

Defend a designOne model for everything, or several?

Worked answer. Several behind a gateway: different tasks have different quality, latency and cost needs, and routing saves money. Keep the number small enough to evaluate and operate.

A plausible wrong answer. "One model for simplicity." You pay frontier prices for trivial tasks.

Follow-ups. How many models would you start with?
