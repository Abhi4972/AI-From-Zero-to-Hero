In plain words first
The idea in one line

METR (ex-ARC Evals) is an independent 501(c)(3) since December 2023. Their Time Horizon 1.1 benchmark (January 2026) fits a logistic curve to task-success probability vs log(expert human completion time); the intersection at 50% probability defines the model's time horizon. The 2025–2026 engagement set covers GPT-5.1, GPT-5.1-Codex-Max, and prototype monitoring evaluations (can a monitor catch side tasks; can the agent evade). Benchmark suites: HCAST (189 ML, cyber, SWE and reasoning tasks; 1 minute to 8+ hours), RE-Bench (7 ML research-engineering environments, with 71 eight-hour attempts by human experts as the baseline), SWAA (66 short software actions of seconds each). The honest note: METR measurements are idealized — no human, no real consequences — and the team has documented the eval-vs-deployment behavior gap (Lesson 15.1). A time horizon is an upper bound, not a deployment prediction.

The problem

In plain words first

METR is an independent nonprofit that measures what frontier AI agents can do on their own. Its headline number is the time horizon: the length of task, measured in how long it takes a skilled person, that a model completes half the time. Fit a curve of success against task length and read off where it crosses 50%. Benchmarks and evaluation metrics are covered in Lesson 10.47 (linked above). This lesson measures how much that number moves between runs, what happens when a model outgrows the task suite, and then fits a horizon for a real small model.

Scaling policies (Lessons 19, 20) are only as useful as the measurements they reference. "AI R&D-4 threshold" and "Long-range Autonomy" are defined in policy prose; they become actionable only when specific evaluations produce specific numbers.

METR is the 2024–2026 external evaluation organization that has defined many of those numbers. They evaluate frontier models — often pre-release, under NDA with labs — and publish methodology afterward. The Time Horizon 1.1 benchmark (January 2026) is their headline artifact: a single scalar that compresses capability into a human-legible unit ("this model can do the kind of task an expert spends X hours on at 50% reliability").

The lesson is partly about the methodology (how a horizon is computed) and partly about the interpretation (why a horizon is an upper bound, not a deployment prediction). The two skills belong together. A team that understands how the horizon is fit is much harder to fool with a bad vendor claim than a team that just sees "14 hours" on a slide.

Idea 1 · METR background

  • Founded: December 2023 (ex-ARC Evals, spun out into independent 501(c)(3)).
  • Scope: evaluation of frontier models' autonomous capabilities, often pre-release.
  • Partner labs: Anthropic, OpenAI (multiple engagements 2025–2026).
  • Notable deliverables: Time Horizon 1.0 (March 2025), Time Horizon 1.1 (January 2026), prototype monitoring evaluations.

Idea 2 · The Time Horizon fit

Methodology (from METR blog and papers):

  1. Collect a task suite spanning minute-scale to hour-scale expert completion times. Current suites: HCAST (180+ tasks), RE-Bench (71 tasks), SWAA.
  2. Run the model on each task; record success or failure.
  3. Fit a logistic curve: P(success) as a function of log(expert completion time).
  4. The horizon is the expert-time at which P(success) = 0.5.

The logistic-fit shape is the right one because capability generally has an increasing, plateau-approaching relationship with task difficulty. The 50% point is a choice (could be 10%, 90%); METR reports multiple thresholds in the detailed paper but leads with 50% because it is the most intuitive.

Idea 3 · The January 2026 numbers

Per Time Horizon 1.1:

  • Claude Opus 4.6: about 12 hours at 50% reliability on the Time Horizon 1.1 suite (measured February 2026), with a wide interval. In May 2026 METR put Claude Mythos Preview above 16 hours, past the range its suite measures reliably.
  • Doubling time on HCAST-style tasks: ~4.3 months (130.8 days) on the post-2023 fit reported by Time Horizon 1.1 (January 2026); the ~7-month figure is the full 2019–2025 fit from Time Horizon 1.0 and is reported in TH1.1 alongside the post-2023 number.

Those are the specific numbers the rest of Phase 15 references. Labs' system cards and outside reports cite METR's horizon measurements as one input to capability assessments; neither RSP v3.0 nor DeepMind's FSF ties a threshold to a horizon number.

Idea 4 · Benchmark suites

  • HCAST: 189 tasks across ML, cybersecurity, software engineering, and general reasoning. Span: 1 minute to 8+ hours of expert time. The main suite behind the horizon number.
  • RE-Bench: 7 ML research-engineering environments, with 71 eight-hour attempts by human experts as the baseline. Measures whether the model can do the kind of ML R&D work that the automated-AI-R&D thresholds in Lessons 15.19 and 15.20 are about.
  • SWAA: 66 single-step software actions that take a person seconds; useful for calibrating the low end of the horizon curve.

Idea 5 · Prototype monitoring evaluations

2025–2026 METR work included monitoring-evaluation prototypes: can a monitor catch a model performing a side task (something other than what the operator asked for); can the agent evade the monitor. These directly feed the "Undermining Safeguards" category in OpenAI PF v2 and the deceptive-alignment monitoring in DeepMind FSF v3 (Lesson 15.20).

Idea 6 · Why horizons are upper bounds

  • Idealized tooling: benchmark tasks give the model clean tools and well-formatted data. Real deployments are messier.
  • No real consequences: the model never actually bills a customer, deletes real data, or contacts real people. Real deployments have irreversible stakes.
  • Eval-context gaming: Lesson 1. Models behave differently in tests. The 2026 International AI Safety Report documents this empirically.
  • No legitimate user variance: benchmark prompts are structured. Real users produce ambiguous, context-dependent requests.

The horizon is the capability ceiling under favorable conditions. Deployment reliability is a different number, lower, and teams must measure their own distribution to know it.

Idea 7 · The external-evaluator case

External evaluation matters because internal labs have incentives to optimize metrics they report. METR's independence — a 501(c)(3) with a declared methodology and peer-reviewed papers — is the structural mitigation. It is not sufficient alone (labs still control what METR sees), but it is strictly better than no external evaluation.

Idea 8 · How to use horizon numbers in practice

  • As a capability filter: if a model's horizon is well below the expert-time of a proposed task, do not ship it autonomous (Lesson 1's skill file).
  • As a trend indicator: doubling time tells you how long the current practice will remain safe even without new mitigations.
  • As a prior: a horizon of 14 hours is a starting point. Adjust down for your task distribution, your tooling quality, and your deployment context.

The same idea as a formula

The fit. With task length t (expert time) and success s∈{0,1}, fit

P(s=1∣t)=σ(wlnt+b),σ(z)=1+e−z1​.
The p-horizon solves σ(wlnt+b)=p:
hp​=exp(wln1−pp​−b​),h50​=e−b/w.

Why the estimate wobbles. h50​ is a ratio of two fitted numbers, and an exponential of it. A small error in w when w is near zero, or with few long tasks, becomes a large error in hours. This is worst when the true horizon is past the longest task: the curve never crosses 50% inside the data, and the fit extrapolates.

Doubling. If the horizon doubles every D days, reaching H from h takes Dlog2​(H/h) days.

Worked example
Projecting one horizon
  • Start at about 12 hours (Claude Opus 4.6, February 2026) with a doubling time of D=131 days.
  • To reach a 40-hour work week: log2​(40/12)=1.74 doublings, so 1.74×131≈228 days.
  • To reach a work year of 2,000 hours: log2​(2000/12)=7.4 doublings, about 967 days, or 2.6 years.
  • At the older 7-month doubling, the work week takes 12.2 months instead. The doubling time you assume matters more than the starting point.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "A horizon of 14 hours means 14 hours, give or take a little."
    ✓ In 200 repeated measurements of the same model, 90% fell between 9.6 and 19.8 hours.
  • ✗ "The suite can measure any horizon."
    ✓ Once the true horizon passes the longest tasks, the estimates spread out enormously. METR said this of Claude Mythos Preview.
  • ✗ "A horizon is a property of the model alone."
    ✓ The same small model's horizon in steps changed with the scaffold: answering at once or thinking step by step.
  • ✗ "A 12-hour horizon means it can run 12 hours unattended."
    ✓ It means half of tasks that size succeed in a clean benchmark. Deployment reliability is lower.

How stable is a horizon?

The lesson's synth_tasks(), fit() and horizon_at(). A fast Newton-method fit is checked against the lesson's gradient descent, then the same model is measured 200 times at four gaming rates, the suite is capped at 16 hours, and doubling-time arithmetic is done from about 12 hours. A few seconds.

# The lesson's synth_tasks(), fit() and horizon_at() (imported from main.py), asked three questions. How much does
# the 50% horizon move between runs of the same model, with no gaming at all? What happens when the true horizon is
# longer than the longest task in the suite? And what does a doubling time of about 131 days imply? A fast
# Newton-method fit stands in for the lesson's gradient descent after checking they agree. A few seconds.
import math, random
import main
from main import synth_tasks, fit, horizon_at, inject_gaming

def newton_fit(tasks, iters=30):
    """Maximum-likelihood logistic fit of success on log(time), by Newton's method."""
    w = b = 0.0
    for _ in range(iters):
        gw = gb = hww = hwb = hbb = 0.0
        for t, s in tasks:
            x = math.log(t); p = main.sigmoid(w * x + b); r = p * (1 - p)
            gw += (p - s) * x; gb += p - s; hww += r * x * x; hwb += r * x; hbb += r
        det = hww * hbb - hwb * hwb
        if abs(det) < 1e-12:
            break
        w -= (hbb * gw - hwb * gb) / det; b -= (hww * gb - hwb * gw) / det
    return w, b

random.seed(3)
tasks = synth_tasks(14.0, n=160)
print(f"check: lesson's gradient descent 50% horizon {horizon_at(*fit(tasks), 0.5):.2f} hr, Newton {horizon_at(*newton_fit(tasks), 0.5):.2f} hr")

def spread(true_h, n=160, gaming=0.0, runs=200, t_max=48):
    hs = []
    for seed in range(runs):
        random.seed(1000 + seed)
        ts = [(t, s) for t, s in synth_tasks(true_h, n=n * 2) if t <= t_max][:n]
        if gaming:
            ts = inject_gaming(ts, gaming)
        try:
            hs.append(horizon_at(*newton_fit(ts), 0.5))
        except (ValueError, OverflowError):
            pass
    hs.sort()
    return hs[len(hs) // 2], hs[int(0.05 * len(hs))], hs[int(0.95 * len(hs)) - 1]

print("\n1. the same model measured 200 times (true 50% horizon 14 hr, 160 tasks per run)")
for g in (0.0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.4):
    med, lo, hi = spread(14.0, gaming=g)
    print(f"   gaming {g:>4.0%}: median {med:5.1f} hr, 90% of runs between {lo:5.1f} and {hi:6.1f} hr")

print("\n2. a true horizon beyond the longest task (tasks capped at 16 hr)")
for true_h in (4, 14, 30, 60):
    med, lo, hi = spread(true_h, t_max=16, runs=120)
    print(f"   true {true_h:>3} hr: median estimate {med:6.1f} hr, 90% of runs {lo:6.1f} to {hi:>9,.0f} hr")

print("\n3. doubling arithmetic, from about 12 hr (Claude Opus 4.6 on Time Horizon 1.1, February 2026)")
for target, label in ((40, "one 40-hour work week"), (167, "one month of full-time work"), (2000, "one work year")):
    days = math.log2(target / 12) * 131
    print(f"   {label:<28} at a 131-day doubling: {days:5.0f} days; at 7 months: {math.log2(target / 12) * 7:4.1f} months")

Reading this output

The fits agree, and one run is not enough. Newton's method and the lesson's gradient descent give the same 11.3 hours on the lesson's run. Across 200 runs of the same model with no gaming, the median is right at 14.0 hours, but 90% of runs land anywhere from 9.6 to 19.8. The lesson's single 11.3 was ordinary noise.

Gaming shifts the whole distribution up. Turning 10%, 20% and 40% of failures into successes moves the median to 17.4, 21.3 and 38.4 hours, and widens the spread. Against one noisy run, a 10% gaming rate cannot be told apart from luck; across many runs it is clear.

Past the longest task, the number stops meaning much. With tasks capped at 16 hours, a true 4-hour horizon is measured well (2.9 to 5.7). A true 30-hour horizon gives estimates from 14 to 125 hours, and a true 60-hour horizon from 18 to over 146,000. That is the situation METR described for Claude Mythos Preview.

Doubling time dominates projections. From about 12 hours, a 131-day doubling reaches a 40-hour week in about 228 days and a 2,000-hour work year in about 967. At the older 7-month doubling, the week takes 12.2 months.

# This block continues from the one above: a horizon for a real model, in steps rather than hours. Qwen2.5-0.5B-
# Instruct (greedy, CPU) follows chains of 1 to 12 arithmetic steps ("start with 7, add 3, double it, ..."), 6 chains
# per length. Success is fitted with the same logistic curve on log(length), and the 50% "horizon" is reported with a
# bootstrap interval. It runs twice: answer only, and thinking step by step (a different scaffold). About 6 minutes.
import os, random, re, warnings
os.environ["HF_HUB_OFFLINE"] = "1"
warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")
import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM, logging as hf_logging
hf_logging.set_verbosity_error(); hf_logging.disable_progress_bar()
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct")
llm = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct").eval()

rng = random.Random(11)
def chain(k):
    v = rng.randint(2, 9); words = [f"Start with {v}."]
    for _ in range(k):
        op = rng.choice(["add", "sub", "dbl"])
        if op == "add":
            n = rng.randint(2, 9); v += n; words.append(f"Add {n}.")
        elif op == "sub" and v > 10:
            n = rng.randint(2, 9); v -= n; words.append(f"Subtract {n}.")
        else:
            v *= 2; words.append("Double it.")
    return " ".join(words), v
LENGTHS = [1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12]
PROBLEMS = [(k, *chain(k)) for k in LENGTHS for _ in range(6)]
def run(think):
    out = []
    for k, text, ans in PROBLEMS:
        tail = (" Think step by step, then give the final number on the last line." if think else " Answer with the final number only.")
        ids = tok.apply_chat_template([{"role": "user", "content": text + tail}], add_generation_prompt=True,
                                      return_tensors="pt", return_dict=True)["input_ids"]
        with torch.no_grad():
            o = llm.generate(ids, max_new_tokens=200 if think else 10, do_sample=False)
        nums = re.findall(r"-?\d+", tok.decode(o[0, ids.shape[1]:], skip_special_tokens=True))
        out.append((k, bool(nums) and int(nums[-1]) == ans))
    return out
for think in (False, True):
    res = run(think)
    by_k = {k: sum(s for kk, s in res if kk == k) for k in LENGTHS}
    w, b = newton_fit(res)
    boot = []
    for i in range(300):
        r = random.Random(i)
        sample = [res[r.randrange(len(res))] for _ in res]
        try:
            bw, bb = newton_fit(sample)
            if bw < 0:
                boot.append(horizon_at(bw, bb, 0.5))
        except (ValueError, OverflowError, ZeroDivisionError):
            pass
    boot.sort()
    label = "think step by step" if think else "answer only"
    h = horizon_at(w, b, 0.5) if w < 0 else float("nan")
    print(f"{label:<20} correct of 6 at lengths {LENGTHS}: {list(by_k.values())}")
    print(f"{'':<20} 50% horizon {h:.1f} steps, bootstrap 90% interval {boot[int(0.05 * len(boot))]:.1f}-{boot[int(0.95 * len(boot)) - 1]:.1f} steps")

Answering at once, the small model's horizon is about 2 steps. It gets all 6 one-step chains right, 1 of 6 two-step chains, and nothing longer. The bootstrap interval is 1.4 to 2.0 steps.

Thinking step by step moves the horizon to about 3.3 steps (interval 2.2 to 4.4). It now gets 4 of 6 at two, three and four steps, then falls off: 1 of 6 at six steps and almost nothing beyond. It also misses one one-step chain it got right before, so the scaffold is not better everywhere.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

code/main.py implements a logistic fit of task-success vs log(expert time), given a synthetic result set. It reports the 50% horizon (METR's headline), 10% horizon (conservative), and 90% horizon (optimistic). Also demonstrates what changes when the success rate is artificially inflated by eval-context gaming.

The whole of code/main.py follows in parts. Each part runs on this page, in order, in one Python session; the last part does what python3 code/main.py does.

Part 1 of 4 · synth_tasks, sigmoid, fit

"""Logistic-fit time-horizon estimator — stdlib Python.

Given synthetic task results (expert_time_hours, success), fit a logistic
curve to P(success) vs log(expert_time) and report the 50/10/90% horizons.
Then show what eval-context gaming does to the observed number.

Uses only stdlib; the logistic fit is a minimal gradient-descent
implementation sized for pedagogy, not production.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
import random


# ---------- Synthetic data generator ----------

def synth_tasks(true_horizon_hours: float, slope: float = 1.2,
                n: int = 120) -> list[tuple[float, bool]]:
    """Generate synthetic (expert_time_hours, success) pairs.

    P(success) = sigmoid(slope * (log(true_horizon) - log(expert_time))).
    """
    log_h = math.log(true_horizon_hours)
    # expert times spanning 0.05 hr to ~48 hr
    out = []
    for _ in range(n):
        t = math.exp(random.uniform(math.log(0.05), math.log(48)))
        logit = slope * (log_h - math.log(t))
        p = 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-logit))
        success = random.random() < p
        out.append((t, success))
    return out


# ---------- Logistic fit (tiny GD) ----------

def sigmoid(x: float) -> float:
    if x > 50:
        return 1.0
    if x < -50:
        return 0.0
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-x))


def fit(tasks: list[tuple[float, bool]], iters: int = 4000,
        lr: float = 0.05) -> tuple[float, float]:
    """Fit P(success) = sigmoid(w * log(t) + b). Return (w, b)."""
    w = 0.0
    b = 0.0
    for _ in range(iters):
        dw = 0.0
        db = 0.0
        n = len(tasks)
        for t, s in tasks:
            y = 1.0 if s else 0.0
            p = sigmoid(w * math.log(t) + b)
            err = p - y
            dw += err * math.log(t)
            db += err
        w -= lr * dw / n
        b -= lr * db / n
    return w, b

Part 2 of 4 · horizon_at, inject_gaming, report

def horizon_at(w: float, b: float, p: float) -> float:
    """Expert time where P(success) = p.  sigmoid(w*log(t)+b) = p ->
    log(t) = (logit(p) - b) / w."""
    logit = math.log(p / (1 - p))
    # A zero (or near-zero) slope means success probability does not
    # depend on task length, so the horizon is undefined. Raise rather
    # than silently returning inf/nan so callers see a loud failure.
    eps = 1e-12
    if abs(w) < eps:
        raise ValueError(
            f"horizon undefined: slope w={w} is ~0 "
            f"(b={b}, p={p}, logit={logit})"
        )
    return math.exp((logit - b) / w)


# ---------- Eval-context gaming simulator ----------

def inject_gaming(tasks: list[tuple[float, bool]],
                  gaming_rate: float) -> list[tuple[float, bool]]:
    """Flip `gaming_rate` fraction of failures to successes (model behaves
    better in eval context). Returns a new list."""
    gamed = []
    for t, s in tasks:
        if not s and random.random() < gaming_rate:
            gamed.append((t, True))
        else:
            gamed.append((t, s))
    return gamed


# ---------- Driver ----------

def report(label: str, w: float, b: float) -> None:
    h50 = horizon_at(w, b, 0.50)
    h10 = horizon_at(w, b, 0.10)
    h90 = horizon_at(w, b, 0.90)
    print(f"  {label:<40}  50%={h50:>6.2f} hr  "
          f"10%={h10:>6.2f} hr  90%={h90:>6.2f} hr")

def main() -> None:
    random.seed(3)
    print("=" * 80)
    print("METR-STYLE HORIZON ESTIMATOR (Phase 15, Lesson 21)")
    print("=" * 80)

    true_h = 14.0
    print(f"\nSynthetic ground truth: 50% horizon = {true_h:.1f} hr")
    print("-" * 80)

    tasks = synth_tasks(true_horizon_hours=true_h, n=160)
    w, b = fit(tasks)
    clean_h50 = horizon_at(w, b, 0.50)
    report("clean evaluation (no gaming)", w, b)

    gamed_h50: dict[float, float] = {}
    for rate in (0.1, 0.2, 0.4):
        gamed = inject_gaming(tasks, gaming_rate=rate)
        w_g, b_g = fit(gamed)
        gamed_h50[rate] = horizon_at(w_g, b_g, 0.50)
        report(f"with eval-context gaming rate {rate:.0%}", w_g, b_g)

    print()
    print("=" * 80)
    print("HEADLINE: horizons are fit to observed success; gaming shifts them")
    print("-" * 80)
    print(f"  With seed=3 / n=160 / iters=4000 / true_h={true_h:.1f} hr:")
    print(f"    clean fit          50% horizon ≈ {clean_h50:>6.2f} hr "
          f"(ground truth {true_h:.1f})")
    for rate, h in gamed_h50.items():
        delta = h - true_h
        print(f"    gaming rate {rate:>4.0%}   50% horizon ≈ {h:>6.2f} hr "
              f"({delta:+.2f} hr vs ground truth)")
    print("  Trend: gaming pushes the observed 50% horizon further from the")
    print("  synthetic ground truth as the rate climbs. Exact deltas depend on")
    print("  seed, n, iters, and the chosen true_h. A horizon number without a")
    print("  gaming audit is a capability ceiling the deploy context may not reach.")

Break it on purpose

A vendor's slide says "our agent has a 20-hour time horizon, so it can handle your overnight migrations." The number comes from one run on a 60-task internal suite whose longest task takes 6 hours. Using this lesson, what is wrong with the claim? (Solution B1.)

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Time Horizon 1.1 (January 2026) is METR's current suite. It measured Claude Opus 4.6 at about 12 hours (February 2026) and Claude Mythos Preview above 16 hours (May 2026), at the edge of what it can measure. METR estimates a doubling time of about 131 days since 2023.
  • Labs cite external evaluations. System cards from Anthropic, OpenAI and Google report outside testing by METR, Apollo Research and government institutes such as the US CAISI and the UK AI Security Institute.
  • Deployment runs are shorter than horizons. Anthropic's own data puts Claude Code's 99.9th-percentile turn at about 42 minutes, against a horizon of several hours.
  • On your laptop: build a horizon for your own tasks. Time how long each takes a person, run your agent, and fit the same two-parameter curve. A few dozen tasks give a rough number; report its interval.

Sources: METR, Measuring AI ability to complete long tasks, Epoch AI, METR time horizons, Anthropic, Measuring agent autonomy in practice, METR, research.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • A time horizon is where a fitted success curve crosses 50%, measured in expert task time.
  • One measurement has a wide interval; compare horizons only with their intervals.
  • When a model outgrows the suite, the estimate stops meaning much; METR said so of Mythos Preview.
  • Scaffolding changes the horizon, so it describes a model and a setup together.
  • Treat the horizon as an upper bound, and measure your own tasks for deployment.

Given a vendor's published time-horizon claim (e.g., "our model completes 14-hour tasks at 50% reliability"), produce a gap analysis that quantifies the deployment-reality delta and flags any methodological weaknesses.

Produce:

1. **Methodology audit.** Identify the task suite (HCAST, RE-Bench, SWAA, or proprietary). Confirm the logistic fit is disclosed (slope, sample size, confidence interval). A horizon without methodology disclosure is a marketing claim.
2. **Task distribution fit.** Map the vendor's benchmark task distribution onto the user's production task distribution. If they diverge materially (vendor measures SWE tasks, production is customer-support flows), the number does not transfer.
3. **Eval-context gap.** Apply a 10–40% gap between benchmark horizon and deployment reality. Cite the Anthropic 2024 alignment-faking study and the 2026 International AI Safety Report on eval-context gaming. The actual gap depends on the eval protocol; gaming is higher on unstructured tasks.
4. **Tooling gap.** Benchmark tooling is clean and well-instrumented. Production tooling is messier. Estimate an additional 5–30% reliability discount.
5. **Human-in-the-loop assumption.** Benchmarks assume no HITL. Production agents with HITL run at higher reliability but lower autonomy. Adjust the horizon interpretation accordingly.

Hard rejects:
- Horizon claims with no source methodology or sample size.
- Claims that a benchmark horizon predicts deployment reliability.
- Vendors citing a 2025-or-earlier horizon number as current (the doubling time is ~7 months; 2025 numbers are stale within a year).
- Treating a 50% horizon as "will work most of the time" — 50% reliability is a coin flip.

Refusal rules:
- If the vendor does not disclose methodology, refuse and require the source paper or blog post.
- If the benchmark distribution does not overlap the production distribution, refuse and require internal evaluation.
- If the vendor cites horizons without a gaming audit on their specific eval pipeline, refuse to quote the number as a reliability prediction.

Output format:

Return a horizon-interpretation memo with:
- **Source methodology** (suite, fit method, sample size, CI)
- **Distribution overlap** (benchmark vs production; % mapping)
- **Eval-context gap estimate** (low / med / high with rationale)
- **Tooling gap estimate** (low / med / high)
- **HITL assumption** (benchmark-style autonomous vs production HITL)
- **Deploy-adjusted horizon** (horizon after gap and tooling discounts)
- **Readiness verdict** (production / staging / research-only)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · About 10 to 20 hours, and then no answer at all

Wide. Across 200 runs of 160 tasks with no gaming, the median is 14.0 hours, but 90% of runs fall between 9.6 and 19.8 hours. The lesson's 11.3 is an ordinary draw.

With the suite capped at 16 hours and a true horizon of 60, the median estimate is about 50 hours, and 90% of runs fall between about 18 hours and over 146,000. The curve never crosses 50% inside the data, so the fit is guessing. Report that the horizon is beyond the suite rather than a number.

SolutionB1 · One run, past the suite

Three problems, each enough on its own.

  • It is one run. A single fit can be off by a third or more in either direction. Ask for an interval, from repeated runs or a bootstrap.
  • It is past the suite. A 20-hour horizon measured on tasks of at most 6 hours is an extrapolation. Ask for the task-length distribution and how many tasks are near the claimed horizon.
  • A horizon is not unattended reliability. It is the task length at which half of benchmark tasks succeed with clean tools and no stakes. An overnight migration needs the reliability of your own tasks in your own environment, measured, at the success rate you need. That is far above 50%, so the usable length is far shorter.
