The problem

In plain words first

This capstone puts every piece of the phase into one small GPT: character embeddings, positions, a stack of pre-norm blocks with causal attention and a SwiGLU feed-forward, a tied output layer and the shift-by-one loss. Trained on the complete works of Shakespeare as plain characters, such a model learns to write text that looks like Shakespeare. Training it takes many minutes even at this size, so on this page the model is built, measured and run, but not trained: the runs load the real Shakespeare file, count the model's parameters, show why its starting weights must be small, time one training step to estimate the full cost, and compare what the untrained model writes with a simple model built by counting letter pairs, which is the bar a trained model must beat.

You've read every paper. You've implemented attention, multi-head splits, positional encodings, encoder and decoder blocks, BERT and GPT losses, MoE, KV cache. Now make them work together on a real task.

The capstone: train a small decoder-only transformer end-to-end on a character-level language modeling task. It reads Shakespeare. It generates new Shakespeare. It is small enough to train on a laptop in under 10 minutes. It is correct enough that swapping in a bigger dataset and longer training gets you a real LM.

This is the "nanoGPT" of the course. It is not original — Karpathy's 2023 nanoGPT tutorial is the reference implementation every student writes at least once. We lift the shape and retool it around what we've covered.

Idea 1 · What we ship

  • GPTConfig — one place to configure all hyperparameters.
  • MultiHeadAttention — causal, batched, with optional Flash-style pathway (PyTorch's scaled_dot_product_attention).
  • SwiGLUFFN — modern FFN.
  • Block — pre-norm, residual-wrapped attention + FFN.
  • GPT — embeddings, stacked blocks, LM head, generate().
  • Training loop with AdamW, cosine LR, gradient clipping.
  • Char-level tokenizer on Shakespeare text.

Idea 2 · What we don't ship

  • RoPE — implemented conceptually in Lesson 04. Here we use learned positional embeddings for simplicity. The exercises ask you to swap in RoPE.
  • KV cache during generation — each generation step recomputes attention over the full prefix. Slower but simpler. The exercises ask you to add a KV cache.
  • Flash Attention — PyTorch 2.0+ auto-dispatches if the inputs match; we use F.scaled_dot_product_attention.
  • MoE — single FFN per block. You saw MoE in Lesson 11.

Idea 3 · Target metrics

On a Mac M2 laptop, a 4-layer, 4-head, d_model=128 GPT trained for 2,000 steps on tinyshakespeare.txt:

  • Training loss converges from ~4.2 (random) to ~1.5 in about 6 minutes.
  • Sampled output looks Shakespeare-shaped: archaic words, line breaks, proper names like "ROMEO:" emerge.
  • Val loss (held-out final 10% of text) tracks training loss closely; no overfitting at this size/budget.

The same idea as a formula

The whole model on a window of T characters x1​,…,xT​:

ht(0)​=Wtok​[xt​]+Wpos​[t],h(l)=Blockl​(h(l−1)),zt​=RMSNorm(ht(L)​)Wtok⊤​

with each block h←h+Attn(RMSNorm(h)), h←h+SwiGLU(RMSNorm(h)) (Lesson 7.5) and the output head tied to the token embedding.

The objective is the average next-character cross-entropy over the window, L=−T1​∑t​logsoftmax(zt​)xt+1​​. A uniform guess over V characters scores lnV: 4.17 nats for V=65.

Parameters for width d, L blocks, SwiGLU width ≈2.67d, context Tmax​, tied head:

N≈Vd+Tmax​d+L(4d2+3⋅d⋅2.67d)≈Vd+Tmax​d+12Ld2

Training cost for S steps of B×T tokens: C≈6N⋅SBT FLOPs, plus attention.

Initial loss and initialisation. At initialisation the logits are dot products of the final hidden state with embedding rows. If embeddings have standard deviation σ and the normalised hidden state has norm ≈d​, each logit has standard deviation about σd​. With PyTorch's default σ=1 and d=128 that is 11 nats of spread, so the model starts confidently wrong; GPT-2's σ=0.02 gives logits near zero and a loss near lnV.

The capstone's budget by hand

The lesson's model: V=65, d=128, L=4, context 256, batch 32, 2,000 steps.

  • Parameters: $65 \times 128 + 256 \times 128 + 4 \times (4 \times 128^2 + 3 \times 128 \times 341) = 8{,}320 + 32{,}768 + 785{,}920 \approx 828$K.
  • Tokens per step: 32×256=8,192; over 2,000 steps: 16.4M characters, about 16 passes over the 1.0M training characters.
  • Tokens per parameter: 16.4M/828K≈20, the Chinchilla ratio of Lesson 7.13, by coincidence.
  • Compute: 6×828K×16.4M≈8×1013 FLOPs: minutes on a laptop GPU, a quarter of an hour or more on this page's two CPU cores (the run under Step 2 times one step).

import math, time, runpy, torch, torch.nn as nn, torch.nn.functional as F

# The lesson's model classes (from main.py's try_train), at the size the lesson describes: 4 layers, 4 heads, d=128.
class RMSNorm(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, eps=1e-6):
        super().__init__(); self.weight = nn.Parameter(torch.ones(d)); self.eps = eps
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.weight * x / x.pow(2).mean(-1, keepdim=True).add(self.eps).sqrt()

class CausalSelfAttention(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, h):
        super().__init__(); self.h = h; self.qkv = nn.Linear(d, 3 * d, bias=False); self.out = nn.Linear(d, d, bias=False)
    def forward(self, x):
        B, N, D = x.shape
        q, k, v = (t.view(B, N, self.h, D // self.h).transpose(1, 2) for t in self.qkv(x).split(D, dim=2))
        y = F.scaled_dot_product_attention(q, k, v, is_causal=True)
        return self.out(y.transpose(1, 2).reshape(B, N, D))

class SwiGLUFFN(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, expansion=2.67):
        super().__init__(); h = int(d * expansion)
        self.w1, self.w2, self.w3 = nn.Linear(d, h, bias=False), nn.Linear(h, d, bias=False), nn.Linear(d, h, bias=False)
    def forward(self, x):
        return self.w2(F.silu(self.w1(x)) * self.w3(x))

class Block(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, d, h):
        super().__init__(); self.n1, self.attn, self.n2, self.ffn = RMSNorm(d), CausalSelfAttention(d, h), RMSNorm(d), SwiGLUFFN(d)
    def forward(self, x):
        x = x + self.attn(self.n1(x))
        return x + self.ffn(self.n2(x))

class GPT(nn.Module):
    def __init__(self, vocab, d=128, h=4, layers=4, block_size=256):
        super().__init__()
        self.tok_emb, self.pos_emb = nn.Embedding(vocab, d), nn.Embedding(block_size, d)
        self.blocks = nn.ModuleList(Block(d, h) for _ in range(layers))
        self.norm_f = RMSNorm(d); self.lm_head = nn.Linear(d, vocab, bias=False)
        self.lm_head.weight = self.tok_emb.weight                      # tied output head
        self.block_size = block_size
    def forward(self, idx, targets=None):
        x = self.tok_emb(idx) + self.pos_emb(torch.arange(idx.shape[1]))
        for b in self.blocks: x = b(x)
        logits = self.lm_head(self.norm_f(x))
        loss = None if targets is None else F.cross_entropy(logits.view(-1, logits.size(-1)), targets.view(-1))
        return logits, loss

torch.manual_seed(42)
data = torch.tensor(encode(text)); n = int(0.9 * len(data)); train_data, val_data = data[:n], data[n:]
def get_batch(src, B=32, T=256):
    ix = torch.randint(len(src) - T - 1, (B,))
    return torch.stack([src[i:i + T] for i in ix]), torch.stack([src[i + 1:i + T + 1] for i in ix])

The counts agree with the formula box and with the lesson's own estimate (828,160 against 828,288; the estimate's SwiGLU width rounds slightly differently): two thirds of the weights are in the feed-forward layers, the character embedding is tiny (65 × 128), and the position table is four times larger than it. Before training, PyTorch's default initialisation gives a validation loss of about 89 nats, twenty times worse than guessing uniformly: embedding rows with standard deviation 1 make the logits huge, so the model is confidently wrong about everything. Re-initialising every matrix with standard deviation 0.02, as GPT-2 and nanoGPT do, brings it to 4.26, just above ln65. One forward and backward pass takes a fraction of a second here, so the lesson's 2,000 steps would take a quarter of an hour or more on this page's two CPU cores, depending on load; the "6 minutes" target assumes a laptop GPU.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

This capstone is a reference architecture. Three extensions to ship it to something real:

  1. Swap the tokenizer. Use BPE (e.g. tiktoken.get_encoding("cl100k_base")). Vocab size jumps from 65 to ~50,000. Model capacity needs to scale up to compensate.
  2. Train on a bigger corpus. Use OpenWebText or fineweb-edu (HuggingFace). 10B tokens on a single A100 takes ~24 hours for a 125M-param GPT.
  3. Add RoPE + KV cache + Flash Attention. The exercises below walk you through each.

This ends up as a 125M-parameter GPT that generates fluent English. Not a frontier model. But the same structure, scaled up and run with a distributed training framework, is how open research checkpoints are trained (EleutherAI's GPT-NeoX, AI2's OLMo, Karpathy's nanochat).

Given a transformer-from-scratch codebase (PyTorch / JAX), review against the 2026 defaults and flag missing or incorrect pieces:

1. Attention. Causal mask present. Scale by `sqrt(d_head)`. Multi-head split works. Flash Attention used if available. GQA mentioned if d_model ≥ 1024.
2. Positional encoding. RoPE (preferred 2026) or learned absolute (acceptable for small models). Flag sinusoidal as historical.
3. Block wiring. Pre-norm (not post-norm). RMSNorm (not LayerNorm). SwiGLU FFN (not ReLU/GELU). Residuals around every sublayer. Biases dropped in linear layers (modern default).
4. Training. AdamW (or Muon for 2026+), cosine LR schedule with linear warmup, gradient clipping at 1.0, bf16 autocast. Weight tying between token embedding and lm_head.
5. Loss. Shift-by-one cross-entropy at every position. Mask out padding if any. Log train and val loss at a fixed interval.

Refuse to sign off on a codebase with any of: post-norm without explicit reason, LayerNorm in 2026 production code without justification, missing causal mask in decoder self-attention, untied embeddings in a small LM. Flag: no validation split, no gradient clipping, LR > 1e-3 without warmup, or a block_size that exceeds positional embedding range without fallback. Recommend running `python code/main.py` end-to-end and checking final val loss lands under 2.5 on tinyshakespeare at nano config.

Exercises

  1. Easy. Run code/main.py. Verify your trained model's final-step validation loss is under 2.0. Change max_steps from 2,000 to 5,000 — does val loss keep improving?
  2. Medium. Replace learned positional embeddings with RoPE. Apply the rotation to Q and K inside MultiHeadAttention. Train and verify val loss is at least as low.
  3. Medium. Implement a KV cache in the sampling loop. Generate 500 tokens with and without cache. Wall-clock should improve by 5–20× on a laptop.
  4. Hard. Add a second head to the model that predicts the next-plus-one token (MTP — Multi-Token Prediction from DeepSeek-V3). Train jointly. Does it help?
  5. Hard. Replace the single FFN per block with a 4-expert MoE. Router + top-2 routing. See how val loss changes at matched active parameters.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Far worse

Far worse: about 89 nats, against 4.17 for a uniform guess. With PyTorch's default nn.Embedding initialisation each embedding row has entries of standard deviation 1, and because the output head is tied to the embedding, each logit is a dot product of a 128-dimensional hidden state with such a row: values of ±10 to ±30. The softmax then puts almost all probability on an essentially random character, so the true one gets a tiny probability and a huge loss. Training would spend its first steps just shrinking the weights; initialising them with standard deviation 0.02 (GPT-2's choice) starts at 4.26 instead.

SolutionB1 · Positions run out

The position table has block_size = 256 rows. Without the crop, the 257th position asks pos_emb for row 256, which does not exist, and PyTorch raises an IndexError; earlier, while the text is shorter than 256, nothing goes wrong, which is why the bug survives short tests. A learned position table cannot extrapolate at all. A RoPE model has no table and runs on, but its rotations reach angles never seen in training, and quality degrades past the trained length unless the frequencies are rescaled (Lesson 7.4). Cropping to the last 256 tokens keeps the model inside what it was trained on, at the cost of forgetting earlier text.

Interview questions

Questions from real AI engineering interviews that this lesson prepares you for. Answer out loud first, then open the worked answer.

Take-homeThis is the take-home we send: train a small decoder-only transformer on a character-level text corpus of about one megabyte, on a laptop, and return the code, the loss curves and a few samples. Tell me how you would approach it, what a reviewer checks in your submission, and which results prove that the model works.

Level 3 of 4 · about 15 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Build the small reference shape: a character tokenizer over the 65 symbols of the corpus, a token embedding plus a learned positional embedding, a stack of pre-norm blocks with RMSNorm, causal multi-head attention and a SwiGLU network, a final norm and a head tied to the embedding, trained with a shift-by-one cross-entropy on random 256-character windows. Use AdamW, a cosine schedule with warmup and gradient clipping at 1.0, and hold out the last 10 percent of the text for validation. The bar from the lesson: a 4-layer, 4-head model of width 128, about 800K parameters, goes from about 4.2 to about 1.5 training loss in 2,000 steps, about 6 minutes on a laptop, with validation loss under 2.0 that tracks the training loss and samples that look like the corpus.

What to build.

tokens (B, N) -> embedding + position -> [norm, causal attention, residual, norm, SwiGLU, residual] x L
            -> final norm -> head tied to the embedding -> logits (B, N, V) -> shift-by-one loss

A byte-level character tokenizer needs no training. The data split is 90 to 10 by position in the text, so that the validation text is never seen in training.

The training loop.

for step in range(max_steps):
    x, y = get_batch("train")
    logits = model(x)
    loss = F.cross_entropy(logits.view(-1, vocab_size), y.view(-1))
    loss.backward()
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), 1.0)
    opt.step()
    opt.zero_grad()

Log the training and validation loss on the same axis. Sample after training with top-p and stop after 500 characters.

What a reviewer checks.

  1. Correctness of the objective. The causal mask is applied before the softmax and the targets are the inputs shifted by one. A unit test that perturbs a later token and checks earlier outputs is a strong signal.
  2. The first loss. About 4.2 at step zero, the uniform baseline, the logarithm of 65. A start well below it is a reason to check for a leak.
  3. Validation discipline. A held-out split, logged at intervals, that tracks the training loss; the lesson records no overfitting at this size and budget. A validation loss far below training is a reason to check the split and the loss code, because the final 10 percent of text can also be easier.
  4. Training hygiene. Gradient clipping, a schedule, a fixed seed, and a run that reproduces.
  5. Reading the samples. Line breaks, archaic words and speaker names in capitals appear; the text is corpus-shaped, not the corpus.

What proves it works.

The loss curve from about 4.2 to about 1.5, a validation loss under 2.0 that follows it, and samples that a reader recognizes as the style of the corpus. A write-up that names the failure the candidate hit and fixed counts for more than a lower loss.

Stretch goals that separate candidates.

Replace the learned positions with rotation of the queries and keys and show the validation loss is at least as low. Add a cache of keys and values to the sampling loop and show the 500-character sample runs 5 to 20 times faster. Train to 5,000 steps and report whether validation keeps improving.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Use a bigger model to reach a lower loss within the time budget." A larger model trains slower on a laptop and the lesson's bar is reached at 800K parameters in about 6 minutes.
  • "Skip the validation split; the corpus is tiny." The split is the only check against a leak and the reviewer looks for it first.
  • "Report the sample quality instead of the loss." Samples are evidence, not a metric; the curves and the held-out loss are what a reviewer compares across candidates.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) What is the training loss at step zero and how do you know it is right? Expected: about the logarithm of the vocabulary size, about 4.2 for 65 symbols, because a random model is uniform.
  • (level 3) How does a reviewer know your causal mask works without reading the code? Expected: a test that changes a later character and asserts the earlier logits are unchanged, and a loss that starts at the random baseline.
  • (level 3) Why tie the head to the embedding at this size? Expected: it saves parameters and the lesson records a quality improvement; the head weight is the transpose of the embedding.
  • (level 4) A candidate swaps the character tokenizer for a subword vocabulary of about 50,000. What else must change? Expected: the embedding and head grow from 65 rows to about 50,000, the model needs more capacity to use them, and the windows now cover more text per token.
