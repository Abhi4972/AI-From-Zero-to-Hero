The problem

In plain words first

Information retrieval (IR) means finding the few documents, out of thousands or millions, that answer a query. It is the first half of every search box and every RAG system. There are two families of methods. Keyword (sparse) retrieval, with BM25 as the classic, scores documents by the query words they contain, weighting rare words more. It is fast and exact, but blind to synonyms. Dense retrieval turns queries and documents into vectors with a neural network and looks for nearby vectors. It understands paraphrases, but can miss an exact code, name or number. Production systems run both, merge the two ranked lists, and then re-score the best few candidates with a slower, more accurate model. This lesson derives BM25, builds each stage, and measures every combination on a real scientific-claims benchmark with 5,000 documents and expert relevance labels.

The user types "what happens if someone lies to get money" and expects to find the statute that actually covers that: cheating, Section 318 of India's Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (which replaced the long-famous Section 420 of the Indian Penal Code in July 2024). A keyword search misses it entirely (no shared vocabulary). A semantic search misses it if the embeddings were not trained on legal text. Real search has to handle both.

IR is the pipeline under every RAG system, every search bar, every docs site's fuzzy lookup. The 2026 architecture that works in production is not a single method. It is a chain of complementary methods, each catching the failures of the one before.

This lesson builds each piece and names which failures each catches.

The idea

Four layers. Pick the ones you need.

  1. Sparse retrieval (BM25). Fast, precise on exact matches, terrible on semantics. Run over an inverted index. Sub-10ms per query on millions of documents. Gets you statute references, product codes, error messages, named entities right.
  2. Dense retrieval. Encode query and documents into vectors. Nearest neighbor search. Captures paraphrases and semantic similarity. Misses exact keyword matches that differ by one character. Most of the per-query time is encoding the query with the neural model (milliseconds on a GPU, tens of milliseconds on a CPU); the nearest-neighbour search itself takes milliseconds even over millions of vectors with FAISS or a vector database.
  3. Fusion. Merge the ranked lists from sparse and dense. Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) is the easy default because it ignores raw scores (which live in different scales) and only uses rank positions. Weighted fusion is an option when you know one signal dominates for your domain.
  4. Cross-encoder rerank. Take the top-30 from fusion. Run a cross-encoder (query + document together, scoring each pair). Keep the top-5. Cross-encoders are slower per pair than bi-encoders but far more accurate. You amortize by only running them on the top-30.

Three-way retrieval (BM25 + dense + learned-sparse like SPLADE) has beaten two-way combinations in published comparisons (IBM's "Blended RAG", 2024), but needs infrastructure for learned-sparse indexes. For most teams, two-way plus cross-encoder rerank is the sweet spot.

The same idea as a formula

BM25 scores document d for query q:

BM25(d,q)=t∈q∑​idf(t)⋅f(t,d)+k1​(1−b+bavgdl∣d∣​)f(t,d)(k1​+1)​,idf(t)=ln(1+n(t)+0.5N−n(t)+0.5​)

Each piece has a reason. The idf comes from the probabilistic relevance framework: it is roughly the log-odds that a term appears in a relevant rather than a random document, so rare terms count more. The term-frequency factor saturates: as f→∞ it approaches k1​+1, so the tenth occurrence adds far less than the first. The length factor makes a long document need more occurrences for the same credit (b=1: fully proportional to length; b=0: length ignored).

Dense retrieval scores by cosine similarity of learned embeddings, s(d,q)=∥E(q)∥∥E(d)∥E(q)⊤E(d)​. Document vectors are computed once, offline; a query costs one encoder pass plus a nearest-neighbour search.

Reciprocal Rank Fusion (Cormack et al., 2009) ignores scores and uses ranks only:

RRF(d)=rankings r∑​k+rankr​(d)1​,k=60

Bi-encoder versus cross-encoder. A bi-encoder encodes query and document separately (N documents encoded once, then cheap dot products). A cross-encoder reads the pair together, s=f([q;d]), so every word of the query can attend to every word of the document. It is far more accurate, but costs one full forward pass per (query, document) pair, which is why it only reranks a short list.

Metrics. For a query with relevant set R and ranking d1​,d2​,…: - Recall@k =∣R∩{d1​..dk​}∣/∣R∣. - MRR = mean over queries of 1/rank of the first relevant document. - DCG@k=∑i=1k​log2​(i+1)reli​​, and nDCG@k divides by the DCG of the ideal ranking, so a perfect ranking scores 1.

Worked example
BM25, RRF and nDCG by hand

BM25. Corpus: d0 = "the cat sat", d1 = "the dog sat on the mat", d2 = "cat cat food", d3 = "dogs and cats"; N=4, average length 3.75. Query "cat".

  • n(cat)=2 (d0, d2), so idf=ln(1+2.5/2.5)=ln2=0.693.
  • d0: f=1, ∣d∣=3: length factor 1−0.75+0.75⋅3/3.75=0.85; term weight 1⋅2.5/(1+1.5⋅0.85)=1.099; score 0.762.
  • d2: f=2, ∣d∣=3: weight 2⋅2.5/(2+1.275)=1.527; score 1.058.

Doubling the count raised the weight by 39%, not 100%: that is saturation. d3 contains "cats", not "cat", and scores 0; exact tokens only.

RRF. A document ranked 1st by BM25 and 4th by dense gets 1/61+1/64=0.0320; one ranked 2nd by both gets 2/62=0.0323, slightly more. Agreement between the two lists beats a single first place.

nDCG@3. Relevance of the top three results: (0, 1, 1). DCG =0+1/log2​3+1/log2​4=0.631+0.5=1.131. Ideal order (1, 1, 0): 1+0.631=1.631. nDCG@3 =0.693.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Dense retrieval replaces BM25."
    ✓ They fail on different queries. Exact identifiers, rare names and new terms favour BM25; paraphrases favour dense. Under "Use it" the fusion of both beats either alone.
  • ✗ "RRF needs the two systems' scores on the same scale."
    ✓ It uses only ranks, which is exactly why it is the default: BM25 scores and cosine similarities are not comparable.
  • ✗ "A cross-encoder is just a better embedding model."
    ✓ It produces no reusable document vectors at all; it must run on every pair at query time, so it can only rerank a shortlist.

Step 1 · BM25 from scratch

import math
import re
from collections import Counter

TOKEN_RE = re.compile(r"[a-z0-9]+")


def tokenize(text):
    return TOKEN_RE.findall(text.lower())


class BM25:
    def __init__(self, corpus, k1=1.5, b=0.75):
        if not corpus:
            raise ValueError("corpus must not be empty")
        self.corpus = [tokenize(d) for d in corpus]
        self.k1 = k1
        self.b = b
        self.n_docs = len(self.corpus)
        self.avg_dl = sum(len(d) for d in self.corpus) / self.n_docs
        self.df = Counter()
        for doc in self.corpus:
            for term in set(doc):
                self.df[term] += 1

    def idf(self, term):
        n = self.df.get(term, 0)
        return math.log(1 + (self.n_docs - n + 0.5) / (n + 0.5))

    def score(self, query, doc_idx):
        q_tokens = tokenize(query)
        doc = self.corpus[doc_idx]
        dl = len(doc)
        freq = Counter(doc)
        score = 0.0
        for term in q_tokens:
            f = freq.get(term, 0)
            if f == 0:
                continue
            numerator = f * (self.k1 + 1)
            denominator = f + self.k1 * (1 - self.b + self.b * dl / self.avg_dl)
            score += self.idf(term) * numerator / denominator
        return score

    def rank(self, query, top_k=10):
        scored = [(self.score(query, i), i) for i in range(self.n_docs)]
        scored.sort(reverse=True)
        return scored[:top_k]

Two parameters worth knowing. k1=1.5 controls term-frequency saturation; higher means more weight on term repetition. b=0.75 controls length normalization; 0 ignores document length, 1 fully normalizes. These are the common defaults (Robertson and colleagues suggested $k_1$ between 1.2 and 2.0 and $b = 0.75$); tuning them per collection gains a little, usually much less than adding a dense retriever.

Step 2 · dense retrieval with a bi-encoder

from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
import numpy as np


def build_dense_index(corpus, model_id="sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2"):
    encoder = SentenceTransformer(model_id)
    embeddings = encoder.encode(corpus, normalize_embeddings=True)
    return encoder, embeddings


def dense_search(encoder, embeddings, query, top_k=10):
    q_emb = encoder.encode([query], normalize_embeddings=True)
    sims = (embeddings @ q_emb.T).flatten()
    order = np.argsort(-sims)[:top_k]
    return [(float(sims[i]), int(i)) for i in order]

L2-normalize embeddings so dot product equals cosine. all-MiniLM-L6-v2 is 384-dim, fast, and strong enough for most English retrieval. For multilingual work, use paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2. For top accuracy, bge-large-en-v1.5 or e5-large-v2.

Step 3 · Reciprocal Rank Fusion

def reciprocal_rank_fusion(rankings, k=60):
    scores = {}
    for ranking in rankings:
        for rank, (_, doc_idx) in enumerate(ranking):
            scores[doc_idx] = scores.get(doc_idx, 0.0) + 1.0 / (k + rank + 1)
    fused = sorted(scores.items(), key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)
    return [(score, doc_idx) for doc_idx, score in fused]

The k=60 constant comes from the original RRF paper. Higher k flattens the contribution of rank differences; lower k makes top ranks dominate. 60 is the published default and rarely needs tuning.

Break it on purpose

Replace RRF with a plain sum of the raw scores: BM25 score plus cosine similarity for each document. On a typical query, BM25 scores run from 0 to about 20 and cosines from about 0.2 to 0.8. What does the "fused" ranking turn into? (Solution B1.)

Step 4 · hybrid search + rerank

from sentence_transformers import CrossEncoder

reranker = CrossEncoder("cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2")


def hybrid_search(query, bm25, encoder, dense_embeddings, corpus, top_k=5, pool_size=30, reranker=reranker):
    sparse_ranking = bm25.rank(query, top_k=pool_size)
    dense_ranking = dense_search(encoder, dense_embeddings, query, top_k=pool_size)
    fused = reciprocal_rank_fusion([sparse_ranking, dense_ranking])[:pool_size]

    pairs = [(query, corpus[doc_idx]) for _, doc_idx in fused]
    scores = reranker.predict(pairs)
    reranked = sorted(zip(scores, [doc_idx for _, doc_idx in fused]), reverse=True)
    return reranked[:top_k]

Three stages composed. BM25 finds lexical matches. Dense finds semantic matches. RRF merges the two rankings without needing score calibration. Cross-encoder rescores the top-30 using query-document pairs together, which captures fine-grained relevance the bi-encoder missed. Keep top-5.

Step 5 · evaluation

Metric Meaning
Recall@k Of queries where the correct document exists, how often is it in the top-k?
MRR (Mean Reciprocal Rank) Average of 1/rank of first relevant document.
nDCG@k Accounts for relevance gradations, not just binary relevant/not.

For RAG specifically, Recall@k of the retriever is the most important number. Your reader cannot answer if the right passage is not in the retrieved set.

Debugging tip: for failing queries, diff the sparse and dense rankings. If one finds the right document and the other does not, you have a vocabulary mismatch (fix: add the missing half) or a semantic ambiguity (fix: better embeddings or a reranker).

The 2026 stack:

Scale Stack
1k-100k docs In-memory BM25 + all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings + RRF. No separate DB.
100k-10M docs FAISS or pgvector for dense + Elasticsearch / OpenSearch for BM25. Run in parallel.

Best-quality frontier Three-way (BM25 + dense + SPLADE) + ColBERT late-interaction reranking

Whatever you pick, budget for evaluation. Benchmark retrieval recall before benchmarking end-to-end RAG accuracy. A reader cannot fix what the retriever missed.

print(f"{len(corpus_text):,} abstracts, {len(test_q)} test queries; e.g. query: {test_q[0][1]!r}")

def evaluate(rankings, k=10):
    rec, mrr, ndcg = [], [], []
    for (qid, _), ranking in zip(test_q, rankings):
        rel = qrels[qid]; top = [doc_ids[i] for _, i in ranking[:k]]
        rec.append(len(rel & set(top)) / len(rel))
        first = next((r for r, d in enumerate(top, 1) if d in rel), None); mrr.append(1 / first if first else 0.0)
        dcg = sum(1 / np.log2(r + 1) for r, d in enumerate(top, 1) if d in rel)
        ndcg.append(dcg / sum(1 / np.log2(r + 1) for r in range(1, min(len(rel), k) + 1)))
    return np.mean(rec), np.mean(mrr), np.mean(ndcg)

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Embedding models are much stronger than MiniLM. The MTEB leaderboard's retrieval track is led by multi-billion-parameter embedding models (Qwen3-Embedding topped the multilingual board in June 2025), and API models (OpenAI, Gemini, Cohere, Voyage) and compact open models such as BGE and GTE beat MiniLM by a wide margin.
  • Rerankers are a standard stage. Hosted rerankers (Cohere Rerank, Voyage, Jina) and open cross-encoders (BGE reranker) reorder the top results of hybrid search; they usually add more quality than changing the embedding model.
  • Learned sparse and late interaction in search engines. Elastic's ELSER and OpenSearch's neural sparse models add learned term expansion to inverted indexes, and Vespa serves ColBERT-style late interaction.
  • Agentic search. In agentic RAG a model rewrites the query, searches several times and decides when it has enough, so retrieval becomes a tool the model calls.

Sources: MTEB, Qwen3-Embedding, Cohere Rerank, ELSER, Vespa ColBERT.

The hard-won lessons from 2026 production RAG

  • Many RAG failures trace to ingestion and chunking, not the model. Teams spend weeks swapping LLMs and tuning prompts while the retrieval quietly returns the wrong context every third query. Fix chunking first.
  • Chunking strategy matters more than chunk size. Fixed-size splits break tables, code, and nested headers. Sentence-aware is the default; semantic or LLM-based chunking pays off for technical docs and product manuals.
  • Parent-doc pattern. Retrieve small "child" chunks for precision. When multiple children from the same parent section appear, swap in the parent block to preserve context. This consistently lifts answer quality without retraining.
  • Pass only a few reranked chunks. A handful (often 3 to 5) is a common sweet spot: every extra chunk adds token cost and latency, and irrelevant chunks can distract the reader. Measure where the curve flattens for your data; if many more chunks keep helping, the reranker is probably weak.
  • HyDE / query expansion. Generate a hypothetical answer from the query, embed that, retrieve. Bridges the phrasing gap between short questions and long documents. Free precision lift with no training.
  • Set a context budget. Decide a maximum number of retrieved tokens per answer; if queries keep hitting it, the reranker threshold is probably too loose.
  • Version everything. Prompts, chunking rules, embedding model, reranker. Any drift silently breaks answer quality. CI gates on faithfulness, context precision, and unanswered-question rate block regressions before users see them.
  • Three-way retrieval (BM25 + dense + learned-sparse like SPLADE) can beat two-way, especially for queries mixing proper nouns with meaning. Ship it when your infrastructure supports SPLADE indexes and your evaluation shows the gain.

These are rules of thumb from practitioners, not laws; check each on your own evaluation set. The robust lesson underneath them: most RAG quality problems are retrieval problems, so improve and measure retrieval before tuning the model.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • BM25: idf times a saturating, length-normalised term frequency; precise on exact terms, blind to synonyms.
  • Dense retrieval: cosine similarity of learned embeddings; good at paraphrase, can miss exact identifiers, depends on training domain.
  • RRF merges ranked lists by rank alone (1/(60+rank)); no score calibration needed.
  • Cross-encoders read query and document together: most accurate, too slow for anything but reranking a shortlist.
  • Evaluate with recall@k, MRR and nDCG on labelled queries; measure retrieval before end-to-end RAG.

medium25 minimplement

BM25 is the standard ranking function for keyword search. It gives a high score to a document that contains rare query terms many times, and it corrects for the length of the document. Hybrid retrieval pipelines run BM25 next to a dense vector search.

Task

Write three functions. The documents and the query are lists of tokens that are already tokenized.

  1. idf(corpus, term) returns the inverse document frequency of one term.
  2. bm25_scores(corpus, query, k1=1.5, b=0.75) returns the BM25 score of each document for the query.
  3. rank(corpus, query, top_k, k1=1.5, b=0.75) returns the indices of the top_k documents with the highest scores.

Let N be the number of documents. Let n(t) be the number of documents that contain the term t at least once. Use this idf:

idf(t)=ln(1+n(t)+0.5N−n(t)+0.5​)

Let f(t,d) be the number of times t occurs in document d. Let ∣d∣ be the number of tokens in d, and avgdl the mean of ∣d∣ over all documents. The score of document d is

score(d)=t∈query∑​idf(t)⋅f(t,d)+k1​(1−b+bavgdl∣d∣​)f(t,d)(k1​+1)​

The sum runs over the query list. A term that occurs twice in the query adds its weight twice. A term with f(t,d)=0 adds nothing.

Tie rule: rank sorts the scores from high to low. When two documents have the same score, the document with the lower index comes first.

Input and output

Name Type Shape Meaning
corpus list of lists of str N documents the tokens of each document
query list of str any length the query tokens, repeats allowed
term str scalar one token
k1 float scalar the term frequency saturation, default 1.5
b float scalar the length normalization weight from 0 to 1, default 0.75
top_k int scalar the number of indices to return
  • idf returns a Python float.
  • bm25_scores returns a float array of shape (N,). Entry i is the score of document i.
  • rank returns a list of top_k int document indices, best first.

Constraints

  • 1 <= N <= 200 and each document has at most 200 tokens.
  • A document can be empty, but at least one document has a token.
  • 0 <= b <= 1, k1 > 0 and 1 <= top_k <= N.
  • Do not change the corpus or the query.
  • Use Python and numpy only.

Your code
Common mistakes the checks catch
  • Loops over the set of query terms, so a repeated query term counts only once.
  • Counts every occurrence of the term for n, not the number of documents that contain it.
  • Uses ln((N - n + 0.5) / (n + 0.5)) without the 1 +, so idf is negative for common terms.
  • Leaves out the (k1 + 1) factor in the numerator, so every score is too small by that factor.
  • Uses f + k1 as the denominator and ignores the document length.
  • Breaks ties by the higher document index first, not the lower one.
Hint 1

Compute avgdl once from the lengths of all documents. Count the tokens of each document with collections.Counter.

Hint 2

For n(t), count documents, not occurrences. A document that contains the term three times adds 1 to n(t).

Hint 3

Loop over the query list as it is. Do not turn it into a set, because a repeated query term must add its weight again.

Hint 4

The length factor is 1 - b + b * dl / avgdl. It equals 1 for a document of mean length. Multiply it by k1 and add f to get the denominator. For the ranking, sort the indices by the key (-score, index).

Reference solution and notes
import math
from collections import Counter

import numpy as np


def idf(corpus, term):
    n_docs = len(corpus)
    n = sum(1 for doc in corpus if term in set(doc))
    return math.log(1 + (n_docs - n + 0.5) / (n + 0.5))


def bm25_scores(corpus, query, k1=1.5, b=0.75):
    n_docs = len(corpus)
    avg_dl = sum(len(doc) for doc in corpus) / n_docs
    scores = np.zeros(n_docs)
    for i, doc in enumerate(corpus):
        dl = len(doc)
        freq = Counter(doc)
        total = 0.0
        for term in query:
            f = freq.get(term, 0)
            if f == 0:
                continue
            num = f * (k1 + 1)
            den = f + k1 * (1 - b + b * dl / avg_dl)
            total += idf(corpus, term) * num / den
        scores[i] = total
    return scores


def rank(corpus, query, top_k, k1=1.5, b=0.75):
    scores = bm25_scores(corpus, query, k1, b)
    order = sorted(range(len(corpus)), key=lambda i: (-scores[i], i))
    return order[:top_k]

What k1 does

The term weight f(k1​+1)/(f+k1​) grows with f but never passes k1​+1. The tenth occurrence of a term adds much less than the first. A small k1​ saturates fast. A large k1​ acts more like raw term frequency.

What b does

With b=1, a document twice the mean length needs about twice the term count for the same weight. With b=0, length has no effect. The default b=0.75 sits between the two.

Why the 1 + in the idf

The classic Robertson idf ln((N−n+0.5)/(n+0.5)) is negative when a term occurs in more than half of the documents. A negative weight lets a common word push a good document down. The 1 + keeps every idf above 0. Lucene uses the same form.

Tie rule

The lesson sorts (score, index) tuples with reverse=True, so equal scores put the higher index first. This problem fixes the lower index first, to match the other retrieval problems. Ties are common in BM25, because every document without a query term scores exactly 0.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · BM25

On SciFact, BM25 matches or beats all-MiniLM-L6-v2 on nDCG@10 (the run prints both). Scientific claims and abstracts share precise technical terms, and an exact match on a rare term like a gene name is very strong evidence, which is BM25's home ground; MiniLM was trained mostly on general web question-answer pairs and has a weaker sense of biomedical vocabulary. This is a known BEIR result: many dense retrievers trained on MS MARCO underperform BM25 on out-of-domain collections, which is why hybrid retrieval became the default.

SolutionB1 · It becomes BM25 with noise

With BM25 scores spread over about 0 to 20 and cosines squeezed between 0.2 and 0.8, the sum is almost entirely the BM25 score: the dense signal can move a document by less than one BM25 point. The "hybrid" ranking is BM25's ranking with a little noise, and every document dense retrieval found that BM25 missed stays buried. Fixes are rank-based fusion (RRF) or normalising both scores to a common scale (min-max or z-scores per query) with a tuned weight.
