The problem

In plain words first

A voice carries the speaker as well as the words. Speaker recognition turns a recording into a list of numbers, an embedding, chosen so that recordings of the same person land close together and recordings of different people land apart, whatever they are saying. Then everything is distances. Verification ("is this Alice?") compares one embedding with Alice's stored one against a threshold. Identification ("who is this?") picks the closest of many stored speakers. Diarization ("who spoke when?") clusters the embeddings of a recording's segments. The threshold decides how many impostors get in and how many genuine users are turned away, and the point where those two rates are equal, the equal error rate, is the standard score. This lesson measures all of that on real speaker embeddings (x-vectors of seven speakers reading the same 1,130 sentences): how EER falls with more enrolment audio, what centring does, how accurate identification is, and how a clustering threshold makes or breaks diarization. No model is trained.

A user says a passphrase. You want to know: is this the person they claim to be (verification, 1:1), or is it the first person in your enrollment bank (identification, 1:N)? Or neither — is this an unknown speaker (open-set)?

Pre-2018: GMM-UBM + i-vectors. Reasonable EER but fragile to channel shift (phone vs laptop) and emotion. 2018–2022: x-vectors (a TDNN backbone with statistics pooling, trained as a speaker classifier). 2022+: ECAPA-TDNN and WavLM-large embeddings. Today most systems use ECAPA-TDNN-style or self-supervised (WavLM-based) embeddings, and they are compared by one metric.

The metric is EER — Equal Error Rate. Set your decision threshold so False Accept Rate = False Reject Rate. The crossover is EER. Used in every paper, every leaderboard, every procurement call.

The idea

The pipeline. Enrollment: record 5–30 seconds of the target speaker; compute a fixed-dimension embedding (192-d for SpeechBrain's ECAPA-TDNN, 512-d for classic x-vectors). Verification: get the test utterance embedding; compute cosine similarity; compare to a threshold.

ECAPA-TDNN (2020, still dominant 2026). Emphasized Channel Attention, Propagation and Aggregation - Time-Delay Neural Network. 1D conv blocks with squeeze-excitation, multi-head attention pooling, followed by a linear layer to 192-d. Trained on VoxCeleb (SpeechBrain's public model uses the VoxCeleb1 and VoxCeleb2 training sets: over 7,000 celebrities and more than a million utterances from web videos) with Additive Angular Margin loss (AAM-softmax).

WavLM-SV (2022+). Fine-tune a pretrained WavLM-large SSL backbone with AAM loss. Higher quality but much slower: WavLM Large has about 316M parameters against roughly 15M for ECAPA-TDNN.

x-vector (baseline). TDNN + statistics pooling. Classic; still useful on CPU / edge.

AAM-softmax. Standard softmax with added margin m in the angular space: cos(θ + m) for the correct class. Forces inter-class angular separation. Typical m=0.2, scale s=30.

The same idea as a formula

Embedding and score. An encoder f maps an utterance to e=f(x)∈Rd. Verification compares an enrolment embedding (often the mean of several, eˉ=K1​∑k​ek​) with a test embedding:

s=cos(eˉ,etest​)=∥eˉ∥∥etest​∥eˉ⊤etest​​,accept if s≥τ

Error rates. Over impostor trials, FAR(τ)=P(s≥τ∣different); over genuine trials, FRR(τ)=P(s<τ∣same). Raising τ lowers FAR and raises FRR. The EER is the common value where FAR(τ∗)=FRR(τ∗). Deployments pick τ for a target FAR instead (banks care about impostors), and NIST-style evaluations use a weighted detection cost C=Cmiss​Ptarget​FRR+Cfa​(1−Ptarget​)FAR.

AAM-softmax (training only). With unit-length embedding e and unit-length class weights wj​, cosθj​=wj⊤​e. For the true class y the angle is penalised by a margin m:

L=−logescos(θy​+m)+∑j=y​escosθj​escos(θy​+m)​

so the network must place each speaker's embeddings at least m radians closer to their own centre than to any other; at test time, unseen speakers inherit that geometry.

Why averaging enrolments helps. If each embedding is the speaker's true direction plus independent noise of variance σ2, the mean of K has noise variance σ2/K: three enrolment utterances cut the noise by 3​≈1.7.

Diarization error rate. DER=(missed speech+false alarm+speaker confusion)/total speech time, after the best one-to-one mapping of hypothesis clusters to true speakers.

Worked example
An EER and a margin by hand

EER. Five genuine scores {0.91,0.88,0.95,0.80,0.93} and five impostor scores {0.70,0.85,0.60,0.82,0.75}. At τ=0.84: genuine below it: 0.80, so FRR =1/5=20%; impostors at or above it: 0.85, so FAR =1/5=20%. Equal: EER =20% at τ=0.84. Raise τ to 0.89 and FAR drops to 0% while FRR rises to 40%.

Margin. True-class angle θy​=30∘, m=0.2 rad =11.5∘, scale s=30. Without margin the true logit is 30cos30∘=26.0; with it, 30cos41.5∘=22.5. Another class at 45∘ has logit 30cos45∘=21.2. The gap shrinks from 4.8 to 1.3, so the loss keeps pushing until the true angle is well inside the margin.

Enrolment. Moving from 1 to 10 enrolment utterances reduces the noise of the enrolment embedding by 10​≈3.2; only the test side's noise remains, which is why the EER falls but does not reach zero (the run under Step 3: 4.9% → 1.8%).

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "A low EER means the system is secure."
    ✓ EER is one point on the curve. A bank chooses a threshold for a very low false-accept rate and accepts more false rejects; also, replayed or synthetic voices are a separate attack (Lesson 6.16).
  • ✗ "The threshold that worked in testing will work everywhere."
    ✓ Scores shift with microphone, channel and language. Re-tune on target-domain data.
  • ✗ "Speaker embeddings ignore what is said."
    ✓ They try to. Same-text pairs from different speakers still score higher than you would like; short utterances make it worse.

Idea 1 · Scoring

  • Cosine between enrollment and test embeddings. Threshold-based decision.
  • PLDA (Probabilistic LDA). Project embeddings into a latent space where same-speaker vs different-speaker has a closed-form likelihood ratio. Standard with i-vectors and x-vectors; with modern margin-trained embeddings, plain cosine plus score normalisation usually does as well.
  • Score normalization. S-norm or AS-norm: normalize each score against a cohort of imposter means and stds. Essential for cross-domain eval.

Idea 3 · Diarization

"Who spoke when" in a multi-speaker clip. Pipeline: VAD → segment → embed each segment → cluster (agglomerative or spectral) → smooth boundaries. Modern stack: pyannote.audio, which bundles speaker segmentation, embedding and clustering behind one call. Its current open pipeline, community-1 (2025), reports 17.0% DER on AMI (headset mix) against 18.8% for the older 3.1 pipeline; pyannoteAI's hosted precision-2 reports 12.9%.

Step 1 · toy embedding from MFCC statistics

def embed_mfcc_stats(signal, sr):
    frames = featurize(signal, sr, n_mfcc=13)          # main.py's MFCC featurizer (the sketch called it featurize_mfcc)
    mean = [sum(f[i] for f in frames) / len(frames) for i in range(13)]
    std = [
        math.sqrt(sum((f[i] - mean[i]) ** 2 for f in frames) / len(frames))
        for i in range(13)
    ]
    return mean + std  # 26-d

Not SOTA by a mile — for teaching only. code/main.py uses this as a proof-of-concept on synthetic speaker data.

Step 2 · cosine similarity + threshold

def cosine(a, b):
    dot = sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))
    na = math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in a))
    nb = math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in b))
    return dot / (na * nb) if na and nb else 0.0

def verify(enroll, test, threshold=0.75):
    return cosine(enroll, test) >= threshold

Step 3 · EER from similarity pairs

def eer(same_scores, diff_scores):
    thresholds = sorted(set(same_scores + diff_scores))
    best = (1.0, 0.0, thresholds[0])   # (fa, fr, threshold); any start works because best_gap starts at infinity
    best_gap = float("inf")
    for t in thresholds:
        fr = sum(1 for s in same_scores if s < t) / len(same_scores)
        fa = sum(1 for s in diff_scores if s >= t) / len(diff_scores)
        if abs(fa - fr) < best_gap:
            best, best_gap = (fa, fr, t), abs(fa - fr)
    return (best[0] + best[1]) / 2, best[2]

Returns (eer, threshold_at_eer). Report both.

The repository's version started from best = (1.0, 1.0, 0.0), whose gap ∣1.0−1.0∣ is already 0, so no threshold could ever beat it and the function always returned an EER of 100% at threshold 0. Tracking the smallest gap separately fixes it.

Verification on real speaker embeddings

512-dimensional x-vectors from SpeechBrain's VoxCeleb-trained model, computed for the CMU ARCTIC corpus: 7 speakers, each reading the same 1,130 or so sentences. 3,000 genuine and 3,000 impostor trials, scored with Step 2's cosine and Step 3's eer.

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from datasets import load_dataset

# Real speaker embeddings: 512-d x-vectors (SpeechBrain, trained on VoxCeleb) for CMU ARCTIC,
# 7 speakers each reading the same ~1,130 sentences.
xv = load_dataset("Matthijs/cmu-arctic-xvectors", revision="refs/convert/parquet", split="validation")
X = np.array(xv["xvector"], dtype=np.float64)
spk = np.array([str(f.split("_arctic")[0].replace("cmu_us_", "")) for f in xv["filename"]])
sent = np.array([f.split("-")[-1] for f in xv["filename"]])
speakers = sorted(set(spk.tolist()))
print(f"{len(X)} utterances, {len(speakers)} speakers {speakers}, embedding size {X.shape[1]}")

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def trials(n=3000, enroll=1, seed=0):
    """Same-speaker and different-speaker trials; the enrollment side averages `enroll` utterances.
    A fixed seed gives the same trial list every call, so variants are scored on identical pairs."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    same, diff = [], []
    idx = {s: np.where(spk == s)[0] for s in speakers}
    for _ in range(n):
        a = rng.choice(speakers); b = rng.choice([s for s in speakers if s != a])
        e = rng.choice(idx[a], enroll, replace=False); rest = np.setdiff1d(idx[a], e)
        E = X[e].mean(0)
        same.append(cosine(E, X[rng.choice(rest)])); diff.append(cosine(E, X[rng.choice(idx[b])]))
    return same, diff

def cos_np(a, b): return float(a @ b / np.linalg.norm(a) / np.linalg.norm(b))
cosine = cos_np                                        # NumPy version of Step 2's cosine, for speed

thr = {}
for k in [1, 3, 10]:
    s, d = trials(enroll=k)
    e, th = eer(s, d); thr[k] = th
    print(f"enroll with {k:2d} utterance(s): EER {e:.2%} at cosine threshold {th:.3f}  "
          f"(same-speaker mean {np.mean(s):.2f}, different-speaker mean {np.mean(d):.2f})")

s1, d1 = trials(enroll=1)
print(f"enroll with 1 but use the 10-utterance threshold {thr[10]:.3f}: "
      f"FRR {np.mean(np.array(s1) < thr[10]):.1%}, FAR {np.mean(np.array(d1) >= thr[10]):.1%}")

# Centring: subtract the mean embedding before the cosine (a cheap step every production scorer uses).
mu = X.mean(0); X_raw = X.copy(); X = X - mu
s_c, d_c = trials(enroll=1)
print(f"after subtracting the mean embedding: EER {eer(s_c, d_c)[0]:.2%} (enroll with 1, same trials)")
X = X_raw
s1, d1 = trials(enroll=1)

grid = np.linspace(min(d1), max(s1), 300)
far = [np.mean(np.array(d1) >= t) for t in grid]; frr = [np.mean(np.array(s1) < t) for t in grid]
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 3.8))
ax[0].hist(d1, bins=60, alpha=0.6, label="different speakers", color="#c0504d"); ax[0].hist(s1, bins=60, alpha=0.6, label="same speaker", color="#2b5d8a")
ax[0].set_xlabel("cosine score"); ax[0].legend(); ax[0].set_title("Scores of 3,000 trials each (enrol with 1 utterance)")
ax[1].plot(grid, far, label="false accept rate", color="#c0504d"); ax[1].plot(grid, frr, label="false reject rate", color="#2b5d8a")
e1, t1 = eer(s1, d1); ax[1].axvline(t1, ls="--", color="gray"); ax[1].set_xlabel("threshold"); ax[1].legend()
ax[1].set_title(f"FAR and FRR cross at the EER: {e1:.1%}")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

Reading this output

With one enrolment utterance the EER is 4.9%; with 3 it is 2.5% and with 10, 1.8%: averaging removes much of the enrolment side's noise, as the formula box predicts. Look at the raw scores: genuine trials average 0.96 and impostors 0.88. All x-vectors share a large common component, so every cosine is high, and the useful signal is the small difference. Subtracting the mean embedding removes that shared part and lowers the EER on identical trials from 4.9% to 3.8%. The two curves on the right are FAR and FRR as the threshold moves; the EER is where they cross. A system would pick its own operating point on these curves.

Identification, and what the embedding still knows about the text

Each speaker enrolled with 10 utterances; every other utterance assigned to the nearest speaker centroid. Then: same sentence by two different speakers against two different sentences by one speaker.

# Identification (1:N) and the role of the text: enrol each speaker with 10 utterances, test on the rest.
enroll_idx = {s: rng.choice(np.where(spk == s)[0], 10, replace=False) for s in speakers}
centroids = np.stack([X[enroll_idx[s]].mean(0) for s in speakers])
centroids /= np.linalg.norm(centroids, axis=1, keepdims=True)
test = np.setdiff1d(np.arange(len(X)), np.concatenate(list(enroll_idx.values())))
pred = np.array(speakers)[(X[test] / np.linalg.norm(X[test], axis=1, keepdims=True) @ centroids.T).argmax(1)]
print(f"1:{len(speakers)} identification accuracy on {len(test)} utterances: {np.mean(pred == spk[test]):.2%}")
conf = {(a, b): int(np.sum((spk[test] == a) & (pred == b))) for a in speakers for b in speakers if a != b}
print("errors (true -> predicted: count):", {f"{a}->{b}": v for (a, b), v in conf.items() if v})

# Same sentence, different speakers vs different sentences, same speaker: what does the embedding encode?
same_text_diff_spk, diff_text_same_spk = [], []
for _ in range(2000):
    a, b = rng.choice(speakers, 2, replace=False); t = rng.choice(sent[spk == a])
    i = np.where((spk == a) & (sent == t))[0]; j = np.where((spk == b) & (sent == t))[0]
    if len(i) and len(j): same_text_diff_spk.append(cosine(X[i[0]], X[j[0]]))
    k = rng.choice(np.where(spk == a)[0], 2, replace=False); diff_text_same_spk.append(cosine(X[k[0]], X[k[1]]))
print(f"same sentence, different speakers: mean cosine {np.mean(same_text_diff_spk):.2f}; "
      f"different sentences, same speaker: {np.mean(diff_text_same_spk):.2f}")

The clustering half of diarization

A simulated three-person conversation: 40 turns, each an x-vector of a random utterance by one of three speakers, labels hidden. Agglomerative clustering with cosine distance; the distance threshold decides when two clusters are "the same speaker".

from scipy.cluster.hierarchy import linkage, fcluster
from scipy.optimize import linear_sum_assignment

# The clustering half of diarization: 40 "segments" from a 3-person conversation, labels hidden.
conv_spk = rng.choice(speakers, 3, replace=False)
order = rng.choice(conv_spk, 40)                                   # who speaks each turn
seg = np.array([rng.choice(np.where(spk == s)[0]) for s in order])
E = X[seg] - X.mean(0)                                             # centre, then cosine distance
for thr in [0.3, 0.5, 0.7]:
    lab = fcluster(linkage(E, method="average", metric="cosine"), t=thr, criterion="distance")
    k = lab.max()
    M = np.zeros((3, k), int)
    for a, b in zip(order, lab):
        M[list(conv_spk).index(a), b - 1] += 1
    r, c = linear_sum_assignment(-M)                                 # best one-to-one match of clusters to speakers
    print(f"distance threshold {thr}: {k} clusters found (3 speakers), {M[r, c].sum() / 40:.0%} of turns assigned to the right speaker")
What you should see
distance threshold 0.3: 24 clusters found (3 speakers), 40% of turns assigned to the right speaker
distance threshold 0.5: 5 clusters found (3 speakers), 88% of turns assigned to the right speaker
distance threshold 0.7: 3 clusters found (3 speakers), 100% of turns assigned to the right speaker
Reading this output

The threshold is everything. At 0.3 the clustering refuses to merge and finds 24 "speakers", so most turns are attributed to the wrong label. At 0.5 it finds 5. At 0.7 it finds exactly 3 and assigns all 40 turns correctly. Real pipelines tune this threshold (or a speaker-count estimate) on held-out meetings, and their segments are shorter and noisier than these clean utterances, which is why DER on AMI is still above 10%.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Open models in use. SpeechBrain's ECAPA-TDNN and NVIDIA's TitaNet (NeMo) for embeddings; pyannote.audio for diarization, whose community-1 pipeline (2025) is its current open release; WhisperX attaches pyannote's speaker labels to transcripts.
  • Hosted diarization. Most speech-to-text APIs now return speaker labels, and pyannoteAI's precision-2 is a hosted version of the same stack; the open pipelines remain the default when audio cannot leave your servers.

Sources: pyannote.audio README, SpeechBrain ECAPA model card, WavLM paper.

Pitfalls

  • Channel mismatch. Model trained on VoxCeleb (web video) ≠ phone-call audio. Always evaluate on target channel.
  • Short utterances. EER degrades sharply below 3 seconds of test audio.
  • Enrollment with noise. One noisy enrollment poisons the anchor. Use ≥3 clean samples and average.
  • Fixed threshold across conditions. Always tune the threshold on a held-out dev set from the target domain.
  • Dot products on raw embeddings. Cosine divides by the lengths, but a plain dot product (as in many vector databases) does not: L2-normalise first, or the magnitude dominates. Centring (subtracting the mean embedding) also helps, as the run under Step 3 shows.
Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Speaker embedding + cosine + threshold = verification; nearest centroid = identification; clustering = diarization.
  • EER is where false accepts equal false rejects; deployments choose their own operating point.
  • Average several clean enrolment utterances; centre embeddings before cosine.
  • Re-tune thresholds for each channel and for each enrolment protocol.
  • Diarization quality hinges on the clustering threshold or speaker-count estimate.

Given a target (verification vs identification vs diarization, domain, channel, threat model) and data (hours for threshold tuning, number of speakers, enrollment clip budget), output:

1. Embedder. ECAPA-TDNN / WavLM-SV / ReDimNet / x-vector. Reason.
2. Enrollment protocol. Number of clips, min duration, noise gate, channel match.
3. Scoring. Cosine / PLDA; with or without AS-norm; cohort size.
4. Threshold. Target FAR (fraud risk) or EER; tuning set size.
5. Spoof defense. Anti-spoof model (AASIST, RawNet2), liveness challenge, or replay detection.

Refuse any fraud-grade deployment without an anti-spoof front-end. Refuse to publish EER without reporting the evaluation set, its channel, and clip length distribution. Flag cosine thresholds fixed across domains without re-tuning.

Exercises

  1. Easy. Run code/main.py. Builds synthetic "speakers" (different tone profiles), enrolls, computes EER on a 100-pair trial list.
  2. Medium. Use SpeechBrain ECAPA on 30 VoxCeleb1 utterances (5 speakers × 6 each). Compute EER with cosine vs PLDA.
  3. Hard. Build the full enroll → diarize → verify pipeline with pyannote.audio. Evaluate DER on AMI dev set.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · From about 5% to under 2%

4.9% with one enrolment utterance, 2.5% with three, 1.8% with ten. Most of the gain comes from the first few utterances, because the enrolment noise shrinks like 1/K​ while the test utterance's noise stays.

SolutionB1 · A threshold belongs to its protocol

With one enrolment utterance, genuine scores are lower and more spread out (the EER threshold there was about 0.935). At 0.955, 30.2% of genuine trials fall below the threshold (against an EER of 4.9%), while false accepts drop to 0.0%. Users enrolled with less audio would be locked out. Tune the threshold for each enrolment protocol and channel, or calibrate scores (for example with score normalisation) so one threshold means the same thing everywhere.
