In plain words first
The idea in one line

Three documentation formats structure AI transparency. Model Cards (Mitchell et al. 2019) — nutrition labels for models: training data, quantitative disaggregated analyses, ethical considerations, caveats; only 0.3% of Hugging Face model cards document ethical considerations (Oreamuno et al. 2023). Datasheets for Datasets (Gebru et al. 2018, CACM) — motivation, composition, collection process, labeling, distribution, maintenance; electronics-datasheet analogy. Data Cards (Pushkarna et al., Google 2022) — modular layered detail (telescopic, periscopic, microscopic) as boundary objects for diverse readers. 2024-2025 developments: automated generation via LLMs (CardGen, Liu et al. 2024); model-card detail correlates with up to 29% download increase on HF (Liang et al. 2024); verifiable attestations (Laminator, Duddu et al. 2024); sustainability reporting additions for carbon/water (Jouneaux et al. July 2025); EU/ISO regulatory cards emerging. System Cards (Sidhpurwala 2024; Meta system-level transparency; "Blueprints of Trust" arXiv:2509.20394) — end-to-end AI system documentation covering security capabilities, prompt-injection protection, data-exfiltration detection, alignment with human values.

The problem

In plain words first

A model, a dataset or a whole AI system is hard to judge from the outside. Cards are standard documents that answer the questions a user, auditor or regulator would ask. A model card (Mitchell et al., 2019) says what the model is for, how it was evaluated, and how well it works for different groups. A datasheet (Gebru et al., 2018) does the same for a dataset: why it was made, what is in it, how it was collected. A system card covers the deployed product as a whole, including the safety layers around the model; frontier labs now publish one with each major release. The common problem is not the format but the filling-in: many published cards leave out exactly the sections, such as limitations and results per group, that a careful reader needs most.

Regulatory frameworks (Lesson 18.24) and lab safety policies (Lesson 18.18) both require documentation. Documentation formats evolved from model-specific (model cards) to dataset-specific (datasheets) to system-specific (system cards). Each addresses a different scope of transparency. The 2024-2025 automation and verifiable-attestation work addresses the long-standing adoption problem.

Idea 1 · Model Cards (Mitchell et al. 2019)

Sections: - Model details. - Intended use. - Factors (relevant demographic or environmental factors for evaluation). - Metrics. - Evaluation data. - Training data. - Quantitative analyses (disaggregated by factors). - Ethical considerations. - Caveats and recommendations.

Adoption problem: Oreamuno et al. 2023 audit of Hugging Face model cards found only 0.3% document ethical considerations.

Idea 2 · Datasheets for Datasets (Gebru et al. 2018)

Electronics-datasheet analogy. Sections: - Motivation (why was the dataset created). - Composition (what is in it). - Collection process (how was it assembled). - Labeling (if applicable). - Uses (intended, prohibited, risks). - Distribution. - Maintenance.

Published in CACM 2021. The datasheet is the upstream documentation; the model card depends on the datasheet being accurate.

Idea 3 · Data Cards (Pushkarna et al., Google 2022)

Modular layered detail. Three zoom levels: - Telescopic. High-level summary for non-experts. - Periscopic. Middle-level overview for ML practitioners. - Microscopic. Detailed feature-level documentation for auditors.

Boundary-object framing: different readers extract different information from the same document.

Idea 4 · System Cards

Scope: end-to-end AI system including model + safety stack + deployment context. Sections typically include: - Security capabilities. - Prompt-injection protection. - Data-exfiltration detection. - Alignment with stated human values. - Incident response.

Sidhpurwala 2024 and Meta system-level transparency work. "Blueprints of Trust" (arXiv:2509.20394) formalizes the System Card as the deployment-layer complement to Model Cards.

Idea 5 · 2024-2025 developments

  • CardGen (Liu et al. 2024). Automated model-card generation via LLMs; reports higher objectivity than many human-authored cards on the standardized Mitchell 2019 fields.
  • Download correlation (Liang et al. 2024). Detailed model cards correlate with up to 29% higher download rates on HF — adoption pressure is now market-driven, not only compliance-driven.
  • Laminator (Duddu et al. 2024). Verifiable attestations via hardware TEE / cryptographic signatures — allows the model card to carry a proof-of-claim, not just a claim.
  • Sustainability (Jouneaux et al. July 2025). Additions for carbon, water, and compute-energy footprint; emerging ISO standards.
  • Regulatory cards. The Transparency chapter of the EU GPAI Code of Practice (Lesson 18.24) includes a Model Documentation Form that providers keep up to date for the AI Office and for downstream providers: in effect a regulator-facing model card.

Worked example
Reading a card for what is missing

A model card for a resume-screening model reports: "Accuracy 91% on our test set. Intended use: ranking applicants. Limitations: none known."

  1. Disaggregated results. Accuracy is not broken down by gender, age or other factors, so there is no evidence about fairness (Lessons 20–21).
  2. Evaluation data. "Our test set" is not described; if it was drawn from the same source as training data, 91% overstates real performance.
  3. Intended and out-of-scope use. "Ranking applicants" is a high-risk use under the EU AI Act (Lesson 24); the card should say who reviews the rankings and what decisions they must not drive alone.
  4. Limitations. "None known" is never true; it usually means nobody looked. A good card lists known failure cases and conditions it was not tested under.
Check your mental model

Which statements are right?

  • ✓ A model card should report results broken down by relevant groups or conditions.
  • ✗ A datasheet documents the model's architecture. It documents a dataset: motivation, composition, collection, uses, maintenance.
  • ✓ A system card covers the deployment, including safeguards around the model.
  • ✗ A complete-looking card proves its claims. Cards record claims; attestation work tries to make some of them verifiable.
Predict before you run

Before running the code below: a card has all nine Mitchell et al. section headings, but three of them contain only "N/A" or "TBD". What completeness score would a heading-only check give it, and what would a content check give?

See it drawn
Headings are not content
SECTIONS = ["model details", "intended use", "factors", "metrics", "evaluation data",
            "training data", "quantitative analyses", "ethical considerations", "caveats and recommendations"]
card = {s: "described in detail ..." for s in SECTIONS}
card.update({"factors": "N/A", "quantitative analyses": "TBD", "ethical considerations": "none"})

EMPTY = {"", "n/a", "tbd", "none", "none known", "-"}
present = sum(s in card for s in SECTIONS)
filled = [s for s in SECTIONS if card.get(s, "").strip().lower() not in EMPTY]
print(f"headings present : {present}/{len(SECTIONS)}")
print(f"sections filled  : {len(filled)}/{len(SECTIONS)}")
print("empty sections   :", ", ".join(s for s in SECTIONS if s not in filled))
What you should see
headings present : 9/9
sections filled  : 6/9
empty sections   : factors, quantitative analyses, ethical considerations
Reading this output

A heading check scores the card 9 of 9; a content check scores it 6 of 9, and the three empty sections are exactly the ones that matter for fairness and risk: factors, disaggregated analyses and ethical considerations. Audits of public model hubs find the same pattern at scale: basic sections are usually filled in, while limitations, evaluation detail and ethics are the most often missing.

Break it on purpose

A team's system card for a customer-support agent describes the model, its benchmarks and its refusal policy, but has no section on tools, data access or incident response. What is wrong with calling it a system card? (Solution B1.)

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Model cards document a model's use, evaluation and per-group results.
  • Datasheets document a dataset's origin, contents and intended uses.
  • Data Cards layer detail for different readers; system cards cover the deployment.
  • The most important sections are the ones most often left empty.
  • Regulation now asks for card-like documentation, such as the GPAI model documentation form.

Ship it

This lesson produces outputs/skill-card-audit.md. Given a model card, datasheet, or system card, it audits section coverage, numerical disaggregation, and whether verifiable attestations are present.

outputs/skill-card-audit.md

---
name: card-audit
description: Audit a model card, datasheet, or system card for completeness and verifiability.
version: 1.0.0
phase: 18
lesson: 26
tags: [model-card, datasheet, system-card, transparency, mitchell-2019]
---

Given a model card, datasheet, or system card, audit for completeness, numerical disaggregation, and verifiability.

Produce:

1. Section coverage. Check every canonical section is filled. Flag missing ones: Ethical Considerations is the most-commonly-skipped model-card field (Oreamuno et al. 2023).
2. Quantitative disaggregation. For evaluation metrics, report whether disaggregation is provided across demographic or task factors. Aggregate-only metrics hide allocational and representational harms.
3. Datasheet alignment. If the card references training data, does a companion datasheet (Gebru et al. 2018) exist? Model-card claims are only as strong as the underlying datasheet.
4. Verifiable attestation. Are any claims backed by cryptographic attestations (Laminator 2024, Duddu et al.) or other third-party verification? Unverified claims are labelled self-report.
5. Sustainability footprint. Is carbon / water / energy usage reported? 2025 emerging ISO / regulatory requirement.

Hard rejects:
- Any model card without Ethical Considerations.
- Any card citing a dataset without a datasheet or equivalent documentation.
- Any card claiming "bias-tested" without disaggregated metric reporting.

Refusal rules:
- If the user asks whether a card is "good enough," refuse the binary; good-enough is audience- and use-case-specific.
- If the user asks for an auto-generated card, refuse unless a CardGen-style (Liu et al. 2024) system with human review is used.

Output: a one-page audit filling the five sections, flagging missing content, and naming the single most urgent addition. Cite Mitchell et al. 2019 and Gebru et al. 2018 once each.

Exercises

  2. Extend the model card with a quantitative disaggregated analysis across two demographic groups (Lesson 18.20).

  3. Read Oreamuno et al. 2023 on the 0.3% adoption rate. Propose one structural change to the model card specification that would increase ethical-considerations adoption.

  4. Laminator (Duddu et al. 2024) uses TEEs for verifiable attestations. Design a model-card field that carries a cryptographic attestation of an evaluation result and describe the verifier's role.

  5. Write a System Card (System Card, not Model Card) for one of your past projects or a hypothetical deployment. Identify the highest-value section for third-party auditors.

Solutions

SolutionB1 · Model card, not system card

Everything listed is about the model. A system card is about the deployed system: which tools the agent can call, what data it can read, which safety layers sit around it (input and output classifiers, monitoring), how prompt injection is handled (Lessons 15 and 25), and what happens when something goes wrong. Without those, a reader cannot judge the deployment's risk, which is the card's purpose.
