The problem

In plain words first

Question answering (QA) means giving a direct answer to a question, such as "June 29, 2007", rather than a document that might contain it. The classic approach, extractive QA, is given a passage and points at the answer inside it. A model reads the question and passage together and scores every word as a possible start and end of the answer, and the best-scoring stretch of text is the answer. Since 2018 models can also say "this passage does not contain the answer". When there is no passage, a retriever first finds relevant ones in a large collection, which is the "R" in RAG (retrieval-augmented generation). This lesson writes out the span-prediction math, rebuilds the answer step that newer transformers versions removed, measures a real model on SQuAD 2.0 (including the questions it should refuse), and shows that in a retrieve-then-read system the retriever often limits the accuracy.

A user types "When did the first iPhone launch?" and expects "June 29, 2007." Not "Apple's history is long and varied." Not "2007" sitting in isolation with no sentence. A direct, grounded, correct answer.

Three architectures have dominated QA over the last decade.

  • Extractive QA. Given a question and a passage that is known to contain the answer, find the start and end indices of the answer span in the passage. SQuAD is the canonical benchmark.
  • Open-domain QA. The passage is not given. Retrieve the relevant passage first, then extract or generate an answer. This is the bedrock of every RAG pipeline today.
  • Generative / Closed-book QA. A large language model answers from its parametric memory. No retrieval. Fastest at inference, least reliable on facts.

The trend in 2026 is hybrid: retrieve the best few passages, then prompt a generative model to answer grounded in those passages. That is RAG, and lesson 14 covers the retrieval half in depth. This lesson builds the QA half.

The idea

Extractive. Encode question and passage together with a transformer (BERT family). Train two heads that predict start and end token indices of the answer. Loss is cross-entropy over valid positions. Output is a span from the passage. Never hallucinates (by construction), never handles questions the passage cannot answer (by construction).

Retrieval-augmented (RAG). Two stages. First, a retriever finds the top-k passages from a corpus. Second, a reader (extractive or generative) produces the answer using those passages. The retriever-reader split lets each be trained and evaluated independently. Modern RAG often adds a reranker between them.

Generative. A decoder-only LLM (GPT, Claude, Llama) answers from learned weights. No retrieval step. Excellent on common knowledge, catastrophic on rare or recent facts. The hallucination rate is inversely correlated with fact frequency in the pretraining data.

The same idea as a formula

Span prediction. The question and passage are encoded together, <s> question </s></s> passage </s>, giving one vector hi​ per token. Two learned vectors ws​,we​ score every token as a start and as an end:

P(start=i)=∑k​ews⊤​hk​ews⊤​hi​​,P(end=j)=∑k​ewe⊤​hk​ewe⊤​hj​​

Training minimises −logP(start=i∗)−logP(end=j∗) for the true span (i∗,j∗). At inference, the answer is the span with the best total logit, under two constraints: it must lie inside the passage, and i≤j<i+L for a maximum length L:

(i^,j^​)=argi≤j<i+Lmax​(ws⊤​hi​+we⊤​hj​)

No answer. SQuAD 2.0 models are trained to put both start and end on the first token for unanswerable questions, so null=ws⊤​h0​+we⊤​h0​. Answer only if the best span beats the null score by a margin τ, which you tune on held-out data to trade wrong answers against wrong refusals.

Metrics. After normalising both strings (lowercase, strip punctuation and the articles a, an, the), Exact Match is 1 if they are identical. Token F1 treats them as bags of tokens: precision =∣common∣/∣pred∣, recall =∣common∣/∣gold∣, F1​=2PR/(P+R). With several gold answers, take the best score over them. For an unanswerable question, both metrics are 1 only if the prediction is empty.

Retrieve then read. If the retriever puts the right passage in its top k with probability R@k and the reader is right on a correct passage with probability a, end-to-end accuracy is at most R@k⋅a: the reader cannot answer from a passage it never sees.

Worked example
EM and F1 by hand

Gold answer "June 29, 2007", prediction "on June 29th, 2007".

  • Normalise: gold tokens june 29 2007; prediction tokens on june 29th 2007 (punctuation removed; "on" is not an article, so it stays).
  • Exact Match: the strings differ, so EM = 0.
  • Common tokens: june, 2007, so 2. Precision =2/4=0.5, recall =2/3=0.667.
  • F1​=2×0.5×0.667/(0.5+0.667)=0.571.

A correct answer earns 0 EM and 0.57 F1 because of "on" and "th". That is why SQuAD reports both, and why production QA uses human or LLM judgement for answer correctness.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Extractive QA cannot be wrong, because it copies from the passage."
    ✓ It cannot invent words, but it can point at the wrong span (a different date in the same passage), or answer a question the passage does not answer.
  • ✗ "The span score is the model's confidence."
    ✓ It is a sum of two logits, meaningful only relative to other spans and the no-answer score; calibrated probabilities need extra work.
  • ✗ "A better reader fixes a RAG system."
    ✓ If the retriever misses the passage, no reader can recover. Measure retrieval recall first.

Step 1 · extractive QA with a pretrained model

import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForQuestionAnswering

model_id = "deepset/roberta-base-squad2"
qa_tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained(model_id)
qa_model = AutoModelForQuestionAnswering.from_pretrained(model_id).eval()


def qa(question, context, max_answer_len=30, handle_impossible_answer=True):
    enc = qa_tok(question, context, return_tensors="pt", truncation="only_second", max_length=384, return_offsets_mapping=True)
    offsets = enc.pop("offset_mapping")[0]
    with torch.no_grad():
        out = qa_model(**enc)
    start, end = out.start_logits[0], out.end_logits[0]          # one score per token for "answer starts / ends here"
    in_context = torch.tensor([sid == 1 for sid in enc.sequence_ids(0)])   # only passage tokens can be part of the answer
    best, best_span = -float("inf"), None
    for i in torch.nonzero(in_context).flatten().tolist():
        for j in range(i, min(i + max_answer_len, len(start))):
            if in_context[j] and start[i] + end[j] > best:
                best, best_span = (start[i] + end[j]).item(), (i, j)
    null = (start[0] + end[0]).item()                             # position 0 (<s>) stands for "no answer"
    if handle_impossible_answer and null > best:
        return {"score": null, "start": 0, "end": 0, "answer": ""}
    s, e = offsets[best_span[0]][0].item(), offsets[best_span[1]][1].item()
    return {"score": best, "start": s, "end": e, "answer": context[s:e]}


passage = (
    "Apple Inc. released the first iPhone on June 29, 2007. "
    "The device was announced by Steve Jobs at Macworld in January 2007."
)
question = "When was the first iPhone released?"

answer = qa(question=question, context=passage)
print(answer)

deepset/roberta-base-squad2 is trained on SQuAD 2.0, which includes unanswerable questions. transformers 5 removed the question-answering pipeline, so the block above (rewritten for this page) does what it did, with the model directly: score every start and end position, pick the best valid span, and compare it with the model's "no answer" score. The pipeline's old default returned the best span even when "no answer" scored higher; the function above returns an empty answer in that case unless you pass handle_impossible_answer=False. Always look at the score either way.

Step 2 · a retrieval-augmented pipeline (sketch)

from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
import numpy as np

encoder = SentenceTransformer("sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2")

corpus = [
    "Apple Inc. released the first iPhone on June 29, 2007.",
    "Macworld 2007 featured the iPhone announcement by Steve Jobs.",
    "Android launched in 2008 as Google's mobile operating system.",
    "The first iPod was released in 2001.",
]
corpus_embeddings = encoder.encode(corpus, normalize_embeddings=True)


def retrieve(question, top_k=2):
    q_emb = encoder.encode([question], normalize_embeddings=True)
    sims = (corpus_embeddings @ q_emb.T).squeeze()
    order = np.argsort(-sims)[:top_k]
    return [corpus[i] for i in order]


def answer(question):
    passages = retrieve(question, top_k=2)
    combined = " ".join(passages)
    return qa(question=question, context=combined)


print(answer("When was the first iPhone released?"))

Two-stage pipeline. Dense retriever (Sentence-BERT) finds relevant passages by semantic similarity. Extractive reader (RoBERTa-SQuAD) pulls the answer span from the combined top passages. Works on small corpora. For a million-document corpus, use FAISS or a vector database.

Break it on purpose

In qa, delete the in_context[j] check and the restriction to passage tokens, so any token, including the question's, can start or end the answer. Ask "When was the first iPhone released?" about a passage that does not mention the iPhone. What can go wrong? (Solution B1.)

Step 3 · generative with RAG

def rag_generate(question, llm):
    passages = retrieve(question, top_k=3)
    prompt = f"""Context:
{chr(10).join('- ' + p for p in passages)}

Question: {question}

Answer using only the context above. If the context does not contain the answer, say "I don't know."
"""
    return llm(prompt)

The prompt pattern matters. Explicitly telling the model to ground in the context and return "I don't know" when the context is insufficient measurably reduces unsupported answers compared with naive prompting, though by how much depends heavily on the model and the data, so measure it on your own questions. More elaborate patterns add citations, confidence scores, and structured extraction.

Step 4 · evaluation that reflects the real world

SQuAD uses Exact Match (EM) and token-level F1. EM is a strict match after normalization (lowercase, strip punctuation, remove articles) — either the prediction matches exactly or it scores 0. F1 is computed over token overlap between prediction and reference and gives partial credit. Both under-credit paraphrases: "June 29, 2007" vs "June 29th, 2007" typically gets 0 EM (the ordinal breaks normalization) but still earns substantial F1 from overlapping tokens.

For production QA:

  • Answer accuracy (LLM-judged or human-judged, since metrics do not capture semantic equivalence).
  • Citation accuracy. Does the cited passage actually support the answer? Trivial to check automatically with string match between generated citations and retrieved passages.
  • Refusal calibration. When the answer is not in the retrieved passages, does the system correctly say "I don't know"? Measure false confidence rate.
  • Retrieval recall. Before evaluating the reader, measure whether the retriever gets the right passage into the top-k. A reader cannot fix a missing passage.

Build · RAGAS: the 2026 production eval framework

RAGAS is a widely used open-source framework built for RAG systems. Its four core metrics use an LLM as the judge; three of them need no gold answer:

  • Faithfulness. Does each claim in the answer come from the retrieved context? Measured by having an LLM split the answer into individual claims and check each one against the context. Your primary hallucination metric.
  • Answer relevance. Does the answer address the question? Measured by generating hypothetical questions from the answer and comparing to the real question.
  • Context precision. Of the retrieved chunks, what fraction were actually relevant? Low precision = noise in prompt.
  • Context recall. Did the retrieved set contain all needed information? This one compares against a reference answer, so it needs a labelled set. Low recall = reader cannot succeed.

Reference-free scoring lets you evaluate on live production traffic without curated gold answers. Layer LLM-as-judge on top for open-ended questions where exact-match metrics are useless.

The 2026 stack.

Use case Recommended
Given passage, find answer span deepset/roberta-base-squad2
Over a fixed corpus, closed-book not acceptable RAG: dense retriever + LLM reader
Real-time over a document store RAG with hybrid (BM25 + dense) retriever + reranker (lesson 14)

Extractive QA is unfashionable in 2026 because RAG with LLMs handles more cases. It still ships in contexts where literal quotation is required: legal research, regulatory compliance, audit tools.

import re, string, time
import pyarrow.parquet as pq
from collections import Counter
from huggingface_hub import hf_hub_download

squad = pq.read_table(hf_hub_download("rajpurkar/squad_v2", "squad_v2/validation-00000-of-00001.parquet", repo_type="dataset")).to_pylist()[:200]
def normalize(t):
    t = "".join(ch for ch in t.lower() if ch not in set(string.punctuation))
    return " ".join(w for w in t.split() if w not in ("a", "an", "the"))
def f1(pred, gold):
    p, g = normalize(pred).split(), normalize(gold).split()
    if not p or not g:
        return float(p == g)
    common = sum((Counter(p) & Counter(g)).values())
    return 0.0 if common == 0 else 2 * common / (len(p) + len(g))
def score(pred, golds):
    golds = golds or [""]
    return max(float(normalize(pred) == normalize(g)) for g in golds), max(f1(pred, g) for g in golds)

print(f"worked example: F1('on June 29th, 2007', 'June 29, 2007') = {f1('on June 29th, 2007', 'June 29, 2007'):.3f}")
start = time.time()
rows = []
for ex in squad:
    golds = ex["answers"]["text"]
    with_null, forced = qa(ex["question"], ex["context"]), qa(ex["question"], ex["context"], handle_impossible_answer=False)
    rows.append((bool(golds), score(with_null["answer"], golds), score(forced["answer"], golds)))
print(f"{len(rows)} questions ({sum(r[0] for r in rows)} answerable) in {time.time() - start:.0f} s")
for label, k in (("may answer 'no answer'", 1), ("must always give a span", 2)):
    for subset, name in ((lambda r: True, "all"), (lambda r: r[0], "answerable"), (lambda r: not r[0], "unanswerable")):
        sel = [r[k] for r in rows if subset(r)]
        print(f"   {label:24s} {name:13s} EM {100 * np.mean([s[0] for s in sel]):5.1f}   F1 {100 * np.mean([s[1] for s in sel]):5.1f}")

With the option to refuse, the model scores about 78 F1 on this sample (its published result on the full SQuAD 2.0 set is around 83). The interesting part is the trade-off. Forced to always answer, it does better on answerable questions (89 against 82 F1), because it no longer wrongly refuses some of them, but every unanswerable question now gets a confident wrong span and scores 0, so the overall F1 halves to about 40. The "no answer" threshold is a dial between those two kinds of error, and where to set it depends on what a wrong answer costs compared with a wrong refusal. In SQuAD 2.0 about half the questions are unanswerable; in production that share is unknown, which is why refusal calibration is a metric in its own right.

Retrieve, then read: where the errors come from

The 200 questions' passages (fewer, since several questions share a passage) become a small corpus. The lesson's dense retriever (all-MiniLM-L6-v2) looks for each answerable question's passage, and the reader answers from the top retrieved passage instead of the gold one.

from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
encoder = SentenceTransformer("sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
corpus = sorted({ex["context"] for ex in squad})
corpus_emb = encoder.encode(corpus, normalize_embeddings=True, batch_size=32)
answerable = [ex for ex in squad if ex["answers"]["text"]]
q_emb = encoder.encode([ex["question"] for ex in answerable], normalize_embeddings=True, batch_size=32)
ranks = np.argsort(-(q_emb @ corpus_emb.T), axis=1)
gold_idx = np.array([corpus.index(ex["context"]) for ex in answerable])
for k in (1, 3, 5):
    print(f"retrieval recall@{k}: {np.mean([g in r[:k] for g, r in zip(gold_idx, ranks)]):.1%}   ({len(corpus)} passages)")
gold_f1, rag_f1 = [], []
for ex, r in zip(answerable, ranks):
    golds = ex["answers"]["text"]
    gold_f1.append(score(qa(ex["question"], ex["context"], handle_impossible_answer=False)["answer"], golds)[1])
    rag_f1.append(score(qa(ex["question"], corpus[r[0]], handle_impossible_answer=False)["answer"], golds)[1])
print(f"reader F1 given the gold passage: {100 * np.mean(gold_f1):.1f}   given the top retrieved passage: {100 * np.mean(rag_f1):.1f}")

Reading this output

Even on a tiny corpus of 38 Wikipedia paragraphs, the dense retriever puts the right passage first for only about three questions in four (73.6%), rising to 89% within the top 5. The reader's F1 falls from 89 with the gold passage to 66.5 with the top retrieved one, almost exactly the gold-passage score times the recall@1 (88.9×0.736=65.4): given the wrong passage, it answers confidently from the wrong text. This is the "retrieve then read" bound from the formula box, and it gets worse as the corpus grows, which is why the next lesson is entirely about retrieval, and why production systems pass several passages to the reader or rerank them first.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • RAG is the default architecture, and agentic RAG is the frontier. Instead of one retrieve-then-read pass, models now decide when to search, rewrite queries and read several sources in a loop (the "deep research" products of 2025), with citations to the passages used.
  • Long context did not make retrieval obsolete. Models with context windows of hundreds of thousands of tokens can read whole document sets, but benchmarks keep showing accuracy dropping for facts buried in the middle of long inputs, and cost grows with every token, so retrieval still decides what the model reads.
  • Evaluation is LLM-judged and claim-level. Frameworks such as RAGAS score faithfulness by checking each claim of the answer against the retrieved text, and the RAG evaluation literature splits errors into retrieval failures and generation failures, as the last experiment did.

Sources: RAGAS, Lost in the Middle.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Extractive QA predicts a start and an end token: softmax over positions, best valid span by summed logits.
  • SQuAD 2.0 adds "no answer": compare the best span with the null score and tune the margin.
  • EM and token F1 after normalisation; both under-credit correct paraphrases.
  • In retrieve-then-read, end-to-end accuracy is capped by retrieval recall; measure the retriever first.
  • RAG evaluation checks faithfulness, relevance and retrieval quality separately, increasingly with LLM judges.

  2. Medium. Add a refusal classifier. When the top retrieval score is below a threshold (say 0.3 cosine), return "I don't know" instead of calling the reader. Tune the threshold on a held-out set.
  3. Hard. Build a RAG pipeline over a 10,000-document corpus of your choice. Implement hybrid retrieval (BM25 + dense) with RRF fusion (see lesson 14). Measure answer accuracy with and without the hybrid step. Document which question types benefit most.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · It drops a lot

On the answerable half, forcing a span changes little, since the model would have answered anyway. On the unanswerable half, every question now gets a span, and every span scores 0, because the correct answer is "no answer". With about half the questions unanswerable, overall F1 falls by tens of points (the run prints both). The lesson: when a system can be asked things its sources do not contain, the ability to abstain is worth as much as the ability to answer.

SolutionB1 · The answer can come from the question

Without the restriction, the span can start or end in the question or on special tokens. Asked about a passage that does not mention the iPhone, the model may "answer" with a piece of the question itself, such as "the first iPhone", or with a span that runs from the question into the passage, which turns into a character range that makes no sense in the passage. The sequence_ids check is what makes extractive QA extractive: only passage tokens are valid answers, and the first token is reserved for "no answer".

Explain the mechanismYou write the search step of a retrieval pipeline. A teammate wants to rank chunks by the raw dot product between the query vector and each chunk vector. What do you use instead, and when is the dot product the right choice?

Level 1 of 4 · about 5 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Use cosine similarity, which is the dot product divided by the lengths of the two vectors. It measures only the angle, so a long chunk and a short chunk compete on direction and not on size. The raw dot product is right only when the vector length carries meaning that you want to reward.

Three measures.

Measure What it does Behavior
Cosine the cosine of the angle between two vectors, from -1 to 1 ignores length, the default for retrieval
Dot product the sum of the products of each pair of values longer vectors score higher
L2 distance the straight-line distance between the two points smaller is closer, sensitive to length

Cosine normalizes by length, so chunks of different sizes compare on direction alone. When someone says vector search, they almost always mean cosine.

The code.

def cosine_similarity(a, b):
    dot = sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b))
    norm_a = math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in a))
    norm_b = math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in b))
    if norm_a == 0 or norm_b == 0:
        return 0.0
    return dot / (norm_a * norm_b)

The guard returns zero when a vector has no length, so an empty chunk does not divide by zero.

When the two agree.

Many embedding models return unit-length vectors, or the caller asks for normalized output. The question-answering lesson encodes with normalization on and then ranks with a plain matrix product. With unit vectors the dot product equals the cosine, so the cheaper operation gives the same order.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The dot product and the cosine give the same order." Only when every vector has the same length; otherwise longer vectors win on the dot product.
  • "A smaller cosine means a closer match." A larger cosine means a smaller angle; smaller is closer only for a distance such as L2.
  • "Use whatever the vector store defaults to." The measure must match how the vectors were produced and normalized, so check the embedder before the store.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 1) What do the values 1, 0 and -1 mean for cosine? Expected: the same direction, orthogonal, and opposite directions.
  • (level 2) Your embedder returns unit vectors. Does the choice still matter? Expected: no, because with unit vectors the dot product and the cosine are equal, which is why the lesson normalizes and then uses a matrix product.
  • (level 2) Why does L2 distance treat a long and a short chunk differently? Expected: it measures the straight-line distance, so a longer vector sits farther away even when it points the same way.
  • (level 3) When would you keep the raw dot product on purpose? Expected: when the model was trained so that length carries a signal, such as more content in a longer document, and you want that signal in the rank.

Where it is taught. The lesson RAG defines the three measures and the guard, and the lesson question answering ranks normalized vectors with a matrix product.

Explain the mechanismTwo answer-grade metrics sit in your RAG eval report: faithfulness and answer relevance. A teammate says they measure the same thing and wants to drop one. Explain what each one grades, and give one answer that scores high on one and low on the other.

Level 2 of 4 · about 7 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Faithfulness asks whether each claim in the answer is supported by the retrieved context. Answer relevance asks whether the answer addresses the question. A grounded summary of the retrieved chunks that never gets to the question scores high on faithfulness and low on relevance, and a short direct answer from the model's own memory scores high on relevance and low on faithfulness.

How each one is computed.

Metric Input to the judge Output
Faithfulness one claim from the answer, plus the retrieved context yes or no, aggregated to the fraction of claims that pass
Answer relevance the question, plus the answer does the answer address the question

The eval splits the answer into sentence-shaped claims first. Faithfulness is also called groundedness, support or attribution. In its production form, the question answering lesson describes it as entailment between claim and context, and relevance as generating hypothetical questions from the answer and comparing them with the real one.

Why both stay.

Each one catches a failure the other cannot see:

  • Faithfulness catches an invented fact, because the fact has no support in the context.
  • Relevance catches an answer that wandered, because the judge never sees the context and only asks whether the question was answered.

The diagnosis table in the eval lesson uses the pair. High MRR with low faithfulness points at the generation prompt. High faithfulness with low relevance points at the query rewriter or the prompt.

No gold answer needed.

Both are judged against the context and the question, not against a reference answer. That lets you score live traffic without labels, next to context precision and context recall for the retrieval side.

The offline stand-in.

The lesson ships a deterministic judge based on token overlap above a threshold, so the eval runs with no network call. It cannot read meaning, so production swaps it for a model judge from a different family than the generator.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "They are the same, a faithful answer is relevant." A faithful answer can summarize the context and ignore the question.
  • "Relevance is enough when retrieval is good." Relevance never sees the context, so an answer from the model's own memory can be relevant and ungrounded.
  • "Faithfulness needs a gold answer." It is judged against the retrieved context, which is why it works on live traffic.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Which of the two catches a hallucination? Expected: faithfulness, because an invented fact has no support in the retrieved context.
  • (level 2) Low relevance with high faithfulness points at which stage? Expected: the query rewriter or the generation prompt; the context was about something else, or the answer drifted from the question.
  • (level 3) Why does neither metric need a reference answer? Expected: both are judged against the context and the question, which is what makes them usable on live traffic.
  • (level 3) What is the limit of the lesson's offline judge? Expected: it counts token overlap above a threshold, so it cannot read meaning; production uses a model judge, from a different family than the generator.

Where it is taught. The lesson RAG evaluation defines both metrics and the stand-in judge, and the lesson question answering gives the production forms.

Debug a failureYour faithfulness score comes from a model that reads each claim next to the retrieved context and answers yes or no. The score has sat near the top for a month, yet reviewers keep finding invented facts in sampled answers. The generator and the judge are the same model. What is going on, and what do you change?

Level 3 of 4 · about 10 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. A judge from the same family as the generator grades its own output as more faithful than it is, so the number stays high while the samples show the truth. Switch the judge to a different model family or hand-grade a sample, and add a sample-level review, because a per-sentence check can pass while the whole answer misleads.

How the metric is built.

The eval splits the answer into sentence-shaped claims, asks the judge whether each claim is supported by the retrieved context, and reports the fraction that pass. The lesson ships a deterministic stand-in judge based on token overlap so the eval runs offline, and swaps in a model call in production. The question answering lesson describes the production form: entailment between each claim and the context, with no gold answer needed.

Two blind spots.

  1. Judge bias. A model judges the output of its own family as more faithful than it is. The lesson's rule: use a different model family for the judge than for the generator, or hand-grade a sample.
  2. Micro-checks miss macro-claims. Each sentence can be supported while the answer as a whole draws a conclusion that the context does not. Add a sample-level qualitative review on top of the automated metric.

What to change.

  • Change the judge family, then measure the disagreement between the old judge and the new one on the same fixture. The lesson's exercise does the same between the mock judge and a real model.
  • Hand-grade a fixed sample each week and treat it as the calibration set for the automated score.
  • Consider graded faithfulness with three values, unsupported, partially supported and fully supported, instead of a yes or no.
  • Keep answer relevance next to faithfulness. A grounded answer to the wrong question is strong on one metric and weak on the other.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The score is high, so the reviewers are wrong." The score is one model's opinion of its own family's output; the sampled answers are the ground truth.
  • "Make the judge stricter and the problem goes away." A stricter prompt or threshold does not remove a bias toward the judge's own family; only another family or a human sample does.
  • "Replace faithfulness with answer relevance." They measure different things; relevance asks whether the answer addresses the question and says nothing about grounding.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) How do you size the judge problem before you change anything? Expected: hand-grade a sample of claims and measure the disagreement between the judge and the human labels.
  • (level 3) Every sentence passes but the answer as a whole misleads. Which check catches that? Expected: a sample-level qualitative review in addition to the per-claim metric.
  • (level 4) Why can a high faithfulness score sit next to a useless answer? Expected: faithfulness compares the answer with the context and not with the question, so a grounded off-topic answer scores high on faithfulness and low on relevance.
  • (level 4) The team wants the strongest model available as judge, and it is also the generator. What do you say? Expected: pick the judge for independence first; a strong judge of its own family is still biased, so use another family and keep a human-graded calibration sample.

Where it is taught. The lesson RAG evaluation names judge bias and the macro-claim gap, and the lesson question answering describes entailment-based faithfulness in production.

Explain the mechanismA user reports that the document assistant gave a wrong answer. The pipeline has a chunker, a retriever, a reranker and a generator. You get one metric to look at before anything else. Which one, and why that one?

Level 1 of 4 · about 6 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Recall at k on the retrieval stage. If the gold chunk is not in the top k, no reranker can bring it back and the generator usually lacks the evidence for the right answer, unless another chunk happens to hold it, so the later metrics are hard to read. Recall comes first in the order, but it is not a gate: when recall holds, or when another retrieved chunk holds the evidence, move on to the rank position and then to the generated text, because a failure there stays hidden otherwise.

The two retrieval metrics.

Precision at k asks what share of the top k is gold. If the gold set has three documents and the top 3 returns two of them plus one wrong one, precision at 3 is 2 / 3. Recall at k asks what share of the gold set is in the top k. If the top 5 holds all three gold documents, recall at 5 is 1.0.

Production teams quote recall. The generator can ignore an extra chunk with ease. It cannot invent an answer from a chunk it never saw.

The order of the checks.

Symptom Likely cause What to fix
Low recall and low precision the chunker cut the answer or the retriever cannot find it chunk boundaries or the retrieval modality
Decent recall, low MRR the right chunk is in the top k but not at position 1 the reranker
High MRR, low faithfulness the generator invents content despite the right context the generation prompt, cite or refuse
High faithfulness, low relevance the answer is grounded but off topic the query rewriter or the prompt
All four high, users still complain the eval set does not represent real queries add real user queries to the qrels

Keep the eval cheap.

Tier the qrels: a smoke set of 20 queries that runs in CI, a regression set of 200 that runs nightly, and a deep set of 2000 that runs weekly. Treat a recall regression like a failing test.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Read the answer and judge it." The answer alone cannot tell which of the four stages failed.
  • "Precision at k, because the noise caused the wrong answer." Precision matters once the right chunk is present; a missing chunk is the first thing to rule out.
  • "Faithfulness, because the model hallucinated." A faithful answer to the wrong chunks is still wrong; faithfulness only grades the generator against what it saw.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 1) What does precision at k add once recall is fine? Expected: it shows how much of the returned list is noise, which matters when a wrong chunk poisons the answer or wastes tokens.
  • (level 2) Recall at 5 is fine but the gold chunk sits at rank 4 or 5. Which metric shows that, and which stage do you fix? Expected: MRR, and the reranker.
  • (level 3) Recall averages 90 percent and one team still complains every day. What is hidden? Expected: one query class that always misses; slice the qrels by class, such as literal, paraphrased and multi-topic, and report each slice.
  • (level 3) How do you keep the eval fast enough to run on every change? Expected: tiers, with a smoke set of 20 queries in CI, 200 nightly and 2000 weekly.

Where it is taught. The lesson RAG evaluation defines the metrics and the diagnosis table, and the lesson question answering says to measure the retriever before the reader.

Defend a designYour document assistant answers every question, including the ones the corpus does not cover, and the invented answers are the ones users remember. Design the path that lets the system say it does not know, and say how you measure that the path works.

Level 3 of 4 · about 10 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Log the reranker's rank-1 score for every query and refuse when it falls below a corpus-specific threshold, instead of handing weak chunks to the generator. Tell the generator to answer only from the snippets, to cite every claim and to say it does not know when the snippets do not answer. Measure refusal calibration: on questions whose answer is not in the store, count how often the system refuses, and track the false confidence rate.

Gate 1: before generation.

The reranker lesson logs the rank-1 cross-encoder score. A query whose top score sits below a corpus-specific threshold is an out-of-domain hit, and the end-to-end lesson refuses on it: the generator returns the refusal text with no citation. The production capstone calls this an unsure mode and measures the drop in false confidence. The question answering lesson starts simpler, with a threshold on the top retrieval similarity, and tunes it on a held-out set.

Gate 2: inside generation.

The prompt tells the model to use only the snippets, to cite every claim with its anchor, and to say it does not know when the snippets do not answer. The question answering lesson measured that the explicit grounding and refusal instruction cuts hallucination by 40 to 60 percent against a naive prompt.

Gate 3: after generation.

Parse the answer and check each claim against the passage that its anchor cites. A claim without an anchor, or with an anchor whose passage does not state the claim, is dropped or sent back for a re-ask. An answer that slipped past the score gate then cannot ship a claim that its cited passage does not support.

How to measure it.

  • Refusal calibration. On a set of questions with no answer in the store, the share that the system refuses.
  • False confidence rate. The share of those questions that got an answer anyway.
  • Wrong refusal rate. On in-corpus questions, the share refused. A threshold that is too high fails here.

Move the threshold on the held-out set, never on production anecdotes, and re-tune it when the embedder, the reranker or the corpus changes.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Tell the model to be careful." An instruction cannot see the retrieval scores; the first gate belongs before generation.
  • "Refuse when retrieval returns fewer than k chunks." A count is not confidence; weak chunks still fill k slots.
  • "Set the threshold once from the fixture." It is corpus specific and moves when the embedder, the reranker or the corpus changes.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) Where does the threshold come from? Expected: the distribution of rank-1 scores on a held-out set with known in-corpus and out-of-corpus questions; it is specific to the corpus.
  • (level 3) What does the generator prompt need beyond "use only the context"? Expected: cite each claim with its anchor and say it does not know when the snippets do not answer; the explicit instruction cut hallucination by 40 to 60 percent in the question answering lesson.
  • (level 4) Too many refusals are also a failure. How do you balance it? Expected: track the false confidence rate on out-of-corpus questions next to the wrong refusal rate on in-corpus questions, and move the threshold on the held-out set.
  • (level 4) How does the citation post-filter work with the refusal gate? Expected: each claim is checked against the passage its anchor cites, and a claim with no anchor or with a passage that does not state it is dropped or re-asked, so a weak answer that passed the score gate cannot ship an unsupported claim.

Where it is taught. The lesson end-to-end RAG builds the refuse path and the cited generator, the lesson question answering defines refusal calibration, the capstone production RAG adds the unsure mode, and the lesson cross-encoder logs the rank-1 score.
