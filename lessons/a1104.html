In plain words first
The idea in one line

Text is discrete. Math is continuous. Every time you ask an LLM to find "similar" documents, compare meanings, or search beyond keywords, you're relying on a bridge between these two worlds. That bridge is an embedding. If you don't understand embeddings, you don't understand modern AI. You just use it.

Terms you'll hear

The problem

In plain words first

An embedding model turns a piece of text into a list of a few hundred or a few thousand numbers, a vector, trained so that texts with similar meanings get vectors pointing in similar directions. Search then becomes geometry: embed the query, and return the stored texts whose vectors are closest, usually by cosine similarity. That finds "transaction failed" for "my payment didn't go through" even though they share no words. This lesson builds the whole loop from scratch with a TF-IDF stand-in, then runs a real 33M-parameter embedding model on the lesson's support-ticket example, adds a cross-encoder reranker, and measures what compression (fewer dimensions, int8, single bits) and HNSW approximate search cost in recall on 3,000 Wikipedia paragraphs. No model is trained.

You have 10,000 support tickets. A customer writes "my payment didn't go through." You need to find similar past tickets. Keyword search finds tickets containing "payment" and "didn't go through." It misses "transaction failed," "charge was declined," and "billing error." These tickets describe the exact same problem with completely different words.

This is the vocabulary mismatch problem. Human language has dozens of ways to say the same thing. Keyword search treats each word as an independent symbol with no meaning. It cannot know that "declined" and "didn't go through" refer to the same concept.

You need a representation of text where meaning, not spelling, determines similarity. You need a way to place "my payment didn't go through" and "transaction was declined" close together in some mathematical space, while pushing "my payment arrived on time" far away despite sharing the word "payment."

That representation is an embedding.

Idea 1 · What Is an Embedding?

An embedding is a dense vector of floating-point numbers that represents the meaning of text. The word "dense" matters -- every dimension carries information, unlike sparse representations (bag-of-words, TF-IDF) where most dimensions are zero.

"The cat sat on the mat" becomes something like [0.023, -0.041, 0.087, ..., 0.012] -- a list of a few hundred to a few thousand numbers depending on the model (384 for the small model used below, 1,024 to 4,096 for current large ones). These numbers encode meaning. You never inspect them directly. You compare them.

Idea 2 · The Word2Vec Breakthrough

In 2013, Tomas Mikolov and colleagues at Google published Word2Vec. The core insight: train a neural network to predict a word from its neighbors (or neighbors from a word), and the hidden layer weights become meaningful vector representations.

The famous result:

king - man + woman = queen

Vector arithmetic on word embeddings captures semantic relationships. The direction from "man" to "woman" is roughly the same as the direction from "king" to "queen." This was the moment the field realized that geometry could encode meaning.

Word2Vec produced 300-dimensional vectors. Each word got one vector regardless of context. "Bank" in "river bank" and "bank account" had the same embedding. This limitation drove the next decade of research.

Idea 3 · From Words to Sentences

Word embeddings represent single tokens. Production systems need to embed entire sentences, paragraphs, or documents. Four approaches emerged:

Averaging: take the mean of all word vectors in the sentence. Cheap, lossy, surprisingly decent for short text. Loses word order entirely -- "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" get identical embeddings.

CLS token: transformer models (BERT, 2018) output a special [CLS] token embedding that represents the entire input. Better than averaging but the [CLS] token was trained for next-sentence prediction, not similarity.

Contrastive learning: train the model explicitly to push similar pairs together and dissimilar pairs apart. Sentence-BERT (Reimers & Gurevych, 2019) used this approach and became the foundation for modern embedding models. Given "How do I reset my password?" and "I need to change my password," the model learns these should have nearly identical vectors.

Instruction-tuned embeddings: the current approach. Models expect a prefix or instruction that says what the text is for: "query: " and "passage: " for E5, "search_query: " and "search_document: " for Nomic, a task instruction for Qwen3-Embedding, and an optional "Represent this sentence for searching relevant passages: " before queries for BGE. Use exactly the prefixes the model card gives; the wrong prefix quietly costs accuracy.

Idea 5 · Similarity Metrics

Given two embedding vectors, three ways to measure how similar they are:

Cosine similarity: the cosine of the angle between two vectors. Ranges from -1 (opposite) to 1 (identical direction). Ignores magnitude -- a 10-word sentence and a 500-word document can score 1.0 if they point the same direction. This is the default for 90% of use cases.

cosine_sim(a,b)=∥a∥⋅∥b∥dot(a,b)​

Dot product: the raw inner product of two vectors. Identical to cosine similarity when vectors are normalized (unit length). Faster to compute. OpenAI's embeddings are normalized, so dot product and cosine give the same ranking.

dot(a,b)=i∑​ai​bi​

Euclidean (L2) distance: straight-line distance in the vector space. Smaller = more similar. Sensitive to magnitude differences. Use when the absolute position in space matters, not just the direction.

L2(a,b)=i∑​(ai​−bi​)2​

When to use which:

Metric Use when Avoid when
Cosine similarity Comparing texts of different lengths; most retrieval tasks Magnitude carries information
Dot product Embeddings are already normalized; maximum speed Vectors have varying magnitudes
Euclidean distance Clustering; spatial nearest-neighbor problems Comparing documents of wildly different lengths
The same idea as a formula

For vectors a,b∈Rd:

cos(a,b)=∥a∥∥b∥a⋅b​,∥a−b∥2=∥a∥2+∥b∥2−2a⋅b.
If every vector is normalised to length 1, then ∥a−b∥2=2−2cos(a,b): cosine, dot product and Euclidean distance give the same ranking, which is why embedding models ship normalised vectors.

Contrastive training (InfoNCE), which makes those geometries meaningful: for a query q, its matching passage p+ and the other passages pj​ in the batch,

L=−log∑j​ecos(q,pj​)/τecos(q,p+)/τ​,
with a temperature τ around 0.01 to 0.05. It pulls matching pairs together and pushes everything else apart.

Worked example
Unit vectors, three metrics, one ranking
  • Two unit vectors at cosine 0.80: squared distance 2−2(0.80)=0.40, distance 0.632.
  • At cosine 0.60: 2−2(0.60)=0.80, distance 0.894. Higher cosine, smaller distance, same order.
  • Scale one vector by 3 without normalising: the cosine is unchanged, the dot product triples and the distance changes. Only cosine ignores length, which is why it is the safe default.
  • Storage: 3,000 vectors × 384 dimensions × 4 bytes = 4.6 MB in float32; one bit per dimension is 48 bytes per vector, 144 KB.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "An embedding search finds documents with the query's words."
    ✓ It finds documents whose vectors point the same way; they may share no words, and sharing words does not guarantee similarity (watch the "payment arrived on time" ticket below).
  • ✗ "Cosine, dot product and Euclidean give different results."
    ✓ For unit-length vectors they give the same ranking; they differ only for unnormalised vectors.
  • ✗ "Cutting an embedding to its first half keeps half the quality."
    ✓ Only for Matryoshka-trained models is the front of the vector the most informative part.

Idea 6 · Vector Databases and HNSW

A brute-force similarity search compares the query against every stored vector. At 1 million vectors with 1536 dimensions, that is 1.5 billion multiply-add operations per query. Too slow.

Vector databases solve this with Approximate Nearest Neighbor (ANN) algorithms. The dominant algorithm is HNSW (Hierarchical Navigable Small World):

  1. Build a multi-layer graph of vectors
  2. Top layers are sparse -- long-range connections between distant clusters
  3. Bottom layers are dense -- fine-grained connections between nearby vectors
  4. Search starts at the top layer, greedily descending to refine
  5. Returns approximate top-k results in O(logn) time instead of O(n)

HNSW trades a small accuracy loss (typically 95-99% recall) for massive speed gains. At 10 million vectors, brute force takes seconds. HNSW takes milliseconds.

Idea 7 · Chunking Strategies

Documents are too long to embed as single vectors. A 50-page PDF covers dozens of topics -- its embedding becomes an average of everything, similar to nothing specific. You split documents into chunks and embed each one.

Fixed-size chunking: split every N tokens with M-token overlap. Simple and predictable. Works well when documents have no clear structure. A 512-token chunk with 50-token overlap: chunk 1 is tokens 0-511, chunk 2 is tokens 462-973.

Sentence-based chunking: split at sentence boundaries, grouping sentences until reaching the token limit. Each chunk is at least one complete sentence. Better than fixed-size because you never cut a thought in half.

Recursive chunking: try splitting at the largest boundary first (section headers). If still too large, try paragraph boundaries. Then sentence boundaries. Then character limits. This is LangChain's RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter and it works well for mixed-format corpora.

Semantic chunking: embed each sentence, then group consecutive sentences whose embeddings are similar. When the embedding similarity drops below a threshold, start a new chunk. Expensive (requires embedding every sentence individually) but produces the most coherent chunks.

Strategy Complexity Quality Best for
Fixed-size Low Decent Unstructured text, logs
Sentence-based Low Good Articles, emails
Recursive Medium Good Markdown, HTML, mixed docs
Semantic High Best Critical retrieval quality

The sweet spot for most systems: 256-512 token chunks with 50-token overlap.

Idea 8 · Bi-Encoders vs Cross-Encoders

A bi-encoder embeds the query and documents independently, then compares vectors. Fast -- you embed the query once and compare against pre-computed document embeddings. This is what you use for retrieval.

A cross-encoder takes the query and a document as a single input and outputs a relevance score. Slow -- it processes each query-document pair through the full model. But far more accurate because it can attend across query and document tokens simultaneously.

The production pattern: bi-encoder retrieves top-100 candidates, cross-encoder reranks them to top-10. This is the retrieve-then-rerank pipeline.

Reranking models: hosted ones from Cohere and Voyage, and open-weight ones such as the BGE rerankers, Qwen3-Reranker and the small MS MARCO cross-encoders (one is used below).

Idea 9 · Matryoshka Embeddings

Traditional embeddings are all-or-nothing. A 1536-dimensional vector uses 1536 floats. You cannot truncate to 256 dimensions without retraining.

Matryoshka Representation Learning (Kusupati et al., 2022) fixes this. The model is trained so that the first N dimensions capture the most important information, like a Russian nesting doll. Truncating a 1536-d Matryoshka embedding to 256 dimensions loses some accuracy but remains functional.

OpenAI's text-embedding-3-small and text-embedding-3-large support Matryoshka truncation via the dimensions parameter; OpenAI reported that text-embedding-3-large shortened to 256 dimensions still beat its previous model (text-embedding-ada-002) at its full 1,536. Truncating a model that was not trained this way loses much more: the run below measures it.

Idea 10 · Binary Quantization

A 1536-dimensional embedding stored as float32 uses 6,144 bytes. Multiply by 10 million documents: 61 GB just for vectors.

Binary quantization converts each float to a single bit: positive values become 1, negative values become 0. Storage drops from 6,144 bytes to 192 bytes -- a 32x reduction. Similarity is computed using Hamming distance (count differing bits), which CPUs can do in a single instruction.

The accuracy hit is around 5-10% on retrieval recall. The common pattern: binary quantization for the first-pass search over millions of vectors, then rescore the top-1000 with full-precision vectors. This gets you 95%+ of full-precision accuracy at 32x less memory.

Step 1 · Text Chunking

We build a semantic search engine from scratch. No vector database. No external embedding API. Pure Python with numpy for the math.

def chunk_text(text, chunk_size=200, overlap=50):
    words = text.split()
    chunks = []
    start = 0
    while start < len(words):
        end = start + chunk_size
        chunk = " ".join(words[start:end])
        chunks.append(chunk)
        start += chunk_size - overlap
    return chunks


def chunk_by_sentences(text, max_chunk_tokens=200):
    sentences = text.replace("\n", " ").split(".")
    sentences = [s.strip() + "." for s in sentences if s.strip()]
    chunks = []
    current_chunk = []
    current_length = 0
    for sentence in sentences:
        sentence_length = len(sentence.split())
        if current_length + sentence_length > max_chunk_tokens and current_chunk:
            chunks.append(" ".join(current_chunk))
            current_chunk = []
            current_length = 0
        current_chunk.append(sentence)
        current_length += sentence_length
    if current_chunk:
        chunks.append(" ".join(current_chunk))
    return chunks

Step 2 · Building Embeddings from Scratch

We implement a simple dense embedding using TF-IDF with L2 normalization. This is not a neural embedding, but it follows the same contract: text in, fixed-size vector out, similar texts produce similar vectors.

import math
import numpy as np
from collections import Counter

class SimpleEmbedder:
    def __init__(self):
        self.vocab = []
        self.idf = []
        self.word_to_idx = {}

    def fit(self, documents):
        vocab_set = set()
        for doc in documents:
            vocab_set.update(doc.lower().split())
        self.vocab = sorted(vocab_set)
        self.word_to_idx = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(self.vocab)}
        n = len(documents)
        self.idf = np.zeros(len(self.vocab))
        for i, word in enumerate(self.vocab):
            doc_count = sum(1 for doc in documents if word in doc.lower().split())
            self.idf[i] = math.log((n + 1) / (doc_count + 1)) + 1

    def embed(self, text):
        words = text.lower().split()
        count = Counter(words)
        total = len(words) if words else 1
        vec = np.zeros(len(self.vocab))
        for word, freq in count.items():
            if word in self.word_to_idx:
                tf = freq / total
                vec[self.word_to_idx[word]] = tf * self.idf[self.word_to_idx[word]]
        norm = np.linalg.norm(vec)
        if norm > 0:
            vec = vec / norm
        return vec

Step 3 · Similarity Functions

def cosine_similarity(a, b):
    dot = np.dot(a, b)
    norm_a = np.linalg.norm(a)
    norm_b = np.linalg.norm(b)
    if norm_a == 0 or norm_b == 0:
        return 0.0
    return float(dot / (norm_a * norm_b))


def dot_product(a, b):
    return float(np.dot(a, b))


def euclidean_distance(a, b):
    return float(np.linalg.norm(a - b))

Step 4 · Vector Index with Brute-Force Search

class VectorIndex:
    def __init__(self):
        self.vectors = []
        self.texts = []
        self.metadata = []

    def add(self, vector, text, meta=None):
        self.vectors.append(vector)
        self.texts.append(text)
        self.metadata.append(meta or {})

    def search(self, query_vector, top_k=5, metric="cosine"):
        scores = []
        for i, vec in enumerate(self.vectors):
            if metric == "cosine":
                score = cosine_similarity(query_vector, vec)
            elif metric == "dot":
                score = dot_product(query_vector, vec)
            elif metric == "euclidean":
                score = -euclidean_distance(query_vector, vec)
            else:
                raise ValueError(f"Unknown metric: {metric}")
            scores.append((i, score))
        scores.sort(key=lambda x: x[1], reverse=True)
        results = []
        for idx, score in scores[:top_k]:
            results.append({
                "text": self.texts[idx],
                "score": score,
                "metadata": self.metadata[idx],
                "index": idx
            })
        return results

    def size(self):
        return len(self.vectors)

Step 5 · The Semantic Search Engine

class SemanticSearchEngine:
    def __init__(self, chunk_size=200, overlap=50):
        self.embedder = SimpleEmbedder()
        self.index = VectorIndex()
        self.chunk_size = chunk_size
        self.overlap = overlap

    def index_documents(self, documents, source_names=None):
        all_chunks = []
        all_sources = []
        for i, doc in enumerate(documents):
            chunks = chunk_text(doc, self.chunk_size, self.overlap)
            all_chunks.extend(chunks)
            name = source_names[i] if source_names else f"doc_{i}"
            all_sources.extend([name] * len(chunks))
        self.embedder.fit(all_chunks)
        for chunk, source in zip(all_chunks, all_sources):
            vec = self.embedder.embed(chunk)
            self.index.add(vec, chunk, {"source": source})
        return len(all_chunks)

    def search(self, query, top_k=5, metric="cosine"):
        query_vec = self.embedder.embed(query)
        return self.index.search(query_vec, top_k, metric)

    def search_with_scores(self, query, top_k=5):
        results = self.search(query, top_k)
        return [
            {
                "text": r["text"][:200],
                "source": r["metadata"].get("source", "unknown"),
                "score": round(r["score"], 4)
            }
            for r in results
        ]

Step 6 · Comparing Similarity Metrics

def compare_metrics(engine, query, top_k=3):
    results = {}
    for metric in ["cosine", "dot", "euclidean"]:
        hits = engine.search(query, top_k=top_k, metric=metric)
        results[metric] = [
            {"score": round(h["score"], 4), "preview": h["text"][:80]}
            for h in hits
        ]
    return results

    "I want to change the email address on my account",
    "The app crashes when I open the settings page",
    "Can I get a refund for a duplicate order?",
    "My package was delivered to the wrong address",
    "How do I reset my password?",
    "The discount code did not apply to my cart",
    "Please cancel my subscription at the end of the month",
    "The tracking number does not show any updates",
    "I cannot log in after updating the app",

    "Your support chat closed before I got an answer",
    "Is there a student discount on the annual plan?",
]
PROBLEM = set(range(6))

# 1) the lesson's TF-IDF embedder (word overlap)
docs = [t.lower().split() for t in TICKETS]
vocab = sorted({w for d in docs for w in d})
idf = {w: math.log((len(docs) + 1) / (sum(w in d for d in docs) + 1)) + 1 for w in vocab}
def tfidf(words):
    c = Counter(words); v = np.array([c[w] / len(words) * idf[w] for w in vocab]); n = np.linalg.norm(v)
    return v / n if n else v
T = np.stack([tfidf(d) for d in docs]); q = tfidf(QUERY.lower().split())

# 2) a real bi-encoder: BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5, 33M parameters, 384 dimensions
enc = SentenceTransformer("BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5", device="cpu")
E = enc.encode(TICKETS, normalize_embeddings=True)
qe = enc.encode("Represent this sentence for searching relevant passages: " + QUERY, normalize_embeddings=True)

def show(name, scores, k=6):

    hits = sum(i in PROBLEM for i in order)
    print(f"\n{name}: {hits} of the top {k} are payment problems")
    for r, i in enumerate(order, 1):
        tag = "problem" if i in PROBLEM else ("NO problem" if i == 6 else "other")
        print(f"  {r}. {scores[i]:+.3f}  [{tag:10}] {TICKETS[i]}")
print(f"query: {QUERY!r}   ({len(TICKETS)} tickets, 6 describe a failed payment)")
show("TF-IDF (shared words)", T @ q)
show("bge-small bi-encoder (cosine)", E @ qe)

ce = CrossEncoder("cross-encoder/ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2", device="cpu")
top = np.argsort(-(E @ qe))[:10]
ce_scores = ce.predict([(QUERY, TICKETS[i]) for i in top])
full = np.full(len(TICKETS), -1e9); full[top] = ce_scores

Reading this output

TF-IDF puts the one ticket that is not a problem at the top: "My payment arrived on time" shares the words "my" and "payment" with the query and scores 0.376. Only 3 of its top 6 are failed payments; "How do I reset my password?" sneaks in on the word "my". "Transaction failed when I tried to pay for my order", the closest paraphrase, is not in its top 6 at all.

The bi-encoder returns all 6 failed-payment tickets as its top 6, with cosines from 0.69 to 0.76, and none of them needs to share a word with the query. That is the vocabulary-mismatch problem solved by geometry.

The cross-encoder made it worse here. The MS MARCO reranker was trained to score web passages by topical relevance, and it pulls "My payment arrived on time" up to second place: the passage is about the same topic, not the same problem. Rerankers are trained for a notion of relevance; when yours differs (problem versus no problem, intent versus topic), check before you add one, and consider fine-tuning or a reranker trained on similar data.

Metrics. A vector and three times itself have cosine 1.000 but a Euclidean distance of 2.000 (for a unit vector). BGE returns unit-length vectors, so dot product and cosine are the same number.

import time, numpy as np, faiss
from datasets import load_dataset
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer

# 2,000 real Wikipedia paragraphs (WikiText-103) as the corpus; 200 other paragraphs' first sentences as queries
rows = [r for r in load_dataset("Salesforce/wikitext", "wikitext-103-raw-v1", split="train[:40000]")["text"]
        if len(r.split()) > 60 and not r.strip().startswith("=")]
docs, queries = rows[:2000], [r.split(" . ")[0] for r in rows[2000:2200]]
enc = SentenceTransformer("BAAI/bge-small-en-v1.5", device="cpu")
enc.max_seq_length = 128                              # first 128 tokens of each paragraph: CPU budget
t = time.time()
D = enc.encode(docs, batch_size=64, normalize_embeddings=True).astype(np.float32)
Q = enc.encode(queries, batch_size=64, normalize_embeddings=True).astype(np.float32)
print(f"embedded {len(docs):,} paragraphs and {len(queries)} queries in {time.time() - t:.0f} s; {D.shape[1]} dimensions")

def topk(S, k=10): return np.argsort(-S, axis=1)[:, :k]
truth = topk(Q @ D.T)                                   # exact float32 top 10 is the reference
def overlap(pred): return np.mean([len(set(p) & set(t)) / len(t) for p, t in zip(pred, truth)])
def row(name, pred, bytes_per_vec, note=""):
    print(f"  {name:34} recall@10 {overlap(pred):6.1%} | {bytes_per_vec:5} bytes/vector {note}")

print("\nA. Compressing the vectors (agreement with the exact float32 top 10):")
row("float32, 384 dims", truth, 384 * 4)
for d in (192, 96):
    Dt = D[:, :d] / np.linalg.norm(D[:, :d], axis=1, keepdims=True); Qt = Q[:, :d] / np.linalg.norm(Q[:, :d], axis=1, keepdims=True)
    row(f"first {d} dims (not Matryoshka-trained)", topk(Qt @ Dt.T), d * 4)
scale = np.abs(D).max(0) / 127                           # one scale per dimension, from the corpus
Di = np.round(D / scale).astype(np.int8)                 # stored: 1 byte per dimension
row("int8 scalar quantization", topk(Q @ (Di * scale).T), 384)   # scores use the de-quantized vectors
Db, Qb = np.packbits(D > 0, axis=1), np.packbits(Q > 0, axis=1)
ham = np.unpackbits(Qb[:, None, :] ^ Db[None, :, :], axis=2).sum(2)
row("binary (sign bits), Hamming", np.argsort(ham, axis=1)[:, :10], 48)
cand = np.argsort(ham, axis=1)[:, :100]                  # binary first pass, then rescore 100 candidates in float32
res = np.array([c[np.argsort(-(D[c] @ q))[:10]] for c, q in zip(cand, Q)])
row("binary top 100, rescored in float32", res, 48, "(+ floats kept on disk)")

print("\nB. Approximate nearest neighbours: HNSW (faiss) against exact search, the 200 queries searched 5 times for timing")
Qm = np.repeat(Q, 5, axis=0)
flat = faiss.IndexFlatIP(D.shape[1]); flat.add(D)
t = time.time(); _, I_exact = flat.search(Qm, 10); t_exact = time.time() - t
hnsw = faiss.IndexHNSWFlat(D.shape[1], 32, faiss.METRIC_INNER_PRODUCT)
t = time.time(); hnsw.add(D); t_build = time.time() - t
for ef in (16, 64):
    hnsw.hnsw.efSearch = ef
    t = time.time(); _, I = hnsw.search(Qm, 10); dt = time.time() - t
    rec = np.mean([len(set(a) & set(b)) / 10 for a, b in zip(I, I_exact)])
    print(f"  HNSW M=32 efSearch={ef:<3} recall@10 {rec:6.1%} | {dt * 1000 / len(Qm):.3f} ms/query (exact {t_exact * 1000 / len(Qm):.3f} ms) | built in {t_build:.1f} s")
print(f"  at 2,000 vectors exact search is already fast; HNSW pays off at millions (exact cost grows with n)")

Reading this output

Truncating dimensions breaks this model. Keeping the first 192 of 384 dimensions finds only 53.1% of the true top 10, and the first 96 only 28.7%. bge-small was not trained with Matryoshka representation learning, so its information is spread over all dimensions. Models trained that way (OpenAI's text-embedding-3, Voyage 4, Gemini Embedding, Nomic and others) put the most important information first and are designed to be cut. Check the model card before truncating.

int8 is nearly free. One byte per dimension instead of four, with a scale per dimension taken from the corpus, keeps 98.8% of the top 10. A quarter of the memory for about one result in a hundred.

Binary is 32 times smaller and needs a second pass. One sign bit per dimension (48 bytes per vector) compared by Hamming distance finds only 41.8% of the top 10 on its own. Use it as a fast first pass: take its top 100, rescore those in float32, and agreement rises to 88.3%. The float vectors are still needed, but they can sit on disk because only 100 per query are read.

HNSW: near-exact at small scale, and not yet faster. With efSearch=64 it returns 99.6% of the exact top 10, and 94.7% at 16. At 2,000 vectors exact search takes a fraction of a millisecond, so HNSW has nothing to save; at 64 it was even slower here. The point of HNSW is that its cost grows roughly with the logarithm of the corpus size, while exact search grows linearly. The gap appears at millions of vectors, not thousands.

easy15 minimplement

A vector search finds the documents whose embeddings point in the same direction as a query embedding. Cosine similarity measures that direction and ignores the length of the vectors. The retrieval step of a RAG system starts with this search.

Task

Write three functions.

  1. normalize_rows(X) returns a copy of X with each row divided by its L2 norm.
  2. cosine_similarity_matrix(Q, D) returns the cosine similarity of each query row against each document row.
  3. top_k(Q, D, k) returns, for each query, the indices of the k documents with the highest cosine similarity.

The cosine similarity of vectors q and d is ∥q∥∥d∥q⋅d​.

Zero vector rule: a row with norm 0 has no direction. normalize_rows leaves it as a row of zeros. Its cosine similarity with any vector is 0.0.

Tie rule: top_k sorts the scores from high to low. When two documents have the same score, the document with the lower index comes first.

Input and output

Name Type Shape Meaning
X float array (n, dim) any matrix of row vectors
Q float array (n_queries, dim) one query embedding in each row
D float array (n_docs, dim) one document embedding in each row
k int scalar the number of documents to return for each query
  • normalize_rows returns a new float array of shape (n, dim).
  • cosine_similarity_matrix returns a float array of shape (n_queries, n_docs). Entry (i, j) is the score of query i against document j.
  • top_k returns an int array of shape (n_queries, k). Row i holds document indices, best first.

Constraints

  • 1 <= n_queries <= 64, 1 <= n_docs <= 1000 and 1 <= dim <= 1024.
  • 1 <= k <= n_docs.
  • Rows of Q and D can be zero vectors.
  • Do not change the input arrays.
  • Use numpy only.

Common mistakes the checks catch
  • Takes the norm over axis 0, so it normalizes columns and not rows.
  • Divides the input array in place, so the caller's vectors change.
  • Divides by a zero norm, so a zero vector gives NaN and not 0.
  • Sorts ascending and reverses the order, so equal scores put the higher index first.
  • Drops the best match and returns ranks 2 to k + 1, as if it removed a self match.
Hint 1

Use np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True) to get one norm for each row, with shape (n, 1).

Hint 2

A zero norm gives a division by zero. Replace each zero norm with 1 before you divide. A zero row divided by 1 stays a zero row, so its scores are 0.

Hint 3

After you normalize both matrices, one matrix product gives all the scores. The shape of the result is (n_queries, n_docs).

Hint 4

Sort the negated scores with np.argsort(..., axis=1, kind="stable"). A stable sort keeps equal scores in index order, so the lower index comes first. Then keep the first k columns.

Reference solution and notes
import numpy as np


def normalize_rows(X):
    norms = np.linalg.norm(X, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    safe = np.where(norms > 0, norms, 1.0)
    return X / safe


def cosine_similarity_matrix(Q, D):
    return normalize_rows(Q) @ normalize_rows(D).T


def top_k(Q, D, k):
    scores = cosine_similarity_matrix(Q, D)
    order = np.argsort(-scores, axis=1, kind="stable")
    return order[:, :k]

Why normalize first

After normalization every nonzero row has length 1. The dot product of two unit vectors is the cosine of the angle between them. One matrix product then scores every query against every document. Many vector indexes store normalized embeddings for this reason and search with the dot product.

The zero vector

A zero vector has no direction, so the cosine is not defined. The lesson function cosine_similarity returns 0 for this case. A zero embedding can come from an empty chunk or from a text with no known words. If you divide by its norm, you get NaN, and NaN breaks the sort.

The tie rule

Exact ties occur more often than you expect. Duplicate chunks give the same score, and so do zero vectors. A stable sort on the negated scores keeps index order inside a tie. The lesson gets the same order from the Python sort with reverse=True, which is also stable.

# Embedding Patterns

Every embedding workflow follows this contract:

```
text -> embed(text) -> vector (float array)
similarity(vector_a, vector_b) -> score (float)
```

The embedding model and similarity metric are the only two decisions that matter. Everything else is plumbing.

## When to use embeddings

- Semantic search across documents (find meaning, not keywords)
- Clustering similar items (support tickets, product reviews, bug reports)
- Classification by nearest neighbors (label new items by similarity to labeled examples)
- Recommendation systems (find items similar to what the user liked)
- Deduplication (find near-duplicate content using similarity threshold)

## When NOT to use embeddings

- Exact keyword matching (use full-text search)
- Structured queries (use SQL, filters)
- Small datasets where manual labeling is faster (<100 items)
- Tasks where explainability matters more than accuracy (embeddings are opaque)

## Model selection

Pick based on your constraints:

- **Need an API, best value**: OpenAI text-embedding-3-small (1536d, $0.02/1M tokens)
- **Need maximum accuracy**: Voyage-3 (1024d, $0.06/1M tokens, highest MTEB)
- **Need local/private**: BGE-M3 (1024d, free, multilingual, GPU recommended)
- **Need fast local prototyping**: all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (384d, free, runs on CPU)
- **Need multilingual**: Cohere embed-v3 (1024d) or BGE-M3 (both strong multilingual)

Rule: never mix embedding models between indexing and querying. Vectors from different models live in incompatible spaces.

## Chunking rules

1. Target 256-512 tokens per chunk with 50-token overlap
2. Never split mid-sentence if you can avoid it
3. Include metadata (source file, section title, position) with every chunk
4. For structured docs (Markdown, HTML), split at heading boundaries first
5. Test chunk quality by searching for known answers and checking retrieval

## Similarity metric selection

- **Cosine similarity**: default choice, handles variable-length text, normalized
- **Dot product**: use when vectors are already unit-normalized (OpenAI models are), slightly faster
- **Euclidean distance**: use for clustering, when absolute position matters

All three give the same ranking when vectors are normalized. The choice only matters for non-normalized vectors.

## Storage optimization

Three levels of compression, stackable:

1. **Matryoshka truncation**: reduce dimensions (1536 -> 256 = 6x savings, 3-5% accuracy loss)
2. **Float16 quantization**: halve storage per dimension (2x savings, <1% accuracy loss)
3. **Binary quantization**: 1 bit per dimension (32x savings, 5-10% accuracy loss, use with rescoring)

Production pattern: binary search over full corpus, rescore top-1000 with float32 vectors.

## Retrieve-then-rerank

Two-stage pipeline for best accuracy:

1. Bi-encoder retrieves top-100 candidates (fast, uses pre-computed embeddings)
2. Cross-encoder reranks to top-10 (slow, processes each query-doc pair)

This beats single-stage retrieval by 10-15% on precision metrics. Use when accuracy matters more than latency.

## Common mistakes

- Using different embedding models for indexing and querying
- Embedding entire documents instead of chunks (embedding becomes average of everything)
- Not normalizing vectors before cosine similarity (most models pre-normalize, but verify)
- Ignoring chunk overlap (sentences split at boundaries lose context)
- Storing only vectors without the original text (you need both for retrieval)
- Not re-embedding when the model changes (old vectors are incompatible)
- Choosing dimensions based on accuracy alone (storage and latency scale linearly with dimensions)

## Debugging embeddings

If search results are poor:

1. Verify the query embedding is non-zero (empty or whitespace input produces zero vectors)
2. Check a known-relevant document's similarity score manually
3. Try rephrasing the query to match document vocabulary
4. Inspect chunk boundaries to ensure relevant content is not split across chunks
5. Compare top-k results across metrics (cosine, dot, euclidean) to spot normalization issues
6. Test with a trivially matching query (copy a sentence from a document) to confirm the pipeline works

## Production parameters

- Chunk size: 256-512 tokens
- Chunk overlap: 50 tokens (10-20% of chunk size)
- Top-k retrieval: 5-10 for direct use, 50-100 for reranking
- Similarity threshold: 0.7+ for cosine (below this, results are usually irrelevant)
- Batch embedding: process 100-500 texts per API call for throughput
- Index rebuild: re-embed when the model changes or documents update significantly

Solutions

SolutionP1 · TF-IDF picks the wrong ticket

TF-IDF ranks "My payment arrived on time, thanks for the quick confirmation" first (0.376), because it shares "my" and "payment" with the query; "Transaction failed when I tried to pay for my order" shares only "my" and does not make its top 6. The neural embedding ranks the transaction-failed ticket second (0.754) and keeps the on-time ticket out of its top 6: it compares meanings, not words.

SolutionB1 · Unit vectors make the metrics agree; mixed models make nonsense

Euclidean distance mixes direction and length: ∥a−b∥2=∥a∥2+∥b∥2−2a⋅b. If the vectors are not unit length, a ticket can rank higher just because its vector is shorter or longer, so the ranking can differ from cosine. Once every vector is normalised the distance is 2−2cos​ and the ranking is exactly the cosine ranking.

Mixing models is worse: two embedding models place texts in unrelated coordinate systems. If the dimensions differ the dot product fails with a shape error; if they happen to match, it runs and returns numbers that mean nothing. Every vector in an index must come from the same model and version, and changing the model means re-embedding the whole corpus.

Q1What problem do embeddings solve that keyword search cannot?

Keyword search treats words as independent symbols. Embeddings map text to high-dimensional vectors where semantic similarity = geometric proximity. Texts with the same meaning cluster together regardless of word choice.

Q2What does cosine similarity measure between two embedding vectors?

Cosine similarity = dot(A,B) / (|A|*|B|). It ranges from -1 (opposite) to 1 (identical direction). Two texts with the same meaning will have vectors pointing in nearly the same direction, giving cosine similarity near 1.

Q3What is the typical dimensionality of modern text embedding models?

Modern embedding models (OpenAI text-embedding-3, BGE, E5) produce vectors with 768 to 3072 dimensions. Higher dimensions capture more nuance but cost more to store and search.

Q4Why should you evaluate embedding quality using retrieval benchmarks rather than just inspecting similarity scores?

A cosine similarity of 0.85 might mean 'very similar' for one model and 'somewhat similar' for another. Retrieval metrics (precision@k, recall) measure what actually matters: does the right document come back?

Q5When would you use a local/open-source embedding model instead of an API-based one?

API embeddings (OpenAI, Cohere) are easy but send your data externally. Local models (BGE, E5, Nomic) keep data private, eliminate per-call costs at scale, and can be fine-tuned on domain-specific data.
