The problem

In plain words first

Building a vision-language model means making five choices: the image encoder, the connector that hands its output to the language model, the language model itself, the training data, and the image resolution. Between 2024 and 2026 several research groups ran careful experiments that change one choice at a time. They agree on a short list of rules: the encoder, the resolution and the number of visual tokens matter most; the connector design matters little; the language model sets the reasoning ceiling; and dense human-written descriptions are better training data than captions copied from another model. This lesson walks through those findings and checks one of them against real models: where the parameters of six current open VLMs actually sit. No model is trained or downloaded.

Hundreds of open-weight VLMs exist. Most of the gap between "good" and "state-of-the-art" is not architecture. It is data, resolution schedule, and encoder choice. Knowing which knob to turn first when your model underperforms saves you a 5-million-GPU-hour mistake.

The 2023 wave (LLaVA-1.5, InstructBLIP, MiniGPT-4) ran on caption-pair pretraining + LLaVA-Instruct-150k. Good baseline. Topped out around MMMU 35%.

The 2024 wave (MM1, Idefics2, Molmo, Cambrian-1, Prismatic VLMs) ran exhaustive ablations. Results were surprising and practical.

Idea 1 · The five-axis design space

Idefics2 (Laurençon et al., 2024) named the axes:

  1. Image encoder. CLIP ViT-L/14, SigLIP SO400m/14, DINOv2 ViT-g/14, InternViT-6B. Encoders differ in patch size, resolution, and pretraining objective.
  2. Connector. MLP (2-4 layers), Q-Former (32 queries + cross-attn), Perceiver Resampler (64 queries), C-Abstractor (convolutional + bilinear pooling).
  3. Language model. Llama-3 8B / 70B, Mistral 7B, Phi-3, Gemma-2, Qwen2.5. LLM size is the dominant param cost.
  4. Training data. Caption pairs (CC3M, LAION), interleaved (OBELICS, MMC4), instruction (LLaVA-Instruct, ShareGPT4V, PixMo, Cauldron).
  5. Resolution schedule. Fixed 224/336/448, AnyRes, native dynamic. Ramped during training or constant.

Every production VLM makes a choice on each axis. The ablation papers agree that the encoder, the image resolution and token count, and the data matter far more than which connector you pick.

Idea 2 · Axis 1: encoder > connector

MM1 concluded that "the image encoder together with image resolution and the image token count has substantial impact, while the vision-language connector design is of comparatively negligible importance." Idefics2 found the same direction: a SigLIP So400m encoder beat a larger CLIP ViT-H, and a perceiver resampler with 64 tokens per image cost little quality while saving many tokens.

Cambrian-1 (Tong et al., 2024) compared about 20 vision encoders inside the same VLM recipe and introduced a vision-centric benchmark (CV-Bench). Language-supervised encoders (SigLIP, CLIP) were strongest overall; among self-supervised encoders DINOv2 was best, and it helped most on vision-centric tasks. Combining encoders helped further.

A common default encoder for open VLMs is SigLIP or SigLIP 2 So400m/14, sometimes combined with DINOv2 features for spatial and dense tasks (Cambrian's "Spatial Vision Aggregator" does this). Several model families (Qwen-VL, InternVL) train their own ViTs instead.

Idea 3 · Axis 2: connector design is a wash

MM1 and Idefics2 reached the same conclusion: at a fixed visual-token count, connector architecture matters much less than the encoder, resolution and data. The run below shows why it is also cheap: in current open VLMs the connector is usually under 2% of the parameters.

What does matter is the token count. More visual tokens = more LLM compute = better performance up to a point, then diminishing returns. 64 tokens per image is too few for OCR. 576-1024 tokens is the sweet spot for most open VLMs. 2048+ helps only for documents and charts.

Q-Former vs MLP is a cost question, not a quality question: Q-Former caps tokens at 32-64 regardless of image resolution; MLP emits all patch tokens. For high-res inputs, Q-Former saves LLM context; for low-res, the difference is noise.

The same idea as a formula

Where the parameters sit. A VLM with encoder E, connector C and language model L has N=NE​+NC​+NL​ parameters; for a LLaVA-style MLP connector from encoder width dv​ to LLM width d,

NC​=dv​d+d+d2+d≈d2(d≫dv​).

What a visual token costs at inference. Each of the nimg​ visual tokens passes through all NL​ language-model weights:

FLOPs per image≈2nimg​NL​+2npatch​NE​,
so the token count multiplies the largest term, and the connector barely appears.

A controlled ablation changes one axis a and holds the others fixed: Δ=score(a1​,bˉ)−score(a0​,bˉ). Comparing models from different papers changes many axes at once, which is why the controlled studies matter.

Worked example
A 7B VLM, priced per image
  • LLaVA-OneVision-7B (measured below): encoder 398M, connector 17M, LLM 7.62B.
  • At 729 visual tokens per image: 2×729×7.62B≈11.1 TFLOPs in the LLM, against 2×729×0.40B≈0.58 TFLOPs in the encoder and about 0.02 TFLOPs in the connector.
  • Halving the visual tokens to 364 saves about 5.5 TFLOPs per image. Replacing the MLP connector with anything else changes a rounding error.
  • So the first questions are which encoder, how many tokens, and which data; the connector is a detail.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "A better connector is the easiest way to a better VLM."
    ✓ Controlled studies found the connector matters little at a fixed token count; encoder, resolution, tokens and data matter more.
  • ✗ "More visual tokens are free once the encoder has run."
    ✓ Every visual token is processed by the whole language model, the largest part of the cost.
  • ✗ "Distilling captions from a strong proprietary model is the best data."

Where the parameters sit in real open VLMs

Six open VLMs are built from their published configurations on PyTorch's meta device, so no weights are downloaded. Parameters are grouped into vision encoder, connector (projector, merger or resampler) and language model. A few seconds.

import torch
from transformers import AutoConfig, AutoModelForImageTextToText, logging
logging.set_verbosity_error()

# Six open VLMs built from their published configs on the meta device (no weights): where do the parameters sit?
MODELS = ["HuggingFaceTB/SmolVLM-256M-Instruct", "HuggingFaceM4/idefics2-8b", "llava-hf/llava-onevision-qwen2-7b-ov-hf",
          "OpenGVLab/InternVL3-8B-hf", "Qwen/Qwen2.5-VL-7B-Instruct", "Qwen/Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct"]
def part(name):
    if "merger" in name or "connector" in name or "multi_modal_projector" in name: return "connector"
    if any(k in name for k in ("visual", "vision_model", "vision_tower", "image_newline")): return "encoder"
    return "language model"
print(f"{'model':<40}{'encoder':>10}{'connector':>11}{'LLM':>9}{'connector share':>17}")
for repo in MODELS:
    cfg = AutoConfig.from_pretrained(repo)
    with torch.device("meta"):
        m = AutoModelForImageTextToText.from_config(cfg)
    sizes = {"encoder": 0, "connector": 0, "language model": 0}
    for n, p in m.named_parameters():                  # named_parameters skips tied duplicates
        sizes[part(n)] += p.numel()
    total = sum(sizes.values())
    print(f"{repo.split('/')[1]:<40}{sizes['encoder'] / 1e6:>9.0f}M{sizes['connector'] / 1e6:>10.0f}M"
          f"{sizes['language model'] / 1e9:>8.2f}B{sizes['connector'] / total:>16.1%}")

Reading this output

The connector is tiny almost everywhere. LLaVA-OneVision-7B's MLP projector is 17M parameters, 0.2% of the model. InternVL3-8B's is 28M (0.4%), and Qwen2.5-VL-7B's patch merger is 45M (0.5%). Qwen3-VL's is larger, 160M, because it also feeds features from several encoder depths into the LLM, but it is still under 2%. This is the parameter side of "connector design is a wash": there is little there to optimise.

Idefics2 is the exception, by design. Its connector is a Perceiver resampler with a modality projection, 743M parameters, larger than its own 418M SigLIP encoder. It buys token savings: 64 tokens per image instead of hundreds.

The language model dominates. In every 7–8B model the LLM is 86% to 95% of the parameters. Since every visual token passes through it, it also dominates inference cost. The encoders range from 304M (InternVL3's InternViT-300M) to 632M (Qwen2.5-VL's ViT).

Small models are shaped differently. SmolVLM-256M spends 86M on vision and only 0.16B on its language model. At that size the encoder is a third of the model, which is why small VLMs compress visual tokens aggressively.

Idea 4 · Axis 3: LLM size sets the ceiling

Larger language models consistently score higher on multimodal reasoning benchmarks within each model family (for example Qwen2.5-VL 3B, 7B and 72B). The VLM's multimodal reasoning ceiling is largely the LLM's text reasoning ceiling — the visual encoder can only feed it, not reason for it.

This is why the largest open and hosted models lead on reasoning-heavy benchmarks such as MMMU-Pro. A 7B VLM cannot substitute for a 70B VLM through clever connector design.

Idea 5 · Axis 4: data — detailed human captions beat distillation

Molmo + PixMo (Deitke et al., 2024) is the 2024 result everyone should read. Allen AI had annotators describe images aloud for about a minute each, then transcribed the speech: about 712K densely captioned images. All PixMo data was collected "without the use of external VLMs."

Molmo-72B outperformed larger proprietary models including Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Gemini 1.5 Pro on the paper's academic benchmarks and human evaluation, second only to GPT-4o. The difference is not architecture — it is caption quality: detailed human descriptions carry far more information per image than short web captions and avoid inheriting another model's hallucinations.

ShareGPT4V (Chen et al., 2023) and Cauldron (Idefics2) followed the same playbook with mixed human + GPT-4V captions. The trend is clear: for the 2026 frontier, caption density > caption quantity > distillation convenience.

Idea 6 · Axis 5: resolution and its schedule

Idefics2's ablations found that splitting images into sub-images (feeding higher effective resolution) mainly helps text-heavy tasks such as OCR and document questions, at a large cost in tokens. Many recipes also raise the resolution over training: start low for speed, finish high for detail.

Resolution and token count trade off at fixed compute: more tokens at lower resolution help general scene understanding less than higher resolution helps reading small text.

The 2026 production recipe: train Stage 1 at 384 fixed, Stage 2 with dynamic resolution up to 1280 for OCR-heavy tasks.

Idea 7 · The Prismatic controlled comparison

Prismatic VLMs (Karamcheti et al., 2024) is the paper that controlled the axes: a family of 7B–13B VLMs, the same evaluation suite, one design choice varied at a time. Its main findings:

  • The separate projector-alignment stage of LLaVA-1.5 can be skipped: training everything in one stage worked as well and was cheaper.
  • Fusing DINOv2 and SigLIP features beat either encoder alone, especially on localisation tasks.
  • Base language models did about as well as instruction-tuned ones.
  • Training longer and on more data helped.

It gives no single ranking of axes, but it is the cleanest controlled study of what to ablate first.

Idea 8 · A picker for 2026

Given the evidence, the default open-VLM recipe for a new project in 2026:

  • Encoder: SigLIP 2 So400m/14 at native resolution with NaFlex (or a model family's own ViT), combined with DINOv2 features if you need segmentation or grounding.
  • Connector: 2-layer MLP on patch tokens. Skip Q-Former unless you are token-constrained.
  • LLM: a current open model family (Qwen3, Gemma 3, Llama 3.1), 7–8B for cost, 70B-class for quality, picked by target latency.
  • Data: PixMo + ShareGPT4V + Cauldron, topped up with task-specific instruction data.
  • Resolution: dynamic (min 256, max 1280 pixels per long side).
  • Schedule: Stage 1 alignment (projector-only), Stage 2 full fine-tune, Stage 3 task-specific fine-tune.

Every one of those defaults traces back to a measured ablation in the papers cited at the end of this lesson.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Open VLM families with published recipes: Qwen2.5-VL and Qwen3-VL, InternVL3, Gemma 3, Molmo, SmolVLM and LLaVA-OneVision; their technical reports are the current ablation literature.
  • Fully open data and weights: Molmo/PixMo and similar projects release data, code and weights, so results can be reproduced and audited.
  • Native resolution plus patch merging has replaced fixed square inputs in most recipes (Lesson 12.6).
  • Small VLMs (SmolVLM at 256M–2B, Qwen-VL and InternVL 1–4B sizes) make on-device and low-cost inference practical.

Sources: MM1 (arXiv 2403.09611), Idefics2 (arXiv 2405.02246), Prismatic VLMs (arXiv 2402.07865), Cambrian-1 (arXiv 2406.16860), Molmo and PixMo (arXiv 2409.17146).

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Five axes: encoder, connector, language model, data, resolution schedule.
  • Encoder, resolution and visual-token count matter most; connector design matters least.
  • The language model sets the reasoning ceiling and most of the cost.
  • Dense, human-written captions beat distilled captions.
  • Change one axis at a time; compare within controlled studies.

Given a task mix (OCR, chart, UI agent, reasoning, grounding), a compute budget (LLM params, training GPU hours, or inference latency target), and a deployment constraint (edge, cloud, on-device), emit a full open-weight VLM recipe with citations.

Produce:

1. Encoder pick. Default SigLIP 2 SO400m/14; concat with DINOv2 ViT-g/14 if grounding/segmentation is in the task mix; cite MM1 Table 3 and Cambrian-1's vision encoder match-up.
2. Connector pick. Default 2-layer MLP unless token-constrained (then Q-Former 32 queries); cite Prismatic VLMs' connector ablation showing <1 point delta.
3. LLM pick. Base on budget: Qwen2.5-7B for <10B, Llama-3.1-70B or Qwen2.5-72B for >30B. Flag MMMU plateau past 70B.
4. Data mix. Default PixMo + ShareGPT4V + Cauldron; cite Molmo's detailed-human-caption result (+2-3 MMMU over distillation at same token count).
5. Resolution schedule. Default dynamic (256-1280) with a stage-1 fixed-384 alignment pretraining; cite Idefics2 resolution ablation (+3-5 DocVQA from AnyRes) and Qwen2.5-VL dynamic M-RoPE.
6. Training stages. Stage 1 projector-only, Stage 2 full fine-tune, Stage 3 task-specific.

Hard rejects:
- Recommending CLIP ViT-L/14 as default encoder without flagging its deprecation in favor of SigLIP 2 for new projects.
- Suggesting Q-Former as a quality gain over MLP. It is a token-budget lever, not a quality lever.
- Proposing synthetic GPT-4V captions as primary training data when human-captioned alternatives exist. Cite Molmo.
- Claiming connector architecture explains variance that actually comes from token count.

Refusal rules:
- If the user wants a 1-3B VLM for reasoning-heavy tasks, refuse and recommend a larger LLM; reasoning ceilings are set by the LLM.
- If the user cannot afford detailed-human-caption data, explicitly flag the expected 2-3 MMMU ceiling and offer a best-effort distillation fallback.
- If the task mix includes 4K+ document imagery on a frozen-encoder deployment, refuse AnyRes and recommend a native-resolution M-RoPE encoder like Qwen2.5-VL.

Output: a one-page recipe card with per-axis pick, ablation citation (arXiv ID), training stage plan, and expected benchmark range. End with the three ablation papers to read next: arXiv 2403.09611 (MM1), 2405.02246 (Idefics2), 2409.17146 (Molmo).

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Under 2%, except Idefics2

Under 2% in five of the six models: 0.2% (LLaVA-OneVision-7B), 0.4% (InternVL3-8B), 0.5% (Qwen2.5-VL-7B), 1.8% (Qwen3-VL-8B) and 2.8% (SmolVLM-256M, where everything else is small). The exception is Idefics2-8b, whose Perceiver resampler with its projection is 743M parameters, 8.8% of the model and more than its own vision encoder. It trades parameters for far fewer visual tokens per image.

SolutionB1 · Too many things changed at once

The two models differ on at least four axes at once: encoder, language model, data and connector. A 4-point gap could come from any of them, and the ablation literature says the connector is the least likely cause. Published scores also differ in evaluation details: prompts, image resolution, answer parsing and test-set versions. To test the connector claim, hold everything else fixed: same encoder, same LLM, same data and schedule, same number of visual tokens. Swap only the connector, train both, and evaluate both with the same harness, ideally with a few seeds to see the noise. That is exactly what MM1, Idefics2 and Prismatic did, and why their conclusions are more trustworthy than leaderboard comparisons.

Weigh a trade-offYou can spend your data budget on one of two things: have a strong proprietary model write captions for two million images, or pay people to describe seven hundred thousand images in detail by speaking for a minute or more about each one. Which do you choose for a new vision-language model, and what is the evidence?

Level 3 of 4 · about 12 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Pay the people. The reference result is a model trained on 712K densely captioned images and no distilled captions that beat a much larger model on 11 of 11 benchmarks. Dense human captions carry 5 to 10 times the information of a short web caption and stay grounded in the image, while distilled captions inherit the hallucinations of the model that wrote them; about 12 percent of crawled pairs already describe things that are not in the image. Caption density beats caption quantity, which beats distillation convenience.

The evidence.

  • The dense-caption recipe: annotators described each image for one to three minutes in speech, transcribed to text, which gave 712K densely captioned images. The 72B model trained on them beat a 90B competitor on every one of 11 benchmarks. The difference was not architecture.
  • Two other datasets followed the same path with mixed human and synthetic captions, and the trend across them is that density wins.
  • The first visual instruction set was written by a text-only model from captions and boxes without seeing the image, 158k turns; it worked to unlock dialogue, with noise.

Why density beats volume.

A dense caption of 80 to 300 tokens names objects, counts, relations, text in the image and context; a web alt text names one thing. For alignment and instruction tuning the model learns from what the caption says about the image, so the information per image is the currency, not the image count.

Why distillation is risky.

The writing model does not see what it does not see. It hallucinates plausible content, and the student learns to hallucinate with the same confidence. A benchmark built from similar data does not punish it; production users do.

When distillation still has a place.

  • Bootstrapping a new domain fast, before human data arrives.
  • Generating questions and answers from a human-written caption, which keeps the grounding in the caption.
  • Filtered: score each synthetic caption against the image with a contrastive checker and drop the ungrounded tail.

What to do with the budget.

Dense human captions for the alignment and instruction stages, task-specific instruction data on top, and synthetic data only where a human caption anchors it. Measure grounding on a held-out human-labelled set, not only benchmark accuracy.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Two million captions beat seven hundred thousand; more data wins." The reference result beat a larger model with fewer, denser, grounded captions; volume of thin or hallucinated captions is no substitute.
  • "The strong model's captions are better than what annotators write." They are longer than web captions but not grounded; the model describes what it expects, and the student learns the same habit.
  • "Caption quality only matters for captioning tasks." The reference model gained on every benchmark, reasoning and reading included, because the alignment stage learned more about each image.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) Why did the speech-based collection matter? Expected: people say far more in a minute than they type, so the captions are long and specific at a cost that scales.
  • (level 3) How do you check that a synthetic caption is grounded? Expected: embed the image and the caption with a contrastive checker and flag low similarity, then read a sample; drop or rewrite the tail.
  • (level 4) How would you prove to a skeptic that the gain is data and not model size? Expected: the reference comparison beat a larger model; replicate at one size with two data mixes and the same encoder, connector and schedule.
  • (level 4) Where in the three-stage pipeline do dense captions pay most? Expected: alignment and the first instruction stage, where the projector and the language model learn what an image contains; task-specific data pays in the last stage.

Explain the mechanismYour 7B vision-language model trails a competitor on a multimodal reasoning benchmark by several points. The team's plan is a new connector design. Argue for or against, using what the ablation literature says about where the score comes from.

Level 2 of 4 · about 10 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Against. Across the controlled studies, swapping the connector moved a reasoning benchmark by less than a point at a fixed token count, while swapping the encoder moved it by three or more, and doubling the language model from 7B to 13B added two to four points. The reasoning ceiling of such a model is the text reasoning ceiling of its language model; the visual side can only feed it. Spend the effort on the language model size, the encoder, the token count and the data before the connector.

The decomposition.

The one study that held every axis fixed but one found that the per-image visual-token count explained about 60 percent of the variance in scores, the encoder about 20 percent, the connector about 5 percent, and everything else about 15 percent.

The language model sets the ceiling.

Going from a 7B to a 13B language model reliably added 2 to 4 points on the flagship reasoning benchmark across the papers in the lesson, and at 70B most benchmarks saturate. The reason is structural: the encoder produces features and the connector translates them, but the reasoning happens in the language model. A 7B model cannot stand in for a 70B model through a cleverer connector.

The encoder is the second lever.

Swapping the common contrastive encoder for a stronger one added 3 or more points on the reasoning benchmark in one study, and self-supervised encoders led a vision-centric benchmark by about 5 to 7 points over the common contrastive one. The 2026 default pairs a strong contrastive encoder with a self-supervised one for dense features when grounding matters.

The connector is a wash.

At a fixed token count, an MLP, a query bridge and a resampler landed within about 1 point of each other in several independent studies. The connector is a cost question about tokens, not a quality question.

What to do instead.

  1. Check the token count per image; more visual tokens up to the sweet spot helps more than any connector.
  2. Check the encoder and the resolution schedule.
  3. Check the data: detailed human captions beat distilled synthetic captions at the same token count.
  4. If the gap is in reasoning, the honest fix is a larger language model, and the budget question follows.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The connector is where the modalities meet, so it must matter most." Its measured share is about 5 percent of the variance; the token count and the encoder dominate.
  • "A better connector lets a small language model reason like a large one." The reasoning ceiling is the language model's; no bridge adds reasoning the language model does not have.
  • "More visual tokens always help." They help up to the sweet spot and then give diminishing returns at a growing compute cost; the gain is in the token count, not in the architecture that emits them.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why does a bigger language model help a visual task? Expected: the benchmark asks for reasoning over what the encoder saw, and the reasoning happens in the language model; the visual side sets what it knows, not how well it thinks.
  • (level 2) When would a connector change still be the right move? Expected: when the token budget binds, because then the connector decides how many tokens you can afford, which is the axis that matters most.
  • (level 3) Which benchmarks move with the encoder and which do not? Expected: vision-centric benchmarks, counting and grounding, move with a self-supervised encoder, by about 5 to 7 points in one study; the reasoning benchmark also moves, by 3 or more points for a stronger contrastive encoder, so the encoder matters for both kinds.
  • (level 3) Design the smallest ablation that settles this for your model. Expected: hold the data, the encoder, the token count, the resolution schedule and the training schedule fixed, swap the language model size in one run and the connector in another, and compare both with the baseline on the same eval.

Where it is taught. The open-weight recipes lesson decomposes the variance across the five design axes, and the vision-language models lesson lays out the pattern the axes belong to.

Defend a designDesign a vision-language model for an on-device mobile assistant that reads the phone screen and taps, types and scrolls. Keep the language model at about 2B parameters. Choose the encoder, the bridge, the resolution plan and the data mix, justify each choice with the ablation evidence, and name the capability you would guard against over-promising.

Level 4 of 4 · about 20 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Encoder: the stronger contrastive encoder at native resolution, with a self-supervised encoder's features concatenated for a dense spatial signal, because the encoder explains about 20 percent of the variance and self-supervised features lead vision-centric tests. Bridge: a two-layer MLP, since the connector explains about 5 percent, with features from several encoder depths stacked so that fine position reaches the language model. Resolution: a ramp from low to high, with dynamic resolution up to about 1280 pixels on the long side, because screens are text-dense and reading gains most from pixels. Data: dense human descriptions of screens plus grounded action traces, not distilled captions; and guard the promise on spatial relations, where general models score 50 to 60 percent and the agent must hit the right element every time.

Constraints first.

A 2B language model sets a low reasoning ceiling: a larger language model adds points on reasoning benchmarks, and this design cannot grow it. So every other axis must carry more, and the task must be framed as perception plus short actions, not long reasoning.

Encoder.

Choice Why
The strong contrastive encoder at native resolution adds 3 or more points over the common one in the ablations; native resolution keeps screen text legible
Concatenated self-supervised features lead vision-centric benchmarks by 5 to 7 points over the contrastive one; UI grounding is vision-centric
Features from several encoder depths deep layers carry semantics and shallow layers carry fine position; stacking both tightens grounding, which an agent that taps needs

Bridge.

A two-layer MLP. The connector explains about 5 percent of the variance at a fixed token count, and the MLP is the simplest to retrain when the encoder or the language model changes. The token count is the knob that matters: screens are documents, so budget toward the upper end of the 576 to 1024 sweet spot, and above it for dense screens.

Resolution schedule.

Start low and ramp. Flat low-resolution training plateaus; a ramp trains faster and ends higher. Dynamic resolution with a minimum of 256 and a maximum near 1280 pixels on the long side handles the range of screen densities, and the document-reading gains of 3 to 5 points from splitting apply to screens.

Data mix.

  • Dense human descriptions of screens: what is on the screen, where, and what is interactive. Dense human captions beat distillation across the board in the reference comparison.
  • Grounded action traces: screenshot, instruction, the element and the action, with coordinates. The visual-agent benchmark that frontier models lead has this shape.
  • Task-specific instruction data for the app families you ship to.
  • Synthetic data only where a human description anchors it, filtered for grounding.

The capability to guard.

Spatial relations. General models score 50 to 60 percent on above, below, left, right and counting, and an agent that taps the wrong element fails the task. Build the eval from real screens with the exact element-selection questions, measure it before any promise, and keep a verification step in the agent loop: read the screen back after the action and check the state.

Serving on the device.

A 2B language model and a strong encoder at a high resolution still need the visual token budget counted per screen; measure the latency per step at your resolution before you set the schedule.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Use a query-based bridge to save tokens on the device." It caps the tokens at a count too low for dense screen text, and the ablations put 64 tokens as too few for reading; spend tokens here and save elsewhere.
  • "Generate the screen descriptions with a strong proprietary model." Distilled descriptions inherit hallucinations, and an agent that taps a hallucinated element fails; dense human descriptions won the reference comparison.
  • "A 2B model with a great encoder matches a 70B agent." The language model sets the reasoning ceiling; frame the task as perception plus short actions and verify each step, instead of promising long reasoning.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) Why stack shallow and deep encoder layers for an agent and not for a captioner? Expected: a captioner needs semantics; an agent needs where exactly, which the shallow layers carry and the last layer blurs.
  • (level 4) The language model is fixed at 2B. Which axis do you lean on hardest, and what is its limit? Expected: resolution and token count, because screens are text-dense; the limit is the latency per step on the device, which the token count sets.
  • (level 4) How do you make the agent robust to being wrong about an element? Expected: a read-back after each action that compares the new screen with the expected state, and a stop when they do not match; the model's own confidence is not a safe signal.
  • (level 4) What does the ablation evidence not tell you about this design? Expected: it comes from general benchmarks on datacenter GPUs; the device latency, the UI domain and the action format need your own ablation, one axis per run.

Weigh a trade-offYou lead a small team training an open vision-language model. The first run lands well below the open-weight models of its size on a general benchmark and on a document-reading benchmark. You can afford three more full runs. Which three changes do you make, in what order, and what do you expect each to move?

Level 3 of 4 · about 15 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Order the runs by the variance each axis explains. First, the resolution schedule and the token count: ramp from a low resolution to a high one and add image splitting for the document benchmark, where moving from 448 to 980 pixels added 3 to 5 points in the ablations. Second, the encoder: swap the common contrastive encoder for the stronger one, which added 3 or more points on the general benchmark, and concatenate a self-supervised encoder if grounding matters. Third, the data: replace distilled synthetic captions with dense human-written ones, which beat the distilled set on every benchmark in the reference comparison; leave the connector alone, because at a fixed token count it moves scores by about a point.

The evidence behind the order.

Axis What the ablations found Expected move
Visual tokens and resolution about 60 percent of the variance; 384 to 448 adds 1 to 2 points; 448 to 980 with splitting adds 3 to 5 on reading tasks the document benchmark
Encoder about 20 percent; a stronger contrastive encoder adds 3 or more; self-supervised features lead vision-centric tests by 5 to 7 the general benchmark, counting and grounding
Data dense human captions beat distillation on 11 of 11 benchmarks in the reference comparison both benchmarks, grounded detail most
Connector about 5 percent; within 1 point at a fixed token count nothing worth a run
Language model 7B to 13B adds 2 to 4 points both, if the budget allows a larger model

Run 1: resolution and tokens.

Flat low-resolution training plateaus at medium accuracy. Ramp from 224 to 448 or native, then add splitting for reading tasks up to about 1280 pixels on the long side. The token count explains the most variance, and documents need pixels.

Run 2: encoder.

Swap to the stronger contrastive encoder at native resolution. If the benchmark includes counting or spatial questions, concatenate a self-supervised encoder's features, as the vision-centric recipes do. Retrain the projector; the language model keeps its adapters.

Run 3: data.

Replace distilled captions with dense human captions where you can get them, and top up with task-specific instruction data for the document questions. The reference result: a model trained on 712K densely captioned images with no distilled data beat a much larger competitor on every benchmark listed. Caption density beats caption quantity, which beats distillation convenience.

What you do not run.

A connector ablation. Several controlled studies put it within a point at a fixed token count; the token count is the knob, and run 1 already turned it.

How to keep the runs comparable.

One axis per run, the same eval, the same seed where the framework allows it, and a record of the data mix and the resolution schedule for each. A run that changed two things cannot be credited to either.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Start with the connector; it is the cheapest to retrain." Cheap and useless: at a fixed token count it moves scores by about a point in every controlled study.
  • "Generate more captions with a stronger proprietary model." Distilled captions inherit hallucinations; the reference comparison shows dense human captions beating distillation across the board.
  • "Train longer at the same settings." The ablations put the gap in resolution, encoder and data, not in steps; more steps at a flat low resolution plateau.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) Why does the document benchmark respond to resolution and the general one less? Expected: reading needs pixels per character; general questions need semantics that the encoder already carries at a low resolution.
  • (level 3) What does a resolution ramp buy over a flat high resolution? Expected: faster alignment early and a higher end point; the ablations show that flat schedules plateau at medium accuracy.
  • (level 4) You cannot get human captions in time. What is the next best data move? Expected: filter the distilled captions for grounding against the image with a contrastive checker and drop the ungrounded tail; density matters, but ungrounded density teaches hallucination.
  • (level 4) If a fourth run appears, is it the connector or the language model? Expected: the language model, if memory allows a larger one; it lifts reasoning by points, where the connector moves a fraction of one.
