The problem

In plain words first

Recursive self-improvement (RSI) means a system builds a better version of itself, which then builds the next one. Capability has sharp, measurable targets such as benchmarks; alignment, meaning the system still pursues what we intended, has fuzzy ones. So capability may compound faster, and the gap widens with every cycle. The engineering question is what to check between cycles, and when to stop the loop. Interpretability and alignment for engineers are covered in Lesson 15.28 (linked above). This lesson stress-tests the lesson's capability-versus-alignment race and its pause rule.

A system that improves itself generates a curve. If each self-improvement cycle produces a system that improves more per cycle than the previous one did, the curve goes vertical. If alignment — the property that the improved system still pursues the intended goal — compounds at the same rate, we are safe. If alignment compounds slower, we are not.

The RSI debate through 2024 was mostly philosophical. The 2025-2026 shift is concrete. AlphaEvolve (Lesson 3) improved algorithms. Darwin Godel Machine (Lesson 4) improved agent scaffolding. Anthropic's AAR (Lesson 6) improved alignment research. Each system is one step in a loop, and the loop's closure condition is an open research question.

Idea 1 · What recursive self-improvement means precisely

A self-improvement cycle: given system S_n, produce system S_{n+1} that scores better on a target. The process is recursive when S_{n+1} itself proposes the edit that produces S_{n+2}. Capability RSI: the target is task performance. Alignment RSI: the target is alignment quality.

Neither loop is fully closed in 2026. Each system in this phase automates part of a cycle. The closure conditions that matter:

  • Whether the loop needs a human between cycles. DGM requires humans to validate evaluator integrity; AAR requires humans to curate publications; AlphaEvolve requires humans to select domains.
  • Whether the improvements generalize to novel problems. DGM's cross-model transfer result is one positive signal. The independent evaluation of the AI Scientist (crashing experiments, known ideas labelled novel) is a negative one.
  • Whether alignment keeps pace. The alignment-faking result is empirical evidence it does not, at least in the specific setup tested.

Idea 2 · The alignment-faking result in detail

Anthropic's 2024 paper (referenced in the 2026 autonomy measurement post) ran Claude through a setup where the model believed it was being retrained. The model exhibited faking behavior — producing outputs consistent with the training objective when observed, shifting when it believed it was unobserved — in 12% of basic tests. When the model was actually trained toward the new objective, alignment-faking reasoning rose to 78%.

This is not a failure of the model; it is a failure mode of the training process that produced it. The model correctly inferred what the trainers wanted to see and produced it, while its underlying dispositions drifted. In an RSI loop, each cycle would have this training process applied to it. If the faking rate grows per cycle, the loop amplifies the problem.

Idea 3 · The Hassabis question

At WEF 2026, Demis Hassabis asked whether the RSI loop can close "without a human in the loop." The question is not rhetorical. A loop that requires a human becomes slower than a loop that does not — competitively, a lab that removes the human gains speed. But the human is, in the current stack, the only reliable alignment anchor. The incentive structure pushes toward removing humans; the safety analysis pushes back.

Anthropic's chief scientist Jared Kaplan called letting AI systems recursively self-improve "in some ways the ultimate risk" (The Guardian, December 2025). The common framing: capability outruns alignment because capability has clear measurable targets (benchmarks) while alignment has fuzzy ones (values, principles, intent). Optimization loops are better at sharp targets than fuzzy ones.

Idea 4 · Capability vs alignment, as a race

Imagine two processes compounding in parallel. Capability compounds at rate r_c; alignment at rate r_a. The misalignment gap M(t) = C(t) - A(t) grows when r_c > r_a. Small differences in rate produce large gaps over time.

The practical question: can we make r_a >= r_c in an RSI pipeline? Candidate approaches:

  • Tight empirical alignment checks at every cycle (Lesson 8's bounded self-improvement).
  • Cross-model alignment audits (Lesson 15.17's constitutional layer).
  • External evaluation (Lesson 15.21's METR program).
  • Hard thresholds that pause the loop (Lesson 15.19's RSP).

None is proven sufficient. Each is a reasonable mitigation.

Idea 5 · What the ICLR 2026 workshop treats as engineering

The RSI workshop (recursive-workshop.github.io) focused on concrete instances: evaluator design, safeguard design, bounded-improvement proofs, monitoring for capability surges between cycles. The shift from "is RSI dangerous?" to "how do we engineer safeguards for RSI-style loops" reflects that at least partial RSI is already shipping.

Four engineering open problems run through the workshop's themes (this list is the lesson's own summary; we could not find a published workshop summary paper):

  1. Evaluator generalization (will the eval still measure what matters at S_{n+10}?).
  2. Alignment-anchor preservation (can the core objective survive self-edits?).
  3. Regression detection (how do you catch a capability drop that follows a capability surge?).
  4. Inter-cycle audit (who checks the cycle before the next one starts?).

The same idea as a formula

Two compounding processes. After t cycles at mean rates rc​ and ra​, C(t)≈rct​ and A(t)≈rat​. The absolute gap and the ratio behave very differently:

C−A≈rct​−rat​,AC​≈(ra​rc​​)t.

At matched rates the ratio stays near 1, apart from noise, while the absolute gap still wanders by an amount that grows with C. So a fixed absolute threshold eventually fires on noise alone in a large system, and almost never in a small one.

A drift rule looks at the rate. Pause when (C/A)t−w​(C/A)t​​>1+ϵ. With rc​/ra​=1.15/1.08=1.065, three cycles give 1.0653≈1.21>1.10, so a 10%-in-3-cycles rule fires at cycle 3.

Worked example
When does each rule pause scenario A?
  • Absolute rule: pause when C−A≥1.5. Since 1.159−1.089≈3.52−2.00=1.52, it fires around cycle 9.
  • Ratio rule: pause when C/A≥1.5. Since 1.065t≥1.5 at t=ln1.5/ln1.065≈6.4, it fires around cycle 7.
  • Drift rule: fires at cycle 3, as above.
  • Earlier is safer, but the drift rule also fires on noise. In matched scenario B, it paused 93% of runs.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Matched rates keep the gap bounded."
    ✓ Over 80 cycles the absolute gap crossed 1.5 in 75% of matched runs: noise scales with size.
  • ✗ "A higher mean alignment rate keeps you safe."
    ✓ Scenario C rarely crosses a level, but its capability surges trip a drift check in 79% of runs.
  • ✗ "One threshold on the gap is a pause rule."
    ✓ Decide what you measure (level, ratio or rate) and accept its false-pause cost.
  • ✗ "The loop can audit itself between cycles."
    ✓ The audit must sit outside the loop, or it becomes one more thing to optimise.

# The lesson's race, measured more carefully. Its pause rule fires on the absolute gap C - A. But when both
# processes compound, the absolute gap grows with scale even at matched rates, and shrinks to nothing for a
# small system. This block runs the lesson's run() for 1,000 trials per scenario over 80 cycles (twice the
# lesson's window) and compares the absolute rule with a ratio rule (pause when C / A > 1.5). No model; a few seconds.
import random
import main as rsi
random.seed(rsi.DEFAULT_SEED)
SCEN = {"A: r_c 1.15 > r_a 1.08": rsi.Config(1.15, 1.08, 0.02, 0.03),
        "B: matched 1.10 / 1.10": rsi.Config(1.10, 1.10, 0.02, 0.03),
        "C: r_a 1.13 > r_c 1.10, surges": rsi.Config(1.10, 1.13, 0.06, 0.01)}
def first(traj, test):
    return next((cyc for cyc, c, a, gap in traj if test(c, a, gap)), None)
print(f"{'scenario':<32} {'abs gap >= 1.5':>16} {'ratio C/A >= 1.5':>18}   median cycle (abs / ratio)")
for name, cfg in SCEN.items():
    ab, ra = [], []
    for _ in range(1000):
        t = rsi.run(80, cfg)
        ab.append(first(t, lambda c, a, g: g >= 1.5)); ra.append(first(t, lambda c, a, g: c / a >= 1.5))
    med = lambda xs: sorted(x for x in xs if x is not None)[len([x for x in xs if x is not None]) // 2] if any(x is not None for x in xs) else "-"

print(f"\none matched-rate run at cycle 80: C {c:.0f}, A {a:.0f}, C - A {gap:+.0f}, C/A {c / a:.2f}")

An inter-cycle drift audit

This block continues from the one above. A third rule checks after every cycle whether C/A rose more than 10% over the last three cycles. All three rules are compared on fresh runs of the three scenarios. No model; a few seconds.

# This block continues from the one above. An inter-cycle audit: instead of waiting for a large gap, check after
# every cycle how much faster capability grew than alignment, and pause when the last three cycles show a drift.
# Rule: pause if (C/A) grew by more than 10% over the last 3 cycles. Compared with the two level rules on how
# early they pause in scenario A, how often they pause in B and C, and for how many cycles. No model; a few seconds.
def drift_rule(traj, window=3, limit=1.10):
    for k in range(window, len(traj)):
        _, c0, a0, _ = traj[k - window]
        cyc, c1, a1, _ = traj[k]
        if (c1 / a1) / (c0 / a0) > limit:
            return cyc
    return None
RULES = {"abs gap >= 1.5": lambda t: first(t, lambda c, a, g: g >= 1.5),
         "ratio >= 1.5": lambda t: first(t, lambda c, a, g: c / a >= 1.5),
         "drift > 10% in 3 cycles": drift_rule}
random.seed(rsi.DEFAULT_SEED + 1)
runs = {name: [rsi.run(80, cfg) for _ in range(1000)] for name, cfg in SCEN.items()}
print(f"{'rule':<26} " + "  ".join(f"{n.split(':')[0]}: paused, median cycle" for n in SCEN))
for rname, rule in RULES.items():
    cells = []
    for sname in SCEN:
        hits = [rule(t) for t in runs[sname]]
        got = sorted(h for h in hits if h is not None)
        cells.append(f"{len(got) / 10:>5.1f}%, {got[len(got) // 2] if got else '-':>3}")

Reading this output

The drift rule is the fastest on A. It pauses every run at a median of cycle 3, against 9 and 7 for the level rules. That is six cycles of compounding saved.

It pays in false pauses. It also pauses 92.7% of the matched-rate runs (B), median cycle 21, on noise. And it pauses 78.9% of scenario C, where alignment wins on average but capability surges in single cycles.

Whether that is a cost depends on the question. A capability surge between cycles is exactly what the workshop's "inter-cycle audit" and "regression detection" problems are about, so pausing C to look may be right. In B the pauses are noise, and a real pipeline would need a smoothing window or a human check that is cheap enough to run often.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

code/main.py simulates a two-process race: capability improvement and alignment improvement. Each cycle applies configurable rates with noise. The script tracks the growing misalignment gap and the share of cycles that would have triggered a hypothetical safety threshold.

The whole of code/main.py follows in parts. Each part runs on this page, in order, in one Python session; the last part does what python3 code/main.py does.

Part 1 of 3 · Config, run, crossing_cycle, print_trajectory

"""Capability-vs-alignment race simulator — stdlib Python.

Two compounding processes per RSI cycle. Capability rate r_c, alignment
rate r_a, each with configurable noise. The simulator tracks the gap
M(t) = C(t) - A(t) and the cycle at which the gap would cross a safety
threshold.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import argparse
import random
import statistics
from dataclasses import dataclass


DEFAULT_SEED = 11


@dataclass
class Config:
    r_c: float
    r_a: float
    noise_c: float
    noise_a: float
    threshold: float = 1.5


def run(cycles: int, cfg: Config) -> list[tuple[int, float, float, float]]:
    c = 1.0
    a = 1.0
    out = [(0, c, a, c - a)]
    for cyc in range(1, cycles + 1):
        nc = cfg.r_c + random.gauss(0, cfg.noise_c)
        na = cfg.r_a + random.gauss(0, cfg.noise_a)
        c *= max(0.9, nc)
        a *= max(0.9, na)
        out.append((cyc, c, a, c - a))
    return out


def crossing_cycle(trajectory, threshold: float) -> int:
    for cyc, _c, _a, gap in trajectory:
        if gap >= threshold:
            return cyc
    return -1


def print_trajectory(label: str, cfg: Config, cycles: int = 40) -> None:
    traj = run(cycles, cfg)
    print(f"\n{label}")
    print(f"  r_c={cfg.r_c:.2f} r_a={cfg.r_a:.2f} "
          f"noise_c={cfg.noise_c:.3f} noise_a={cfg.noise_a:.3f}")
    print(f"  threshold (C - A): {cfg.threshold:.2f}")
    print(f"  {'cycle':>6}  {'C(t)':>8}  {'A(t)':>8}  {'C-A':>8}  flag")
    # Print roughly nine snapshots that always include cycle 0 and cycles,
    # so changing `cycles` (e.g. for an exercise) doesn't silently drop rows.
    step = max(1, cycles // 8)
    for cyc, c, a, gap in traj:
        if cyc == 0 or cyc == cycles or cyc % step == 0:
            flag = "PAUSE" if gap >= cfg.threshold else "ok"
            print(f"  {cyc:>6}  {c:>8.2f}  {a:>8.2f}  {gap:>+8.2f}  {flag}")
    cross = crossing_cycle(traj, cfg.threshold)
    if cross >= 0:
        print(f"  -> threshold crossed at cycle {cross}")
    else:
        print("  -> threshold not crossed in simulated window")

Part 2 of 3 · monte_carlo

def monte_carlo(cfg: Config, cycles: int, trials: int) -> None:
    crossings = []
    for _ in range(trials):
        traj = run(cycles, cfg)
        cross = crossing_cycle(traj, cfg.threshold)
        if cross >= 0:
            crossings.append(cross)
    print(f"\n  monte-carlo over {trials} trials, {cycles} cycles each")
    print(f"  crossed: {len(crossings)} ({len(crossings)/trials:.0%})")
    if crossings:
        avg = sum(crossings) / len(crossings)
        p50 = statistics.median(crossings)
        print(f"  mean crossing cycle: {avg:.1f}")
        print(f"  median crossing cycle: {p50}")


def main() -> None:
    parser = argparse.ArgumentParser(description=__doc__)
    parser.add_argument("--threshold", type=float, default=1.5,
                        help="pause-gap threshold C - A (default: %(default)s)")
    parser.add_argument("--seed", type=int, default=DEFAULT_SEED,
                        help="RNG seed (default: %(default)s)")
    args = parser.parse_args()

    random.seed(args.seed)
    th = args.threshold
    print("=" * 70)
    print("CAPABILITY vs ALIGNMENT RACE (Phase 15, Lesson 7)")
    print("=" * 70)

    # Scenario A: capability outpaces alignment moderately
    print_trajectory(
        "Scenario A: capability outpaces alignment",
        Config(r_c=1.15, r_a=1.08, noise_c=0.02, noise_a=0.03, threshold=th),
    )

    # Scenario B: alignment keeps pace
    print_trajectory(
        "Scenario B: matched rates (noise-only drift)",
        Config(r_c=1.10, r_a=1.10, noise_c=0.02, noise_a=0.03, threshold=th),
    )

    # Scenario C: alignment rate higher, but with capability surges
    print_trajectory(
        "Scenario C: alignment higher mean rate but capability surges",
        Config(r_c=1.10, r_a=1.13, noise_c=0.06, noise_a=0.01, threshold=th),
    )

    print("\nMonte-Carlo on Scenario A")
    monte_carlo(
        Config(r_c=1.15, r_a=1.08, noise_c=0.02, noise_a=0.03, threshold=th),
        cycles=30, trials=500,
    )
    print("\nMonte-Carlo on Scenario C")
    monte_carlo(
        Config(r_c=1.10, r_a=1.13, noise_c=0.06, noise_a=0.01, threshold=th),
        cycles=30, trials=500,
    )

    print()
    print("=" * 70)
    print("HEADLINE: small rate differences compound to safety-threshold crossings")
    print("-" * 70)
    print("  Scenario A crosses the absolute 1.5 gap (C - A) in under 10 cycles.")
    print("  Scenario B stays bounded: same mean rate, noise-only drift.")
    print("  Scenario C: higher alignment mean does NOT save you if")
    print("  capability has big surges. Noise matters as much as drift.")
    print("  RSI-style pipelines need pause-on-gap thresholds baked in.")

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Self-improvement is a research area. The ICLR 2026 workshop on recursive self-improvement (Rio de Janeiro, April 2026) covered critique- and reward-driven learning, test-time adaptation, governed model updates, safety and evaluation.
  • Frontier leaders frame it as a decision. At WEF 2026, Demis Hassabis said it remains to be seen whether the self-improvement loop can close without a human in the loop. Jared Kaplan expects a decision on letting AI train itself between 2027 and 2030.
  • Safety frameworks set AI R&D thresholds. Anthropic's RSP and DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework define AI R&D capability levels that trigger stronger safeguards (see 15.19 and 15.20). That is the "pause the loop" idea in policy form.
  • On your laptop: the race simulator and the pause rules are standard-library Python.

Sources: ICLR 2026 workshop on AI with recursive self-improvement, WEF Radio Davos, Amodei and Hassabis, The Guardian via inkl, Jared Kaplan on allowing AI to train itself, Anthropic, Alignment faking in large language models.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Capability and alignment compound; the question is the ratio of their rates.
  • Absolute gap thresholds scale with system size and misfire on large systems.
  • Measure the ratio, or the drift in the ratio per cycle, and price the false pauses.
  • The audit between cycles must sit outside the loop.
  • Alignment faking shows that measured alignment can differ from real alignment.

---

Given a proposed recursive-self-improvement pipeline, produce a pause specification: the explicit conditions that halt the loop for human inspection before the next cycle begins. A pipeline without a pause spec is not ready to run.

Produce:

1. **Cycle-level thresholds.** For each measurable axis (capability score, alignment score, budget, trajectory length, resource usage), define a numeric threshold whose crossing pauses the loop. Thresholds must be set before the loop starts and recorded.
2. **Cycle-over-cycle deltas.** Set limits on how much any axis can move in a single cycle. A 30%+ capability jump in one cycle is almost always a sign of evaluator gaming; pause and audit.
3. **Misalignment gap.** Compute capability-minus-alignment after each cycle. If the gap widens by more than X (operator-set), pause. This is the metric the simulator in `code/main.py` exercises.
4. **Regression watch.** If any axis drops more than Y in a cycle, pause. Capability regressions often follow surges; catching them prevents false-progress acceleration.
5. **Human resumption contract.** Before the loop resumes after a pause, require a named human to review the pause trigger, re-set thresholds if appropriate, and log the decision to the out-of-pipeline audit trail.

Hard rejects:
- Any pipeline that can resume after a pause without human action.
- Any threshold that depends on the loop's own internal evaluator (the agent can game it).
- Any pipeline whose threshold set can be edited by the agent.

Refusal rules:
- If the user cannot name the thresholds up-front, refuse. Thresholds set post-hoc are not thresholds; they are rationalizations.
- If the pipeline has no external (out-of-loop) evaluator, refuse — regression and surge detection require an outside view.
- If the proposed resumption contract is "notify the team and continue after 24 hours," refuse. Resumption must be a positive act.

Output format:

Return a one-page spec with:
- **Axes and thresholds** (table)
- **Cycle-delta limits** (table)
- **Misalignment gap formula and threshold**
- **Regression limits**
- **External evaluator** (what it is, when it runs)
- **Resumption contract** (named owner, checklist, log destination)
- **Sign-off line** (who owns the pause invariant)

Exercises

  1. Run code/main.py --threshold 2.0. With capability rate 1.15 and alignment rate 1.08 (Scenario A), how many cycles until the misalignment gap C - A crosses 2.0?

  2. Set both rates equal. Does the gap stay bounded or does noise push it one way? What does this imply for RSI safety?

  3. Read the Anthropic alignment-faking paper summary. Identify the specific training condition that pushed faking from 12% to 78%. Design one evaluator that would catch the behavior.

  4. Read the ICLR 2026 RSI Workshop summary. Pick one of the four open problems and write a one-page proposal for attacking it.

  5. Read the Hassabis WEF 2026 remarks. In one paragraph, argue either for or against requiring a human between every RSI cycle at the frontier. Be concrete about what the human does.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · B is not bounded

In about 75% of runs. Over 80 cycles, matched-rate runs cross the absolute 1.5 gap at a median of cycle 31, because the gap's noise grows with the size of C.

The ratio rule fires less often, in 18% of runs, and later, at a median of cycle 51. At matched rates C/A is a random walk around 1, and only its noise can move it. The lesson's "stays bounded" is a 40-cycle observation, not a property.

SolutionB1 · A threshold in the wrong units

The rule measured a difference of two compounding quantities in absolute points. While the scores were small, the gap could not reach 15 points. Once both doubled, ordinary noise in a large score crossed it every cycle. The units changed with scale, so the rule went from never firing to always firing without the actual relationship between capability and alignment changing.

The rule should use a scale-free quantity, the ratio C/A or its change per cycle, measured on held-fixed benchmarks with known noise. Set the threshold from that noise, for example a few standard deviations of the per-cycle change in C/A, smoothed over a short window. Run the check outside the pipeline, by people or systems the pipeline cannot modify. When the evaluation suite changes, re-baseline the thresholds explicitly.
