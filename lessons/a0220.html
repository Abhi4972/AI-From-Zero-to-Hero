In plain words first
The idea in one line

A deployed model fails without an error message. It keeps returning scores while the world moves away from its training data, and only monitoring shows the gap.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it means
Data drift "The data changed" A change in the distribution of inputs, predictions, or labels compared with a reference window
Covariate drift "New kinds of users" P(x) changes while P(y \| x) stays the same
Prior drift "More positives than before" P(y) changes while P(x \| y) stays the same, also called label shift
Concept drift "The rules of the game changed" P(y \| x) changes, so the same input now has a different correct answer
Reference window "The baseline data" A fixed sample, often the training data, that every current window is compared with
PSI "The stability score" A symmetric sum over bins of (current share minus reference share) times the log of their ratio
KS statistic "The KS test" The largest gap between two empirical cumulative distribution functions
Cramér's V "Effect size for categories" The chi-square statistic scaled to the range 0 to 1
Label delay "Ground truth comes later" The time between a prediction and the arrival of its true outcome
Alert fatigue "Too many pages" People ignore alerts because most of them were false alarms

The problem

In plain words first

A model in production can go wrong silently: it keeps answering, but the world has moved. Monitoring compares recent data with a reference sample. Input monitors (PSI, KS, chi-square) warn early but only see changes in the inputs. Outcome monitors (accuracy once the true labels arrive) see every kind of change, but late. An alert policy decides which differences are worth waking someone for.

A payment-risk model goes live with 95 percent accuracy. Two months later, a marketing campaign brings in app users with larger baskets. Four months later, fraudsters change their tactics, and payments that used to be safe start to turn into chargebacks.

The model service returns HTTP 200 for every request during all of this. The latency is normal, and the logs show no errors. The first sign of trouble is a finance report about chargebacks, weeks after the damage.

Software monitoring checks whether a service answers. Model monitoring checks whether the answers are still right, and whether the data still looks like the data that trained the model. This lesson builds the statistics, the windows, and the alert policy for that second job. You run them on a synthetic stream with two injected changes and watch which monitor reacts to which change.

Idea 1 · What to monitor

Monitor four layers. The first three give early warnings. Only the fourth measures correctness, and it arrives late.

Layer Signals Available Question it answers
System health p50 and p99 latency, error rate, throughput, memory At once Does the service answer?
Inputs Distribution of each feature, missing-value rate, schema, new categories At once Does live data look like training data?
Predictions Score distribution, positive rate, share of default answers At once Does the model behave as before?
Outcomes Accuracy, precision, calibration, business metric After the label delay Is the model still right?

Input monitoring is the main subject of this lesson, because it works before labels arrive. It has a limit that the demo shows: some changes leave the inputs untouched.

Idea 2 · Three kinds of drift

Write the joint distribution of inputs x and labels y in two ways:

P(x,y)=P(x)⋅P(y∣x)=P(y)⋅P(x∣y)

Each kind of drift changes a different factor:

Drift What changes What stays Payment example Which monitor sees it
Covariate drift P(x) P(y \| x) More app users with larger baskets Input monitors
Prior drift P(y) P(x \| y) The fraud rate doubles during an attack, and fraud looks the same Prediction and outcome monitors
Concept drift P(y \| x) Can stay The same basket now has a different fraud risk Outcome monitors only

Gama et al. call a change in P(y | x) real concept drift. They call a change in P(x) that leaves P(y | x) alone virtual drift. Their survey also sorts drift by how it happens over time: sudden, incremental, gradual, or as a concept that comes back.

Covariate drift does not always hurt. If the model learned P(y | x) well, it stays right for each input. It can still lose accuracy when traffic moves into regions with little training data or close to the decision boundary.

Idea 3 · Delayed labels

The outcome arrives after the prediction. The delay depends on the problem:

Problem When the label arrives
Ad click Seconds
Churn in the next 30 days 30 days
Card fraud When the chargeback arrives, often weeks later
Loan default Months or years

During the delay, you have inputs and predictions but no outcomes. Monitor the inputs and predictions, join the labels when they arrive, and compute outcome metrics on the labeled part only. The demo uses a label delay of 500 events, so the outcome monitor always looks 500 events into the past.

Idea 4 · Reference and current windows

Every drift test compares two samples:

  • Reference window. The training data, or a period of production that you checked by hand. It stays fixed until you retrain.
  • Current window. The most recent events, either the last N events or the last T hours.

A sliding window moves forward by a step smaller than its size, so consecutive windows overlap. A tumbling window moves by its full size. Window size is a trade-off. A small window reacts quickly but scores noise as drift. A large window is stable but reports a change late.

Seasonality breaks naive windows. Monday traffic differs from Saturday traffic in many products. Make the reference cover a full cycle, or compare each window with the same period in an earlier cycle.

Idea 5 · Population Stability Index

PSI comes from credit scoring. Cut the reference values into bins, usually ten bins at the reference deciles, so that each bin holds 10 percent of the reference. Count the share of current values in each bin. Then add up one term per bin:

PSI=bins∑​(current_share−reference_share)⋅ln(reference_sharecurrent_share​)

A bin with a share of zero makes the log infinite, so clip every share to a small value such as 0.0001. PSI is symmetric. It equals the KL divergence from current to reference plus the KL divergence from reference to current.

Practitioners read PSI with fixed bands: below 0.1 is stable, 0.1 to 0.25 is a moderate shift, and above 0.25 is a significant shift. Treat these bands as a convention, not a test. PSI grows with the number of bins and shrinks with sample size. In the demo, windows of 500 events with no drift score between 0.01 and 0.04.

Worked example
PSI with four bins

The reference is cut into four bins of 25 percent each. The current window puts 15, 20, 30 and 35 percent of its values into them:

Bin Reference Current (current − reference) × ln(current ÷ reference)
1 0.25 0.15 (−0.10) × ln 0.6 = 0.0511
2 0.25 0.20 (−0.05) × ln 0.8 = 0.0112
3 0.25 0.30 (+0.05) × ln 1.2 = 0.0091
4 0.25 0.35 (+0.10) × ln 1.4 = 0.0336

PSI = 0.105, just inside the "moderate shift" band. Every term is positive: a bin that loses share and a bin that gains share both add to the score.

Idea 6 · Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic

The two-sample KS statistic D is the largest vertical gap between the two empirical cumulative distribution functions:

D=xmax​∣Freference​(x)−Fcurrent​(x)∣

D is 0 for identical samples and 1 for samples that do not overlap. It needs no bins. The p-value comes from the Kolmogorov distribution with the effective sample size n * m / (n + m).

With large samples, the p-value goes to zero for a gap that nobody cares about. A shift of 0.02 standard deviations is "significant" with a million events per window. Use D itself as an effect size, and alert only when both D and the p-value pass their thresholds.

Idea 7 · Chi-square test for categorical features

For a categorical feature, such as the payment channel, build a table with two rows (reference, current) and one column per category. The expected count in each cell comes from the row and column totals:

expected[r][c]chi2​=row_total[r]⋅column_total[c]/grand_total=cells∑​(observed−expected)2/expected​

The test has k - 1 degrees of freedom for k categories. A category that appears only in the current window adds a large term, so the test reacts to new values. Cramér's V is the effect size. For a table with two rows, it equals the square root of chi2 / grand_total.

Worked example
The chi-square table from "Use it"

Reference channel counts are 1000, 700, 300 (app, store, web) and current counts 140, 290, 70; the grand total is 2,500. The expected current count for app is 500 × 1140 ÷ 2500 = 228, but only 140 arrived: that cell alone adds (140 − 228)² ÷ 228 = 34.0. All six cells together give χ² = 96.2 with 2 degrees of freedom, p ≈ 1.3 × 10⁻²¹, and Cramér's V = √(96.2 ÷ 2500) = 0.196: a real shift in the channel mix.

Idea 8 · Alert thresholds and false alarms

Each test has a false-alarm rate alpha when nothing changed. Monitoring runs many tests. With 50 features checked every hour at alpha = 0.05, you expect about 60 false alarms per day when nothing changed. People stop reading alerts long before that.

Four rules keep the alert rate useful:

  1. Correct for multiple tests. The Bonferroni correction divides alpha by the number of tests. The demo uses a family alpha of 0.01 over two tests, so each test uses 0.005.
  2. Require an effect size. Alert only when PSI, D, or Cramér's V also passes a threshold.
  3. Require persistence. Alert only after k consecutive drift windows. The demo uses k = 2.
  4. Separate warnings from alerts. A warning goes to a dashboard. An alert goes to a person.

The demo shows rule 2 at work. One clean window has a chi-square p-value of 0.0009, below the per-test alpha. Its Cramér's V is below 0.1, so the policy logs a warning, not an alert.

Idea 9 · Retraining triggers

There are three common triggers for retraining:

  • Schedule. Retrain every week or month. This is simple, but it retrains when nothing changed and waits when something did.
  • Drift. Retrain when input drift on important features stays above the threshold.
  • Performance. Retrain when an outcome metric drops below the reference value minus a tolerance.

The kind of drift decides what retraining can do:

Drift Useful action
Covariate drift, accuracy stable Often none. Keep watching the outcome metric.
Covariate drift into regions with little data Retrain on recent data that includes those regions
Prior drift Recalibrate the scores or move the decision threshold
Concept drift Collect new labels first. Retraining on old labels teaches the old concept again.

After retraining, test the new model on the most recent labeled data. Roll it out with the shadow and canary stages from the system design lesson. Then reset the reference window to the new training data.

The same idea as a formula

Population stability index. Bin a feature using quantiles of the reference window; with reference shares ei​ and current shares ai​,

PSI=i∑​(ai​−ei​)lnei​ai​​

(a symmetrised KL divergence; add a small floor to empty bins). The usual reading: below 0.1 stable, 0.1 to 0.25 moderate shift, above 0.25 major shift.

Kolmogorov–Smirnov. D=supx​∣Fref​(x)−Fcur​(x)∣, the largest gap between the two cumulative distributions. At level α=0.05 the shift is significant if

D>1.358nmn+m​​

for samples of sizes n and m. With 100,000 rows in each window, the critical value is 0.006: any tiny shift is "significant", so judge the size of D, not just the test.

Categorical features. χ2=∑k​(Ok​−Ek​)2/Ek​ over categories, with Ek​ the counts the reference shares predict.

Alert fatigue. Testing m features every day at level α raises a false alarm on some feature with probability 1−(1−α)m per day: with 50 features at 0.05, about 92%. Use stricter thresholds, require persistence over several windows, or alert on model metrics first.

Step 1 · Quantile bins and PSI

The code in code/main.py uses only the standard library.

The bin edges are the reference deciles. bisect_right finds the bin of each value.

def quantile_edges(reference: list[float], bins: int = 10) -> list[float]:
    ordered = sorted(reference)
    n = len(ordered)
    edges = [ordered[min(n - 1, (n * i) // bins)] for i in range(1, bins)]
    return sorted(set(edges))


def psi_from_fractions(expected: list[float], actual: list[float], eps: float = 1e-4) -> float:
    total = 0.0
    for e, a in zip(expected, actual):
        e, a = max(e, eps), max(a, eps)
        total += (a - e) * math.log(a / e)
    return total

Step 2 · The KS statistic and its p-value

Walk both sorted samples together. At each distinct value, move past every copy of it in both samples, then compare the two cumulative shares. This handles ties correctly.

def ks_statistic(sample_a: list[float], sample_b: list[float]) -> float:
    a, b = sorted(sample_a), sorted(sample_b)
    n, m = len(a), len(b)
    i = j = 0
    d = 0.0
    while i < n and j < m:
        x = min(a[i], b[j])
        while i < n and a[i] <= x:
            i += 1
        while j < m and b[j] <= x:
            j += 1
        d = max(d, abs(i / n - j / m))
    return d

The p-value uses the series for the Kolmogorov distribution, 2 * sum((-1)^(k-1) * exp(-2 k^2 lambda^2)), with lambda = sqrt(n * m / (n + m)) * D.

Step 3 · The chi-square test

chi2_sf computes the chi-square survival function through the regularized upper incomplete gamma function. It uses a series for small arguments and a continued fraction for large ones. chi_square_test builds the two-row table, computes the statistic, and returns the p-value and Cramér's V.

def chi_square_test(reference_counts: dict[str, int], current_counts: dict[str, int]) -> ChiSquareResult:
    categories = [c for c in sorted(set(reference_counts) | set(current_counts))
                  if reference_counts.get(c, 0) + current_counts.get(c, 0) > 0]
    rows = [[reference_counts.get(c, 0) for c in categories], [current_counts.get(c, 0) for c in categories]]
    row_totals = [sum(r) for r in rows]
    grand = sum(row_totals)
    if len(categories) < 2 or 0 in row_totals:
        return ChiSquareResult(0.0, 0, 1.0, 0.0)
    statistic = 0.0
    for col, _ in enumerate(categories):
        col_total = rows[0][col] + rows[1][col]
        for r in range(2):
            expected = row_totals[r] * col_total / grand
            statistic += (rows[r][col] - expected) ** 2 / expected
    dof = len(categories) - 1
    return ChiSquareResult(statistic, dof, chi2_sf(statistic, dof), math.sqrt(statistic / grand))

Step 5 · The alert policy

The policy turns each metric set into a level and counts consecutive drift windows per signal:

def level(self, metrics: dict[str, float]) -> str:
    if "psi" in metrics:
        if metrics["psi"] >= self.psi_drift or (metrics["ks"] >= self.ks_drift and metrics["ks_p"] < self.alpha):
            return "drift"
        return "warn" if metrics["psi"] >= self.psi_warn else "ok"
    if metrics["p"] < self.alpha and metrics["v"] >= self.min_cramers_v:
        return "drift"
    return "warn" if metrics["p"] < self.alpha else "ok"

update returns ALERT on the window that completes the streak, and resolved on the first clean window after an alert.

Reading this output

The first two lines are scipy's KS test and ours on the same samples: the statistic D = 0.2975 matches exactly, and the p-values (both around 10⁻³¹) differ a little because scipy uses its exact method at this size. The last line is the channel table from the worked example: χ² = 96.16 with 2 degrees of freedom, as our chi_square_test computes.

We checked ks_statistic and chi_square_test against scipy 1.18.1 on random samples. The KS statistic, the chi-square statistic, and the chi-square p-value matched to floating-point precision. Over 200 random pairs of samples, the KS p-values differed from scipy's by up to 18 percent of scipy's value. The largest gaps were at very small p-values.

The gap comes from the method. Our code uses the limiting Kolmogorov distribution. By default, ks_2samp uses an exact method when both samples have at most 10,000 values. Its asymptotic mode uses a finite-sample distribution with the rounded effective sample size. With two categories, chi2_contingency applies Yates' continuity correction by default, so the statistics differ there.

Monitoring libraries. Several open-source libraries package these tests with reports and dashboards:

  • Evidently picks a drift test per column. For a reference with 1000 rows or fewer, it uses the KS test for numeric columns and the chi-square test for categorical columns. For larger data, it uses the Wasserstein distance and the Jensen-Shannon divergence with a threshold of 0.1. PSI is one of the other methods that you can select.
  • NannyML estimates model performance before labels arrive. Its Confidence-based Performance Estimation (CBPE) uses the model's predicted probabilities, which must be well calibrated. The NannyML documentation states that CBPE handles covariate shift but does not cope with concept drift. The demo stream shows why: only labels revealed the concept drift.
  • TensorFlow Data Validation compares consecutive spans of data. It uses the L-infinity distance for categorical features and an approximate Jensen-Shannon divergence for numeric features. It also compares training data with serving data, which the feature store lesson covers.

The from-scratch code shows what every one of these tools computes. The tools add storage, scheduling, dashboards, and many more tests.

What to keep from this lesson
  • Monitor four layers: system, inputs, predictions, outcomes. Only outcomes measure correctness, and they arrive late.
  • Covariate drift changes P(x), prior drift P(y), concept drift P(y∣x); only outcome monitors see pure concept drift.
  • PSI and KS D measure how far a numeric feature moved; chi-square and Cramér's V do the same for categories.
  • Small windows react fast and raise false alarms; large windows are calm and late.
  • Alert on effect size plus significance, correct for many tests, and require persistence.
  • Concept drift needs new labels before retraining.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · PSI depends on window size

With no drift at all, PSI averages about 0.098 for windows of 100 (one window in twenty reaches 0.18), 0.023 for windows of 500 and 0.006 for windows of 5,000. Small windows put sampling noise into every bin, and PSI adds it all up as "shift". The 0.1 and 0.25 bands only make sense for a fixed window size; calibrate yours by computing PSI on clean windows first.

SolutionB1 · Fast and jumpy

The windows of 100 react sooner: the amount alert comes at 3150 instead of 3500, and the accuracy alert at 7100 instead of 7500. But the clean period before 3000 now shows amount warnings in 7 of 19 windows, the channel alert switches on and off three times during the drift, and accuracy alerts fire at 4200, 5350 and 5500, when the labelling rule had not changed. Each of those would have paged someone for nothing. Choose the window from the delay you can afford and the false-alarm rate you can tolerate, measured on clean data.

# Drift Monitoring

Use this skill in two situations. Use Part A when a model is about to launch and needs monitors. Use Part B when a drift alert fires.

## Part A: Plan the monitors

### Step 1: List the signals per layer

| Layer | Minimum signals |
|---|---|
| System health | p99 latency, error rate, requests per minute |
| Inputs | One drift test per important feature, missing-value rate, new categories |
| Predictions | Score distribution (PSI on score deciles), positive-prediction rate |
| Outcomes | The offline metric from the design, computed on labeled events only |

### Step 2: Pick the test for each input

- Numeric feature: PSI on reference deciles, plus the KS statistic `D`
- Categorical feature: chi-square test on a two-row table, plus Cramér's V
- Feature with five or fewer distinct values: treat it as categorical

### Step 3: Set the windows

- Reference: the training data, or a production period that someone checked
- Current: a window large enough that clean windows stay below the warning level. Run the tests on old clean data to measure this.
- Cover a full weekly cycle in the reference when traffic changes by weekday

### Step 4: Set the alert policy

Write these numbers down before launch:

```text
family alpha          = 0.01
per-test alpha        = family alpha / number of tests
PSI warn / drift      = 0.10 / 0.25
KS D for drift        = 0.10 together with p < per-test alpha
Cramér's V for drift  = 0.10 together with p < per-test alpha
persistence           = 2 consecutive drift windows before an alert
outcome alert         = metric below reference metric - tolerance, for 2 windows
```

Send warnings to a dashboard. Send alerts to a person.

### Step 5: Record the label delay

State how long labels take to arrive and what the team watches until they do. If the delay is longer than a week, add a performance estimate that uses calibrated probabilities. Such an estimate handles covariate drift but cannot detect concept drift.

## Part B: Triage an alert

### Step 1: Rule out a pipeline defect

Check these first, because they look like drift:

- A schema change, a renamed column, or a unit change
- A spike in missing values or in a default value
- A new category that comes from a code change, not from users
- A broken join that fills features with nulls

### Step 2: Name the kind of drift

| Evidence | Likely drift | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Inputs moved, outcome metric stable | Covariate drift | Keep watching. Retrain only if traffic moved into regions with little data. |
| Positive rate moved, inputs per class look the same | Prior drift | Recalibrate scores or move the decision threshold |
| Inputs stable, outcome metric dropped | Concept drift | Collect new labels, then retrain on recent labeled data |
| Inputs moved and outcome metric dropped | Covariate drift with damage, or both kinds | Retrain on recent labeled data and check the new model on the newest period |

### Step 3: Check the size of the effect

A small p-value with a large window says little. Report PSI, `D`, or Cramér's V, and the change in the outcome metric. Rank the drifted features by their importance to the model.

### Step 4: Write the triage note

```text
Alert: <signal, window end, value, threshold>
Pipeline check: <defect found or none>
Drift kind: covariate | prior | concept | unclear
Impact: <outcome metric change, or "labels pending until <date>">
Action: wait | recalibrate | collect labels | retrain | roll back
Owner and review date: <name, date>
```

After any retraining, reset the reference window to the new training data.

Interview questions

Questions from real AI engineering interviews that this lesson prepares you for. Answer out loud first, then open the worked answer.

Debug a failureA payment model's accuracy, measured as labels arrive, fell from 0.946 to between 0.76 and 0.79. The feature monitors and the score distribution stayed quiet the whole time. The team proposes retraining on the last quarter of labeled data tonight. What happened, why did the monitors miss it, and is the proposal right?

Level 3 of 4 · about 12 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. The relation between inputs and labels changed while the inputs themselves did not, which is concept drift. Input and prediction monitors compare distributions of features and scores, so a change in the conditional label distribution is invisible to them, and only the outcome monitor can see it, after the label delay. Retraining tonight on the last quarter is the wrong move: most of those labels still describe the old relation, so the model relearns it; collect labels from after the change first, then retrain on them.

Three kinds of drift.

Drift What changes Which monitor sees it
Covariate The input distribution Input monitors
Prior The label rate Prediction and outcome monitors
Concept The label given the input Outcome monitors only

Covariate drift moves features and often leaves accuracy alone. Prior drift moves the positive rate. Concept drift changes which answer is right for the same input, so nothing upstream of the label moves.

What the demo shows.

The lesson's stream injects both kinds. During the covariate segment the PSI and the KS statistic alert on the amount feature while accuracy stays between 0.89 and 0.95. During the concept segment the inputs are identical to the reference, both input monitors stay at their clean values, and accuracy falls from 0.946 to the 0.76 to 0.79 range. The accuracy alert fires 1000 events after the change, and the label delay of 500 events accounts for half of that lag.

Why the lag.

The outcome monitor always looks one label delay into the past, because it can only score the events whose outcome has arrived. The persistence rule, which requires consecutive drift windows before an alert, adds the rest. Both are deliberate: without them the alert rate is unusable.

Why the retraining proposal is wrong.

Retraining answers covariate drift into regions with little data. Concept drift needs labels from after the change. A quarter of labels where the change happened late in the window teaches mostly the old concept. The right sequence is: confirm the change point from the outcome monitor, gather enough labels past it, retrain on those, test on the most recent labeled slice, roll out through shadow and canary, and reset the reference window to the new training data.

What to do tonight.

Route more cases to review if the business impact is large, as a temporary measure while labels from after the change arrive. A threshold move is the lesson's action for prior drift. For concept drift it helps only when the boundary moved on the same score, and it cannot restore a risk order that changed. Keep the model; replace it only with labels that describe the new relation.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The feature monitors are broken; nothing changed in the inputs." They worked as designed; the inputs did not change, the labels did.
  • "Retrain on everything so the model sees the new pattern." The old labels outnumber the new ones and the model relearns the old relation.
  • "Shorten the windows to catch it faster." Smaller windows add noise and false alarms; the lag here comes from the label delay, which windows cannot remove.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) Which signal could you add that reacts before the labels? Expected: none for pure concept drift; a proxy such as a review queue outcome or a faster partial label is the only earlier signal.
  • (level 3) How does a performance estimator that uses predicted probabilities behave here? Expected: it assumes the calibration still holds, so it misses concept drift; the lesson notes that such estimators handle covariate shift and not concept drift.
  • (level 4) How do you choose how many post-change labels are enough? Expected: enough to test the retrained model on a recent held-out slice with a tolerance you set in advance; the test on recent labels is the gate, not a count.
  • (level 4) After retraining, the input monitors start to alert on the clean reference. Why? Expected: the reference window was not reset to the new training data, so it compares live traffic with an old distribution.

Defend a designA payment risk model scores every transaction. Labels arrive weeks later as chargebacks. Design the monitoring: what you watch, how you compare windows, which statistics you compute, and how you keep the alert rate low enough that people read the alerts.

Level 2 of 4 · about 15 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Monitor four layers: system health, input distributions, prediction distributions, and outcomes once labels arrive. Compare a fixed reference window with sliding current windows, with the Population Stability Index and the two-sample KS statistic for numeric features and a chi-square test with Cramér's V for categorical ones. Alert only when a corrected p-value and an effect size both pass and the condition persists for consecutive windows, and send warnings to a dashboard rather than to a person.

Four layers.

Layer Signals Available Question
System health p50 and p99 latency, error rate, throughput At once Does the service answer?
Inputs Feature distributions, missing rates, schema, new categories At once Does live data look like training data?
Predictions Score distribution, positive rate, share of default answers At once Does the model behave as before?
Outcomes Accuracy, precision, calibration, business metric After the label delay Is the model still right?

Only the last layer measures correctness and it arrives late, so the first three are the early warning.

Windows.

The reference is the training data or a hand-checked production period, fixed until retraining. The current window is the last N events or the last T hours, sliding by a step smaller than its size. A small window reacts quickly and scores noise as drift; a large window is stable but late. Seasonality breaks naive windows, so the reference covers a full cycle or each window is compared with the same period of an earlier cycle. The lesson's demo uses windows of 500 events that move by 250, with labels 500 events late.

Statistics.

  • PSI over ten reference-decile bins, with shares clipped to a small value so no bin is empty; conventional bands read below 0.1 as stable and above 0.25 as a significant shift, with the caveat that PSI grows with bins and shrinks with sample size.
  • KS statistic D as the effect size for numeric features, with its p-value from the effective sample size; with large samples the p-value alone flags gaps nobody cares about.
  • Chi-square with Cramér's V for categorical features, which also reacts to a category that appears only in the current window. With few events in a cell the p-value is not reliable, so pool rare categories into one bin before the test.

Alert policy.

With 50 features checked every hour at an alpha of 0.05, about 60 false alarms a day arrive when nothing changed. Four rules keep the rate useful: correct for multiple tests, so a family alpha of 0.01 over two tests gives 0.005 each; require an effect size; require persistence over consecutive drift windows, two in the demo; and separate dashboard warnings from alerts to a person.

What retraining does.

Covariate drift with stable accuracy often needs no action. Covariate drift into regions with little data calls for retraining on recent data. Prior drift calls for recalibration or a new threshold. Concept drift needs new labels first, because retraining on old labels teaches the old concept again.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Alert on any p-value below 0.05." With many features and hourly checks that is dozens of false alarms a day and people stop reading.
  • "Watch accuracy; it is the only metric that matters." Accuracy arrives after the label delay; input and prediction monitors give the early warning.
  • "Retrain on a schedule and skip monitoring." A schedule retrains when nothing changed and waits when something did.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why fix the reference window instead of sliding it too? Expected: a sliding reference follows the drift and hides it; the reference changes only at retraining.
  • (level 2) A clean window shows a tiny chi-square p-value. Why is it a warning and not an alert? Expected: the effect size is below threshold, so the policy logs a warning; the p-value alone is not enough.
  • (level 3) Labels arrive 500 events late. How does the outcome monitor handle that? Expected: it joins labels as they arrive and computes metrics on the labeled part, always looking that far into the past.
  • (level 3) Which drift stays invisible to this whole design except the last layer? Expected: concept drift, where the inputs do not change and only outcomes reveal the new relation.
