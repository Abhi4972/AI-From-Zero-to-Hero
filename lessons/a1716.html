In plain words first
The idea in one line

A dynamic broker evaluates every request (task type, token length, embedding similarity, confidence) and sends simple queries to a cheap model, escalating complex ones to a frontier model. Also called model cascading. Production case studies show 20-60% cost reduction at iso-quality across US/UK/EU deployments; a 30% routing efficiency improvement on high-volume SaaS turns into six-figure annual savings. The problem

In plain words first

Model routing sends each request to the cheapest model that can answer it well. Most traffic is easy (rephrase this, what time is it in Paris) and a small model handles it at a few per cent of a frontier model's price; the rest needs the big model. There are three patterns. A pre-route classifier looks at the request and picks a model before calling anything. A cascade tries the cheap model first and escalates to the expensive one when the answer looks weak: low confidence, a refusal, hedging. An ensemble runs both and picks the better answer, used only for tests. The risk is quiet: as users change what they ask, a router tuned on last quarter's traffic starts sending hard questions to the small model, and quality drops without an error anywhere.

Your service costs $80k/month on GPT-5. Your analytics show 70% of queries are simple: "what time is it in Paris?" "rephrase this sentence." A Haiku-class model handles those perfectly at 3% of the cost. 30% need GPT-5's reasoning — coding, math, multi-step planning.

If you route the 70% to cheap and 30% to expensive, your bill drops ~65% at the same product quality. This is routing. The trick is building the broker without regressing quality.

Idea 1 · Four routing signals

  1. Task classification: simple/complex/codegen/math/chat. Can be a rules-based classifier, a small LLM (Haiku-class at $0.25/M), or embedding similarity to labeled buckets. Output: route = cheap / balanced / frontier.

  2. Prompt length: prompts >4K tokens often need frontier for coherence. Prompts <500 tokens usually don't.

  3. Embedding similarity to known-hard set: if the query is close (cosine > 0.88) to a known-hard bucket, escalate to frontier directly.

  4. Self-confidence from first-pass: send to cheap; if model's log-probs show low confidence OR it refuses OR outputs hedging language, retry on frontier. Adds P95 latency on ~10% of traffic but saves 50%+ on the other 90%.

Idea 2 · Three patterns

Pre-route (classifier up front): ~5-10ms latency added; fastest overall.

Cascade (cheap-first, escalate on low confidence): ~1.2x median latency (cheap run plus verify), ~2x on escalated. Best quality floor.

Ensemble route (run cheap and frontier in parallel for a sample, reward-model pick): highest quality, highest cost; use only for critical A/B.

Idea 3 · Implementation

AI gateways (Phase 17 · 19) expose routing. LiteLLM has router config with fallback and cost-routing. Portkey has guards + routing. Kong AI Gateway has plugin-based routing. OpenRouter's model marketplace exposes a recommendation API.

Open-source: RouteLLM (LMSYS), Prompt Mule. Commercial: Not Diamond.

Idea 4 · The 2026 price curve

Model class Late 2022 2026 Change
GPT-4-level quality ~$20/M ~$0.40/M 50x cheaper
Frontier (GPT-5, Claude 4) — ~$3-10/M new tier

Most of the improvement is serving efficiency — the core lessons in Phase 17 · 04-09 turned into provider-side cost drops. Routing lets you capture those gains at the app layer instead of waiting for all your users to migrate to the cheap tier.

Idea 5 · Drift is the real risk

Your route sends 40% to the cheap model. Over six months, the task distribution shifts (users get more sophisticated, ask longer questions). The router doesn't notice because its classifier was trained on Q1 data. Quality drops silently. Nobody complains loud enough. You find out in a competitor benchmark you lost.

Gate routes by online quality metrics:

  • User thumbs-up / thumbs-down per route.
  • Automated LLM-judge on a held-out sample (5%) per route.
  • Escalation rate: if cascade is kicking up-route >30%, the cheap model is being over-routed.
  • Refusal rate per route.

Idea 6 · Numbers you should remember

  • 2026 routing savings at iso-quality: 20-60% case studies.
  • LLM price drop at a fixed capability level: ~10x per year (a16z LLMflation estimate); about 50x total at the GPT-4 tier from 2022 to 2026.
  • GPT-4-level 2022 vs 2026: ~$20/M → ~$0.40/M.
  • Cascade latency impact: ~1.2x median, ~2x escalated (~10% of traffic).

The same idea as a formula

Blended cost of routing. If a fraction s of traffic goes to the cheap model at cost c per request and the rest to the frontier model at cost F, and a cascade escalates a fraction e of the cheap requests (paying for both calls),

cost per request=sc+seF+(1−s)F

Pre-routing has e=0 but depends on the classifier; a cascade pays c on every escalated request as well.

Worked example
Routing 70% of an $80,000-a-month bill
  1. All on the frontier model: $80,000.
  2. Pre-route 70% to a model costing 3% as much: 0.7 × 0.03 × 80,000 + 0.3 × 80,000 = $1,680 + $24,000 = $25,680, a 68% saving.
  3. Cascade instead, with 15% of the cheap requests escalating: add 0.7 × 0.15 × 80,000 = $8,400, so about $34,080, a 57% saving with a better quality floor.
  4. If drift pushes escalation to 40%, the cascade costs $24,000 + $1,680 + $22,400 = $48,080 and saves only 40%: a rising escalation rate is your drift alarm.
Check your mental model

Which statements are right?

  • ✓ A cascade adds latency only on the requests it escalates.
  • ✗ A router trained once keeps working. Task mix drifts; gate routes on online quality, escalation and refusal rates.
  • ✓ Prompt length is a cheap routing signal: very long prompts often need the frontier model.
  • ✗ Pre-routing always beats a cascade on quality. Pre-routing is only as good as its classifier; a cascade checks the actual answer.

Each lost point of classifier accuracy costs about 0.3 points of quality (hard questions now sent cheap) and, oddly, raises cost too, because easy questions get misread as hard and sent to the frontier model. A pre-router needs a well-measured classifier; when you cannot get one, a cascade, which checks the real answer, is the safer default.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

code/main.py simulates pre-route, cascade, and ensemble on a mixed workload. Reports blended cost, quality loss, and escalation rate.

Here is code/main.py, in 4 parts. Each part runs on this page, in order, in one Python session; the last part does what python3 code/main.py does.

Part 1 of 4 · Query, make_workload, cost_of

"""Model routing simulator — stdlib Python.

Three patterns on the same workload:
  NO_ROUTE   : all requests to frontier
  PRE_ROUTE  : classifier up front routes to cheap or frontier
  CASCADE    : cheap first, escalate on low confidence

Reports blended cost, quality loss, escalation rate.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass
import random


CHEAP_INPUT = 0.25
CHEAP_OUTPUT = 1.00
FRONTIER_INPUT = 3.00
FRONTIER_OUTPUT = 15.00


@dataclass
class Query:
    difficulty: str  # 'simple' | 'medium' | 'hard'
    prompt_tokens: int
    output_tokens: int


def make_workload(n: int = 1000, seed: int = 7) -> list[Query]:
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    reqs = []
    for _ in range(n):
        p = rng.random()
        if p < 0.6:
            reqs.append(Query("simple", rng.randint(200, 1000), rng.randint(50, 200)))
        elif p < 0.9:
            reqs.append(Query("medium", rng.randint(800, 3000), rng.randint(100, 400)))
        else:
            reqs.append(Query("hard", rng.randint(2000, 8000), rng.randint(200, 1500)))
    return reqs


def cost_of(route: str, q: Query) -> float:
    if route == "cheap":
        return (q.prompt_tokens / 1e6) * CHEAP_INPUT + (q.output_tokens / 1e6) * CHEAP_OUTPUT
    return (q.prompt_tokens / 1e6) * FRONTIER_INPUT + (q.output_tokens / 1e6) * FRONTIER_OUTPUT

Part 2 of 4 · quality, simulate, report

def quality(route: str, q: Query) -> float:
    """Toy quality score per difficulty on route."""
    if route == "frontier":
        return 1.0
    return {"simple": 0.99, "medium": 0.92, "hard": 0.75}[q.difficulty]


def simulate(pattern: str, reqs: list[Query]) -> dict:
    total_cost = 0.0
    total_q = 0.0
    escalated = 0
    rng = random.Random(11)

    for q in reqs:
        if pattern == "NO_ROUTE":
            total_cost += cost_of("frontier", q)
            total_q += 1.0
        elif pattern == "PRE_ROUTE":
            if q.difficulty == "simple":
                total_cost += cost_of("cheap", q)
                total_q += quality("cheap", q)
            else:
                total_cost += cost_of("frontier", q)
                total_q += 1.0
        elif pattern == "CASCADE":
            total_cost += cost_of("cheap", q)
            confident = (q.difficulty == "simple") or (q.difficulty == "medium" and rng.random() < 0.5)
            if confident:
                total_q += quality("cheap", q)
            else:
                escalated += 1
                total_cost += cost_of("frontier", q)
                total_q += 1.0

    return {
        "pattern": pattern,
        "cost": total_cost,
        "mean_quality": total_q / len(reqs),
        "escalated": escalated,
    }


def report(row: dict, baseline: float) -> None:
    save = (baseline - row["cost"]) / baseline * 100
    print(f"{row['pattern']:12}  cost=${row['cost']:7.2f}  save={save:5.1f}%  "
          f"quality={row['mean_quality']*100:5.1f}%  escalated={row['escalated']:4}")

Part 3 of 4 · main

def main() -> None:
    print("=" * 80)
    print("MODEL ROUTING — three patterns, 1000 requests, mixed difficulty")
    print("=" * 80)
    base = make_workload()
    baseline = simulate("NO_ROUTE", base)["cost"]
    for p in ("NO_ROUTE", "PRE_ROUTE", "CASCADE"):
        report(simulate(p, base), baseline)

    print("\nRead: PRE_ROUTE saves big when the classifier is accurate. CASCADE")
    print("guarantees quality floor but adds latency on escalated requests.")

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Route easy requests to cheap models; most traffic is easy.
  • Signals: task class, prompt length, similarity to known-hard requests, first-pass confidence.
  • Pre-route is fastest; a cascade checks the answer; ensembles are for tests.
  • Watch escalation, refusals and per-route quality; drift is the main risk.

Given workload mix (task classification sample), quality floor, latency tolerance, and current monthly spend, produce a routing plan.

Produce:

1. Pattern. Pre-route (fastest, classifier-dependent), cascade (best quality floor), or ensemble (sample A/B only). Justify with quality tolerance + latency budget.
2. Signals. Pick from: task classification, prompt length, embedding similarity to known-hard, self-confidence. State which combine (usually 2-3) and the composition rule.
3. Cheap/frontier pair. Name the specific models. Example: Claude Haiku 3.5 + GPT-5. Justify with cost curve + capability.
4. Expected savings. Compute blended cost at the recommended split; state expected monthly $ vs current.
5. Online quality gates. Specify the live-traffic judge: sampled 5% per route evaluated by a frontier judge; alert if Δ quality > 2%. Track escalation rate; alert if climbs >10 points in a month.
6. Rollout. Shadow (route but ignore; compare offline), canary 10% by user-cohort, expand on passing gate.

Hard rejects:
- Routing without online quality gates. Refuse — drift is the #1 failure.
- Using only task classification as the signal. Refuse — misses difficulty within tasks.
- Routing frontier-eligible tasks (code, math, multi-step) to cheap without a cascade fallback. Refuse — quality floor will breach.

Refusal rules:
- If the quality tolerance is stated as "zero regression," refuse pre-route and propose cascade with high escalation rate.
- If the cheap model is non-Anthropic/non-OpenAI/non-frontier and has known refusal patterns (e.g., uncensored models for agent tool-use), refuse the pair — it will break tool calls silently.
- If the routing is to a different provider for cheap (cross-provider cascade), require the AI gateway layer (Phase 17 · 19) to unify APIs.

Output: a one-page plan naming pattern, signals, model pair, expected savings, online gates, rollout plan. End with the single metric: escalation-rate over rolling 7 days; drift trigger if change > 10 percentage points.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · When a cascade beats pre-routing

In the visual above, pre-route quality falls from about 98.4% to 92.4% as classifier accuracy drops from 95% to 75%, while a cascade's quality depends on its confidence check rather than a classifier. In code/main.py, with its accurate classifier, pre-routing gives higher quality and the cascade lower cost; once the classifier drops below roughly 90% accuracy, the cascade's quality floor is the better trade.

SolutionB1 · Escalation creeping up

The task mix drifted: users ask longer, harder questions than the data the router was tuned on, so the cheap model fails more often and the cascade pays for both calls. Fixes: re-label recent traffic and retrain the router or threshold; route by prompt length or a hard-query set directly to the frontier model; and alert when escalation passes about 30%.

Questions from real AI engineering interviews that this lesson prepares you for. Answer out loud first, then open the worked answer.

Debug a failureSix months ago a cascading router cut the bill by more than half with no measurable quality loss. Since then the escalation rate has crept from 8 percent to 22 percent, and a sales engineer reports that a competitor's demo now answers better than your product. Diagnose it.

Level 3 of 4 · about 12 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. The traffic drifted and the router did not. Its classifier learned the question mix of six months ago, users now ask longer and harder questions, and the cheap model's first pass fails its own confidence check more often, which is what the rising escalation rate records. Quality fell silently on the requests that were not escalated. Gate every route with online quality metrics, retrain the classifier on current traffic, and treat an escalation rate above about 30 percent as proof that the cheap model is over-routed.

The failure mode the lesson warns about.

A route sends a large share of traffic to a weaker model. Over months the task distribution shifts: users get more sophisticated and ask longer questions. The router does not notice, because its classifier was trained on the earlier quarter. Quality drops by a few percent on reasoning tasks, nobody complains loudly enough, and the team finds out from a competitor benchmark. The lesson's numbers for the pattern: 40 percent routed cheap, a 3 to 5 percent quality drop, unnoticed for a quarter.

Three causes of a rising escalation rate.

  1. Distribution shift. More long prompts and more multi-step questions. The lesson's length signal says prompts above 4K tokens often need the frontier model, and prompts under 500 tokens usually do not. If the mix moved up, more first passes fail.
  2. The cheap model changed. A provider update behind the same alias, or a routine version bump, moves the confidence signal and the refusal behavior.
  3. Stale thresholds. The confidence cutoff and the embedding set of known-hard questions were tuned on old traffic.

Each cause has its own fix: retrain the classifier on recent labeled traffic, pin the model version and log the served model on every call, and refresh the hard set and the cutoff on a new eval.

Gates on live traffic.

The lesson gates routes with live signals, not with the offline set:

  • thumbs up and down per route;
  • an automated judge on a 5 percent held-out sample per route;
  • the escalation rate, where a cascade that kicks up more than 30 percent of requests is over-routing;
  • the refusal rate per route.

What the cascade now costs.

The cascade pattern was designed for about 1.2 times the median latency and about 2 times on the escalated share, with roughly 10 percent escalated. At 22 percent escalated, a fifth of the requests pay for two model calls and the slow path, so the saving shrank while the quality fell.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The cheap model got worse, swap it." Possible, and the first suspect is the router's stale classifier against drifted traffic.
  • "Quality is fine, the eval score did not move." The eval set comes from the old distribution and cannot see the new questions.
  • "Raise the confidence cutoff so that more goes to the frontier model." That buys quality back at a cost without finding the cause. Retrain the router and gate it online.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) Why did the offline eval not catch the loss? Expected: the eval set was built from the old distribution. It scores the model on questions that users no longer ask.
  • (level 3) Which online metric gives the earliest warning? Expected: the escalation rate per route. It rises as the cheap model fails its own confidence check more often, before the thumbs or a judge sample show the loss.
  • (level 4) The provider updated the cheap model behind the same alias. How do you know? Expected: a step change in escalations or judge scores on a date with no deploy on your side. Pin the model version and log the served model on every call.
  • (level 4) Where do you set the alarm on the escalation rate? Expected: near the design target, about 10 percent escalated for the cascade pattern, with the lesson's 30 percent as the hard line for over-routing.
