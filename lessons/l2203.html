Why this came about

Probability began in the 1600s with games of chance and became the mathematics of uncertainty. Machine learninglater · 2.22 adopted it because real data is noisy and predictions are never certain: a good model does not just guess, it says how sure it is. A language model is, at its heart, a probability machine: for every possible next token it outputs a probability (you saw 8.5% for " the" in Lesson 0.15). It is trained by making the probability of the actual next token as high as possible, and judged by how surprised it is by real text. This lesson builds each of those ideas from counting.

In plain words first
Weather forecasts for words

A probability is a number from 0 to 1 saying how likely something is: 0 never, 1 certainly, 0.3 "about three times in ten". A distribution lists the probability of every possible outcome, and the probabilities add up to 1, like a weather forecast that splits 100% between sun, cloud and rain. A language model gives a forecast for the next word. Softmax is the recipe that turns the model's raw scores into such a forecast. And surprise measures how badly a forecast did once you see what actually happened: a forecast of 1% rain followed by a downpour was very surprising.

Step 1 · From counts to probabilities

The simplest way to estimate a probability is to count. Here are the categories of 200 ShopKart support tickets:

import numpy as np

categories = np.array(["delivery", "refund", "payment", "account"])
counts = np.array([90, 60, 30, 20])
probs = counts / counts.sum()             # divide by the total: a distribution
for c, p in zip(categories, probs):
    print(f"{c:<9} {p:.2f}")
print("sum:", probs.sum())
What you should see
delivery  0.45
refund    0.30
payment   0.15
account   0.10
sum: 1.0

Dividing counts by their total is called normalizing, and it always gives numbers between 0 and 1 that add up to 1. This is a categorical distribution: one probability per category. A language model's output is exactly this shape, with one probability per vocabulary token, 50,257 of them for GPT-2.

Step 2 · Sampling: letting chance choose

To sample from a distribution is to pick an outcome at random, with each outcome as likely as its probability says. Picking the next word this way is how language models generate varied text.

rng = np.random.default_rng(seed=1)
print(rng.choice(categories, size=10, p=probs))
for n in [10, 100, 10_000]:
    draws = rng.choice(len(categories), size=n, p=probs)
    freq = np.bincount(draws, minlength=4) / n          # how often each category came up
    print(f"{n:>6} samples: {np.round(freq, 3)}")
What you should see
['refund' 'account' 'delivery' 'account' 'delivery' 'delivery' 'payment'
 'delivery' 'refund' 'delivery']
    10 samples: [0.6 0.2 0.2 0. ]
   100 samples: [0.41 0.3  0.22 0.07]
 10000 samples: [0.444 0.303 0.148 0.105]

With 10 samples the frequencies wobble a lot; with 10,000 they settle close to [0.45, 0.3, 0.15, 0.1]. That settling is the law of large numbers, and it is why one sample says little and evaluation in the Production engineering and AI system design section runs many cases. It is also why the same prompt can give different answers: if a model samples, each run is a fresh draw.

Step 3 · Conditional probability: it depends on what came before

A conditional probability is the probability of something given that something else is known, written P(A | B), read "the probability of A given B". Language models compute exactly this: P(next word | the words so far). Here it is estimated by counting which word follows which in a tiny corpus:

corpus = ("my order is delayed . my order is delivered . my refund is pending . "
          "my order is delivered . the refund is processed .").split()
pairs = list(zip(corpus[:-1], corpus[1:]))               # every (word, next word) pair

def next_word_probs(word):
    followers = [b for a, b in pairs if a == word]
    values, counts = np.unique(followers, return_counts=True)
    return {str(v): round(float(c) / len(followers), 2) for v, c in zip(values, counts)}   # plain Python types print neatly

print("P(next | 'my')    =", next_word_probs("my"))
print("P(next | 'order') =", next_word_probs("order"))
print("P(next | 'is')    =", next_word_probs("is"))
What you should see
P(next | 'my')    = {'order': 0.75, 'refund': 0.25}
P(next | 'order') = {'is': 1.0}
P(next | 'is')    = {'delayed': 0.2, 'delivered': 0.4, 'pending': 0.2, 'processed': 0.2}

After "is", "delivered" has probability 0.4 because it followed "is" in 2 of the 5 times "is" appeared. This counting model is called a bigram model, and Lesson 5.34 builds and samples from one. Its weakness is plain: it only looks at one previous word. "My refund is ..." and "my order is ..." get the same forecast. Large language models condition on thousands of previous tokens, which is what makes their forecasts so much better.

Step 4 · Describing numbers: mean, spread and the bell curve

For numeric data, three numbers summarize a lot. The mean is the average. The variance is the average squared distance from the mean, and the standard deviation (std) is its square root, back in the original units: it says how far a typical value sits from the mean.

delivery_days = np.array([2, 3, 3, 4, 2, 5, 3, 9, 3, 4])
mean = delivery_days.mean()
var = ((delivery_days - mean) ** 2).mean()
print(mean, round(var, 2), round(np.sqrt(var), 2), round(delivery_days.std(), 2))
What you should see
3.8 3.76 1.94 1.94

Many measurements cluster symmetrically around a mean in a bell-shaped pattern called the normal (or Gaussian) distribution, described completely by its mean and standard deviation. About 68% of values fall within one standard deviation of the mean and about 95% within two. It appears all over AI: the starting values of a neural network's weights are drawn from it (Lesson 3.18), and normalization layers rescale values to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 (Lesson 7.19).

samples = rng.normal(loc=0.0, scale=1.0, size=100_000)
print(round(samples.mean(), 3), round(samples.std(), 3))
print(round(np.mean(np.abs(samples) < 1), 3), round(np.mean(np.abs(samples) < 2), 3))
standardized = (delivery_days - delivery_days.mean()) / delivery_days.std()
print(standardized.round(2))                   # mean 0, std 1: the 9-day delivery stands out
What you should see
-0.003 0.999
0.683 0.954
[-0.93 -0.41 -0.41  0.1  -0.93  0.62 -0.41  2.68 -0.41  0.1 ]

The last line standardizes the delivery times: subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation. The result is measured in "standard deviations from typical", and the 9-day delivery stands out at about 2.7. This is the same rescaling normalization layers do inside transformerslater · 7.19.

Step 5 · Softmax: scores into probabilities

A model's last layer produces raw scores, called logits, one per possible answer. They can be any numbers, positive or negative, and they do not add up to 1. Softmax turns them into a distribution in two moves: make every score positive with the exponential function e^x, then normalize.

def softmax(scores, temperature=1.0):
    z = np.array(scores, dtype=float) / temperature
    z = z - z.max()                  # subtract the largest: same result, avoids overflow
    e = np.exp(z)
    return e / e.sum()

words = [" Paris", " the", " a", " France", " Lyon"]
logits = [3.0, 2.2, 1.8, 1.0, 0.2]
print(softmax(logits).round(3), softmax(logits).sum().round(6))
print(softmax(np.array(logits) + 100).round(3))     # adding the same number to every score changes nothing
for t in [0.5, 1.0, 2.0]:
    print(f"temperature {t}:", softmax(logits, t).round(3))
What you should see
[0.514 0.231 0.155 0.07  0.031] 1.0
[0.514 0.231 0.155 0.07  0.031]
temperature 0.5: [0.761 0.154 0.069 0.014 0.003]
temperature 1.0: [0.514 0.231 0.155 0.07  0.031]
temperature 2.0: [0.353 0.237 0.194 0.13  0.087]
Reading this output
  • The highest score gets the highest probability, but not all of it: softmax keeps every option alive.
  • Adding 100 to every score leaves the probabilities unchanged: only the differences between scores matter. The code subtracts the maximum for exactly this reason, so e^x never overflows to infinity.
  • Temperature divides the scores before softmax. Below 1, differences grow and the top choice dominates (0.5 gives " Paris" over 70%); above 1, differences shrink and the distribution flattens. Temperature near 0 approaches always picking the top word; this is the "temperature" setting on every model API (Lesson 11.19).

Step 6 · Log-probabilities and surprise

When a model generates or reads a long text, it assigns a probability to each token. The probability of the whole text is the product of them, and products of many small numbers become too small for a computer to store:

p_each = np.full(1000, 0.2)                 # 1000 tokens, each given probability 0.2
print(np.prod(p_each))                       # underflows to exactly 0.0
print(np.log(p_each).sum().round(2))         # the log of the product: a normal-sized number
print(np.log(0.2) * 1000)
What you should see
0.0
-1609.44
-1609.4379124341003

Logarithms turn products into sums, log(a × b) = log a + log b, so models work with log-probabilities throughout. The negative log-probability of what actually happened is its surprise: near 0 when the model was confident and right, large when it gave the truth a low probability.

Averaging the surprise over every token of a text gives the cross-entropy loss, the number a language model is trained to make as small as possible (Lesson 5.34). Taking exp of it gives perplexity: roughly, "the model was as unsure as if it were choosing uniformly among this many words".

def cross_entropy(prob_of_truth):
    return float(np.mean(-np.log(prob_of_truth)))

confident_right = np.array([0.9, 0.8, 0.95, 0.85])   # probability the model gave to each true next token
unsure = np.array([0.3, 0.2, 0.25, 0.4])
confident_wrong = np.array([0.9, 0.01, 0.95, 0.85])  # one confident mistake
for name, p in [("confident and right", confident_right), ("unsure", unsure), ("one confident mistake", confident_wrong)]:
    ce = cross_entropy(p)
    print(f"{name:<22} loss {ce:.3f}   perplexity {np.exp(ce):.2f}")
What you should see
confident and right    loss 0.136   perplexity 1.15
unsure                 loss 1.279   perplexity 3.59
one confident mistake  loss 1.231   perplexity 3.42

One confident mistake (probability 0.01 for the truth, a surprise of 4.6) costs more than the other three good predictions combined, and drags the average almost down to the level of the "unsure" model. Cross-entropy punishes overconfidence hard, which is why trained models learn to hedge when they are unsure. A perplexity near 1 means "almost certain every time"; uniformly guessing among GPT-2's 50,257 tokens would give a perplexity of 50,257.

The same idea as a formula
  • softmax: pᵢ = e^(zᵢ / T) / Σⱼ e^(zⱼ / T), for scores zᵢ and temperature T
  • cross-entropy over N tokens: L = −(1/N) Σₜ log pₜ, where pₜ is the probability given to the true token t
  • perplexity = e^L
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Softmax picks the highest score."
    ✓ It gives every option a probability; picking happens afterwards (greedy or sampling).
  • ✗ "Temperature makes a model more creative."
    ✓ It only reshapes the probability distribution: flatter (more varied picks) or sharper (more predictable).
  • ✗ "A loss of 0 is achievable and good."
    ✓ It would mean probability 1 for every true token, which no honest model of real text can do; aiming for it on training data leads to overfittinglater · 2.26 (Lesson 2.26).
  • ✗ "P(A | B) is the same as P(B | A)."
    ✓ P(rain | clouds) is not P(clouds | rain). The order matters.

Exercises

Predict before you run

softmax([1, 1, 1, 1]) gives what? And softmax([5, 1]) versus softmax([50, 10]): which is sharper? (Solution P1.)

Make it your own

Add three sentences of your own to corpus in Step 3 (for example about payments) and print next_word_probs for a word you added. How many sentences would you need before the forecasts look reliable? (Solution M1.)

Debugging exercise

A colleague writes np.exp(scores) / np.exp(scores).sum() and gets nan for scores like [1000, 999]. Why, and what is the one-line fix? (Solution D1.)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Equal and scaled scores

Equal scores give a uniform distribution: [0.25, 0.25, 0.25, 0.25]. softmax([5, 1]) is about [0.982, 0.018]; softmax([50, 10]) is about [1.0, 0.0], much sharper, because softmax depends on differences: 4 versus 40. Scaling all scores up is the same as lowering the temperature.

SolutionM1 · More data

Any additions work; the forecasts for words you add will be based on very few examples, so one sentence can swing them from 0 to 1. Counting models need a lot of text to estimate rare continuations; that hunger for data, and the fact that unseen pairs get probability 0, are the problems neural language models solved (Lessons 5.34 and 5.35).

SolutionD1 · Overflow

np.exp(1000) is larger than the biggest number a float can hold, so it becomes inf, and inf / inf is nan. Subtract the maximum first: e = np.exp(scores - scores.max()). Since only differences matter, the probabilities are unchanged and the largest exponent is now e^0 = 1.

Recap
  • Probabilities are between 0 and 1; a distribution's probabilities add up to 1; normalizing counts gives one.
  • Sampling picks outcomes at random by their probabilities; many samples settle near the true frequencies.
  • P(next | previous) is a conditional probability; counting it gives a bigram model.
  • Mean, variance and standard deviation summarize numbers; the normal distribution is the bell curve; standardizing rescales to mean 0, std 1.
  • Softmax turns scores into probabilities; temperature sharpens or flattens them.
  • −log p is surprise; its average is the cross-entropy loss; perplexity is its exponential.
