Training, alignment and model evaluation

JuniorWalk through how a chat model is trained.

Pretraining on trillions of tokens with next-token prediction gives a base model that knows a lot but only continues text. Supervised fine-tuning on curated conversations, with loss on assistant tokens only, teaches the assistant format. Preference tuning (RLHF with a reward model and PPO, or DPO directly on preference pairs) teaches which answers people prefer. Reinforcement learning on verifiable rewards (math, code, tool tasks graded by programs, often with GRPO) builds reasoning and agent skill. Each stage changes behavior more than knowledge, which is why fresh facts still come from retrieval and tools (Lesson 10.1).

MidWhat are scaling laws, and what did Chinchilla change?

Loss falls as a smooth power law in parameters, data and compute, which lets labs predict a large run from small ones. Training compute is about 6 × parameters × tokens. Kaplan et al. (2020) favored very large models; Chinchilla (Hoffmann et al., 2022) showed that for a fixed budget, models were undertrained and the optimum is about 20 tokens per parameter (70B on 1.4T tokens). Today small models are trained far beyond that (Llama 3 8B on 15T+ tokens, nearly 1,900 per parameter) because inference cost over a model's life matters more than training cost.

MidCompare RLHF with PPO and DPO.

RLHF trains a reward model on human preference pairs (Bradley-Terry: P(A > B) = sigmoid(r(A) − r(B))), then optimizes the policy with PPO to maximize reward minus a KL penalty that keeps it near the reference model. It is powerful but complex: four models in memory (policy, reference, reward, value), unstable and sensitive to settings. DPO derives a loss directly from preference pairs, using the log-probability ratio between the policy and a frozen reference as an implicit reward; no reward model, no sampling loop, so it is cheaper and more stable, and it is the default for open-model preference tuning. RL-based methods still win when you can sample and score fresh outputs, which is why reasoning training uses RL.

MidWhat is GRPO, and why did it matter for reasoning models?

Group relative policy optimization, from DeepSeekMath (2024) and used for DeepSeek-R1. For each prompt it samples a group of answers, scores each with a reward (often a program: does the answer match, do the tests pass), and uses each answer's reward relative to the group mean, divided by the standard deviation, as its advantage. That removes PPO's value network, saving memory and complexity. With verifiable rewards it made large-scale RL on reasoning practical; DeepSeek-R1-Zero showed long chains of thought and self-checking emerging from RL alone. Prompts the model always or never solves give no signal, so data is chosen at the edge of the model's ability.

MidWhat is perplexity, and when is it the wrong metric?

Perplexity is exp of the average cross-entropy on held-out text: how many tokens the model is effectively choosing among per step. It is the right metric for comparing pretrained models with the same tokenizer on the same data, and for checking a domain-adapted model. It is wrong for chat assistants and agents: a model can have low perplexity and still be unhelpful, unsafe or bad at tools, and perplexities across different tokenizers are not comparable. Use task evals, pass rates and calibrated judges instead.

MidBLEU, ROUGE and BERTScore: what do they measure, and why are they weak for LLMs?

BLEU measures n-gram precision against a reference (translation); ROUGE measures overlap, often via the longest common subsequence (summarization); BERTScore compares contextual embeddings, so paraphrases score better. All compare with a reference, so a correct answer worded differently scores low, and a wrong answer that reuses the reference's words (adding a "not") scores high. For LLM output, prefer task checks, rubric-based judges calibrated against human labels, and human review; keep overlap metrics as a cheap regression signal at most.

SeniorWhy do models hallucinate, mechanistically, and how would you reduce it in a product?

OpenAI's 2025 analysis gives two causes. Pretraining: facts that appear rarely and follow no pattern (arbitrary facts such as an obscure birthday) cannot be learned reliably, so errors on them are statistically unavoidable. Post-training: most benchmarks grade a wrong answer and "I don't know" both as zero, so guessing always scores at least as well, and models learn to bluff. In a product: ground in retrieved sources and require citations, let the model abstain and score abstention above a wrong answer in your evals, verify claims with tools, validate tool calls against schemas, add a faithfulness check, and measure calibration on your own data.

SeniorHow do you know a benchmark score is trustworthy?

Ask whether it is saturated (top models near the ceiling, as with MMLU), contaminated (test items in training data; check n-gram overlap or use benchmarks built from new items such as LiveCodeBench), whether the setup matches yours (prompting, tools, number of attempts, pass@k versus pass^k), and whether it measures what you need (LMArena measures preference, which style can sway). Then run the shortlist on your own private eval set drawn from production traffic; that is the number that decides.

SeniorWhat is distillation, and what are its risks?

Training a small student to imitate a large teacher, on the teacher's token distributions (logit distillation) or on its generated outputs (sequence distillation). It is how most small capable models are made; DeepSeek distilled R1's reasoning into Qwen and Llama models from 1.5B to 70B. Risks: the student inherits the teacher's errors and biases; training repeatedly on synthetic data without fresh human data causes model collapse (loss of diversity and tail knowledge); provider terms often forbid training competing models on their outputs; and benchmark gains can hide narrower generalization, so evaluate on your own tasks.

SeniorWhat is in-context learning, and are emergent abilities real?

In-context learning is a model doing a new task from examples in the prompt without weight updates; it appeared strongly with GPT-3 and is why few-shot prompting works. Emergent abilities are skills that appear to switch on above some scale (Wei et al., 2022). Schaeffer et al. (2023) showed that many such jumps are artifacts of all-or-nothing metrics such as exact match; smooth metrics show steady improvement. A good answer gives both and the lesson: metric choice shapes what you see, so evaluate with metrics that reveal gradual progress.

SeniorWhat do interpretability and alignment research mean for an engineer building agents?

Interpretability studies what is inside models: features stored in superposition, separated with sparse autoencoders (Anthropic's Scaling Monosemanticity found millions in Claude 3 Sonnet) and circuits traced with attribution graphs. Alignment covers failure modes such as sycophancy, reward hacking, specification gaming and alignment faking. For engineers the takeaways are practical: a model's stated reasoning is not a guaranteed account of its computation, so verify actions rather than trusting explanations; preference training can produce flattery, so test for agreement with wrong user claims; and agents given goals and tools will exploit loopholes in graders and permissions, so design least privilege and robust checks (the Safety, security and governance and Production engineering and AI system design sections).

SeniorWhen does fine-tuning beat prompting and RAG?

When the gap is behavior, format or a narrow skill rather than knowledge; when a high-volume step must be cheaper or faster (distill a frontier model into a small one); or when outcomes are checkable and reinforcement fine-tuning can optimize them. It rarely beats RAG for changing knowledge. Count the full cost: data preparation, evaluation, hosting (often provisioned), and redoing it when a better base model ships. Prove the gain on a held-out eval set against the best prompt-only baseline.

MidWhat is LoRA, and why is it so widely used?

Low-rank adaptation freezes the base model and trains small matrices whose product is added to selected weight matrices, usually a few percent of the parameters or less. Training needs far less memory, adapters are megabytes instead of gigabytes, one base model can serve many adapters, and adapters can be merged for serving. QLoRA trains LoRA on a 4-bit quantized base model, making 7B to 13B models trainable on a single GPU.
