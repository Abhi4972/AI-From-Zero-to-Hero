In plain words first
The idea in one line

Gradient descent tells you which direction to move. It says nothing about how far or how fast. SGD is a compass. Adam is GPS with traffic data.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
Learning rate "Step size" The scalar multiplier on the gradient update; the single most impactful hyperparameter in training
SGD "Basic gradient descent" Stochastic gradient descent: update weights by subtracting $\text{lr} \cdot \text{gradient}$, computed on a mini-batch
Momentum "Rolling ball analogy" Exponential moving average of past gradients; dampens oscillation and accelerates consistent directions
RMSProp "Adaptive learning rate" Divides each parameter's gradient by the running RMS of its recent gradients; equalizes learning rates
Adam "The default optimizer" Combines momentum (first moment) and RMSProp (second moment) with bias correction for the initial steps
AdamW "Adam done right" Adam with decoupled weight decay; applies regularization directly to weights rather than through the gradient
Bias correction "Warmup for running averages" Dividing by $(1 - \beta^t)$ to compensate for the zero-initialization of Adam's moment estimates
Weight decay "Shrink the weights" Subtracting a fraction of the weight value at each step; a regularizer that penalizes large weights
Learning rate schedule "Changing lr over time" A function that adjusts the learning rate during training; warmup + cosine decay is the modern default
Gradient clipping "Capping the gradient norm" Scaling down the gradient vector when its norm exceeds a threshold; prevents exploding gradient updates

The problem

In plain words first

An optimiser turns gradients into weight changes. Plain SGD steps against the gradient by a fixed fraction. Momentum keeps a running velocity, so steady directions speed up and zig-zags cancel. Adam also tracks how large each weight's gradients usually are and divides by that, giving every weight its own step size. AdamW adds weight decay as a separate shrink, so it is not distorted by those per-weight step sizes. The learning rate still matters more than which of these you pick.

You computed the gradients. You know that weight #4,721 should decrease by 0.003 to reduce the loss. But 0.003 in what units? Scaled by what? And should you move the same amount on step 1 as on step 1,000?

Vanilla gradient descent applies the same learning rate to every parameter on every step: w=w−lr⋅gradient. This creates three problems that make training neural networks painful in practice.

First, oscillation. The loss landscape is rarely shaped like a smooth bowl. It's more like a long, narrow valley. The gradient points across the valley (steep direction), not along it (shallow direction). Gradient descent bounces back and forth across the narrow dimension while making tiny progress along the useful one. You've seen this: loss drops fast then plateaus, not because the model converged but because it's oscillating.

Second, one learning rate for all parameters is wrong. Some weights need large updates (they're in the early, underfitting stage). Others need tiny updates (they're near their optimal value). A learning rate that works for the former destroys the latter, and vice versa.

Third, saddle points. In high dimensions, the loss landscape has vast flat regions where the gradient is near zero. Vanilla SGD crawls through these at the speed of the gradient, which is effectively zero. The model looks stuck. It isn't stuck -- it's in a flat region with useful descent on the other side. But SGD has no mechanism to push through.

Adam solves all three. It maintains two running averages per parameter -- the mean gradient (momentum, handles oscillation) and the mean squared gradient (adaptive rate, handles different scales). Combined with bias correction for the first few steps, it gives you a single optimizer that works on 80% of problems with default hyperparameters. This lesson builds it from scratch so you understand exactly when and why it fails on the other 20%.

Idea 1 · Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD)

The simplest optimizer. Compute the gradient on a mini-batch and step in the opposite direction.

w=w−lr⋅gradient

The "stochastic" means you use a random subset (mini-batch) of data to estimate the gradient, rather than the full dataset. This noise is actually useful -- it helps escape sharp local minima. But the noise also causes oscillation.

Learning rate is the only knob. Too high: the loss diverges. Too low: training takes forever. The optimal value depends on the architecture, the data, the batch size, and the current stage of training. For vanilla SGD on modern networks, typical values range from 0.01 to 0.1. But even within a single training run, the ideal learning rate changes.

Idea 2 · Momentum

The ball-rolling-downhill analogy is overused but accurate. Instead of stepping by the gradient alone, you maintain a velocity that accumulates past gradients.

mt​w​=βmt−1​+gradient=w−lr⋅mt​​

Beta (typically 0.9) controls how much history to keep. With β=0.9, the momentum is roughly the average of the last 10 gradients (1/(1−0.9)=10).

Why this fixes oscillation: gradients that point in the same direction accumulate. Gradients that flip direction cancel out. In that narrow valley, the "across" component flips sign each step and gets dampened. The "along" component stays consistent and gets amplified. The result is smooth acceleration in the useful direction.

Real numbers: SGD alone on a badly conditioned loss landscape might take 10,000 steps. SGD with momentum (β=0.9) typically takes 3,000-5,000 steps on the same problem. The speedup is not marginal.

Worked example
A long, narrow valley

Take f(x,y)=21​(x2+100y2): 100 times steeper across (y) than along (x). Start at (−4,1) and count the steps until f<10−6, giving each method its best learning rate from a grid:

Method Best learning rate Steps
SGD 0.018 443
SGD + momentum (β=0.9) 0.0056 145
Adam 0.18 140

SGD's learning rate is capped by the steep direction: above 2/100=0.02 the y coordinate overshoots further each step and diverges, so along x it crawls. Momentum lets the consistent x gradient build up speed while the alternating y gradient cancels. The paths are drawn under Step 5.

Idea 3 · RMSProp

The first per-parameter adaptive learning rate method that actually worked. Proposed by Hinton in a Coursera lecture (never formally published).

st​w​=βst−1​+(1−β)⋅gradient2=w−lr⋅st​​+ϵgradient​​

st​ tracks the running average of squared gradients. Parameters with consistently large gradients get divided by a large number (smaller effective learning rate). Parameters with small gradients get divided by a small number (larger effective learning rate).

This solves the "one learning rate for all parameters" problem. A weight that's already been getting large updates is probably near its target -- slow it down. A weight that's been getting tiny updates might be undertrained -- speed it up.

Epsilon (typically 1e-8) prevents division by zero when a parameter hasn't been updated.

Idea 4 · Adam: Momentum + RMSProp

Adam combines both ideas. It maintains two exponential moving averages per parameter:

mt​vt​​=β1​mt−1​+(1−β1​)⋅gradient=β2​vt−1​+(1−β2​)⋅gradient2​​(first moment: mean)(second moment: variance)​

Bias correction is the key detail most explanations skip. At step 1, m1​=(1−β1​)⋅gradient. With β1​=0.9, that's 0.1⋅gradient -- ten times too small. The moving average hasn't warmed up yet. Bias correction compensates:

m^v^​=1−β1t​mt​​=1−β2t​vt​​​

At step 1 with β1​=0.9: m^=m1​/(1−0.9)=m1​/0.1= the actual gradient. At step 100: (1−0.9100) is approximately 1.0, so the correction vanishes. For the first moment (β₁ = 0.9) the correction matters for about the first 10 steps and is negligible after 50. For the second moment (β₂ = 0.999) it lasts far longer: after 50 steps the divisor 1 − 0.999⁵⁰ is still only 0.049, after 1,000 steps 0.63, and it fades only after a few thousand steps.

The update:

w=w−lr⋅v^​+ϵm^​

Adam defaults: lr=0.001, β1​=0.9, β2​=0.999, ϵ=10−8. These defaults work for 80% of problems. When they don't, change lr first. Then β2​. Almost never change β1​ or ϵ.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Adam is always faster than SGD."
    ✓ On this lesson's circle data, plain SGD at 0.05 reaches 95 percent training accuracy by epoch 8; Adam at its default 0.001 needs 54 epochs. With a learning rate of 0.01 Adam also gets there by epoch 8. Defaults are a starting point, not a tuned value.
  • ✗ "Bias correction only matters for a handful of steps."
    ✓ True for m, not for v: with β₂ = 0.999 the correction is still large after hundreds of steps.
  • ✗ "Momentum with the same learning rate takes the same size steps as SGD."
    ✓ With β = 0.9 the velocity settles at about 10 times the gradient, so momentum at lr 0.05 steps like SGD at 0.5. That is why Step 5's momentum run overshoots.

Idea 5 · AdamW: Weight Decay Done Right

L2 regularization adds λw2 to the loss. In vanilla SGD, this is equivalent to weight decay (subtracting λw from the weight at each step). In Adam, this equivalence breaks.

The Loshchilov & Hutter insight: when you add L2 to the loss and then Adam processes the gradient, the adaptive learning rate scales the regularization term too. Parameters with large gradient variance get less regularization. Parameters with small variance get more. This is not what you want -- you want uniform regularization regardless of the gradient statistics.

AdamW fixes this by applying weight decay directly to the weights, after the Adam update:

w=w−lr⋅v^​+ϵm^​−lr⋅λw

The weight decay term (lr⋅λw) is not scaled by Adam's adaptive factor. Every parameter gets the same proportional shrinkage.

This seems like a minor detail. It's not. AdamW converges to better solutions than Adam + L2 regularization on virtually every task. It's the default optimizer in PyTorch for training transformers, diffusion models, and most modern architectures. BERT, GPT, LLaMA, Stable Diffusion -- all trained with AdamW.

Worked example
Adam + L2 against AdamW

Two weights both start at 1.0, and their gradients are pure noise: standard deviation 1 for the first, 100 for the second. Weight decay should shrink both the same way. After 1,000 steps at lr = 0.01 with decay 0.1:

Quiet weight (noise 1) Noisy weight (noise 100)
Adam with L2 added to the gradient 0.333 1.006
AdamW (decoupled decay) 0.334 0.319

With L2, the decay term rides inside the gradient, so Adam divides it by the noisy weight's large v^​: that weight is never regularised. AdamW applies the same shrink to both.

Idea 6 · Learning Rate: The Most Important Hyperparameter

If you tune one hyperparameter, tune the learning rate. A 10x change in learning rate matters more than any architectural decision you'll make. Common defaults:

  • SGD: lr = 0.01 to 0.1
  • Adam/AdamW: lr = 1e-4 to 3e-4
  • Fine-tuning pretrained models: lr = 1e-5 to 5e-5
  • Learning rate warmup: linear ramp over first 1-10% of steps

Idea 8 · When Each Optimizer Wins

The same idea as a formula

With gradient gt​ at step t and learning rate η:

Optimiser Update
SGD w←w−ηgt​
Momentum v←βv+gt​;  w←w−ηv
RMSProp s←ρs+(1−ρ)gt2​;  w←w−ηgt​/(s​+ϵ)
Adam m←β1​m+(1−β1​)gt​,  v←β2​v+(1−β2​)gt2​;  w←w−ηm^/(v^​+ϵ)
AdamW Adam's step, plus w←w−ηλw applied separately

with Adam's bias corrections m^=m/(1−β1t​), v^=v/(1−β2t​). Typical values: β=0.9; β1​=0.9, β2​=0.999 (0.95 for large language models), ϵ=10−8.

Effective step sizes. Momentum with a steady gradient reaches v=g/(1−β), a step 1/(1−β)=10 times SGD's at β=0.9. Adam's step per weight is roughly η whatever the gradient's scale, so its learning rates (around 10−4 to 10−3) are not comparable with SGD's.

Why AdamW. Adding λw to the gradient inside Adam divides the decay by v^​, so weights with large gradients are barely regularised. AdamW's separate decay shrinks every weight by the same fraction ηλ per step.

Memory. Adam stores m and v: two extra numbers per parameter. With fp32 weights, gradients and states, training costs about 16 bytes per parameter, 112 GB for a 7-billion-parameter model before activations.

Step 1 · Vanilla SGD

class SGD:
    def __init__(self, lr=0.01):
        self.lr = lr

    def step(self, params, grads):
        for i in range(len(params)):
            params[i] -= self.lr * grads[i]

Step 2 · SGD with Momentum

class SGDMomentum:
    def __init__(self, lr=0.01, beta=0.9):
        self.lr = lr
        self.beta = beta
        self.velocities = None

    def step(self, params, grads):
        if self.velocities is None:
            self.velocities = [0.0] * len(params)
        for i in range(len(params)):
            self.velocities[i] = self.beta * self.velocities[i] + grads[i]
            params[i] -= self.lr * self.velocities[i]

Step 3 · Adam

import math

class Adam:
    def __init__(self, lr=0.001, beta1=0.9, beta2=0.999, epsilon=1e-8):
        self.lr = lr
        self.beta1 = beta1
        self.beta2 = beta2
        self.epsilon = epsilon
        self.m = None
        self.v = None
        self.t = 0

    def step(self, params, grads):
        if self.m is None:
            self.m = [0.0] * len(params)
            self.v = [0.0] * len(params)

        self.t += 1

        for i in range(len(params)):
            self.m[i] = self.beta1 * self.m[i] + (1 - self.beta1) * grads[i]
            self.v[i] = self.beta2 * self.v[i] + (1 - self.beta2) * grads[i] ** 2

            m_hat = self.m[i] / (1 - self.beta1 ** self.t)
            v_hat = self.v[i] / (1 - self.beta2 ** self.t)

            params[i] -= self.lr * m_hat / (math.sqrt(v_hat) + self.epsilon)

Step 4 · AdamW

class AdamW:
    def __init__(self, lr=0.001, beta1=0.9, beta2=0.999, epsilon=1e-8, weight_decay=0.01):
        self.lr = lr
        self.beta1 = beta1
        self.beta2 = beta2
        self.epsilon = epsilon
        self.weight_decay = weight_decay
        self.m = None
        self.v = None
        self.t = 0

    def step(self, params, grads):
        if self.m is None:
            self.m = [0.0] * len(params)
            self.v = [0.0] * len(params)

        self.t += 1

        for i in range(len(params)):
            self.m[i] = self.beta1 * self.m[i] + (1 - self.beta1) * grads[i]
            self.v[i] = self.beta2 * self.v[i] + (1 - self.beta2) * grads[i] ** 2

            m_hat = self.m[i] / (1 - self.beta1 ** self.t)
            v_hat = self.v[i] / (1 - self.beta2 ** self.t)

            params[i] -= self.lr * m_hat / (math.sqrt(v_hat) + self.epsilon)
            params[i] -= self.lr * self.weight_decay * params[i]

Step 5 · Training Comparison

Train the same two-layer network on the circle dataset from lesson 05 with all four optimizers. Compare convergence.

import random

def sigmoid(x):
    x = max(-500, min(500, x))
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-x))

def make_circle_data(n=200, seed=42):
    random.seed(seed)
    data = []
    for _ in range(n):
        x = random.uniform(-2, 2)
        y = random.uniform(-2, 2)
        label = 1.0 if x * x + y * y < 1.5 else 0.0
        data.append(([x, y], label))
    return data


class OptimizerTestNetwork:
    def __init__(self, optimizer, hidden_size=8):
        random.seed(0)
        self.hidden_size = hidden_size
        self.optimizer = optimizer

        self.w1 = [[random.gauss(0, 0.5) for _ in range(2)] for _ in range(hidden_size)]
        self.b1 = [0.0] * hidden_size
        self.w2 = [random.gauss(0, 0.5) for _ in range(hidden_size)]
        self.b2 = 0.0

    def get_params(self):
        params = []
        for row in self.w1:
            params.extend(row)
        params.extend(self.b1)
        params.extend(self.w2)
        params.append(self.b2)
        return params

    def set_params(self, params):
        idx = 0
        for i in range(self.hidden_size):
            for j in range(2):
                self.w1[i][j] = params[idx]
                idx += 1
        for i in range(self.hidden_size):
            self.b1[i] = params[idx]
            idx += 1
        for i in range(self.hidden_size):
            self.w2[i] = params[idx]
            idx += 1
        self.b2 = params[idx]

    def forward(self, x):
        self.x = x
        self.z1 = []
        self.h = []
        for i in range(self.hidden_size):
            z = self.w1[i][0] * x[0] + self.w1[i][1] * x[1] + self.b1[i]
            self.z1.append(z)
            self.h.append(max(0.0, z))

        self.z2 = sum(self.w2[i] * self.h[i] for i in range(self.hidden_size)) + self.b2
        self.out = sigmoid(self.z2)
        return self.out

    def compute_grads(self, target):
        eps = 1e-15
        p = max(eps, min(1 - eps, self.out))
        d_loss = -(target / p) + (1 - target) / (1 - p)
        d_sigmoid = self.out * (1 - self.out)
        d_out = d_loss * d_sigmoid

        grads = [0.0] * (self.hidden_size * 2 + self.hidden_size + self.hidden_size + 1)
        idx = 0
        for i in range(self.hidden_size):
            d_relu = 1.0 if self.z1[i] > 0 else 0.0
            d_h = d_out * self.w2[i] * d_relu
            grads[idx] = d_h * self.x[0]
            grads[idx + 1] = d_h * self.x[1]
            idx += 2

        for i in range(self.hidden_size):
            d_relu = 1.0 if self.z1[i] > 0 else 0.0
            grads[idx] = d_out * self.w2[i] * d_relu
            idx += 1

        for i in range(self.hidden_size):
            grads[idx] = d_out * self.h[i]
            idx += 1

        grads[idx] = d_out
        return grads

    def train(self, data, epochs=300):
        losses = []
        for epoch in range(epochs):
            total_loss = 0.0
            correct = 0
            for x, y in data:
                pred = self.forward(x)
                grads = self.compute_grads(y)
                params = self.get_params()
                self.optimizer.step(params, grads)
                self.set_params(params)

                eps = 1e-15
                p = max(eps, min(1 - eps, pred))
                total_loss += -(y * math.log(p) + (1 - y) * math.log(1 - p))
                if (pred >= 0.5) == (y >= 0.5):
                    correct += 1
            avg_loss = total_loss / len(data)
            accuracy = correct / len(data) * 100
            losses.append((avg_loss, accuracy))
            if epoch % 75 == 0 or epoch == epochs - 1:
                print(f"    Epoch {epoch:3d}: loss={avg_loss:.4f}, accuracy={accuracy:.1f}%")
        return losses

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • SGD steps against the gradient; its learning rate is capped by the steepest direction.
  • Momentum averages gradients, cancelling zig-zags and speeding up steady directions; its effective step is about lr ÷ (1 − β).
  • Adam divides each weight's step by its typical gradient size, with bias correction for the zero start.
  • AdamW decays weights directly, so noisy weights are regularised like quiet ones.
  • Tune the learning rate first; compare optimisers only at their own best learning rates.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Plain SGD, and momentum never gets there

SGD reaches 95% at epoch 8, Adam at epoch 54 and AdamW at 57; SGD with momentum stalls at 93% and never reaches 95%. The learning rates, not the algorithms, decide this race: momentum with β=0.9 multiplies SGD's step by about 10 and overshoots, while Adam's steps are about its learning rate, 0.001 per weight, fifty times smaller than SGD's. M1 reruns the race with matched step sizes.

SolutionM1 · Fair learning rates

Momentum at 0.005 now matches SGD exactly: 85 percent at epoch 4, 95 percent at epoch 8, 99 percent at the end. Adam at 0.01 also reaches 85 and 95 percent at epochs 4 and 8 and finishes with the lowest loss (0.0095, accuracy 99.5 percent). Once each optimiser gets a sensible learning rate, the differences shrink on a problem this small.

SolutionB1 · Past the stability limit

Each SGD step multiplies y by 1−0.021×100=−1.1: it flips sign and grows by 10 percent. After 100 steps ∣y∣=1.1100≈13,800 and the loss is around 1010. Momentum is stable up to 2(1+β)/100=0.038, so at 0.021 it still converges, though it oscillates. The largest stable learning rate is set by the steepest direction, which is why learning-rate warmup and gradient clipping exist.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

PyTorch optimizers handle parameter groups, gradient clipping, and learning rate scheduling:

import torch
import torch.optim as optim

model = torch.nn.Sequential(
    torch.nn.Linear(784, 256),
    torch.nn.ReLU(),
    torch.nn.Linear(256, 10),
)

optimizer = optim.AdamW(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4, weight_decay=0.01)

scheduler = optim.lr_scheduler.CosineAnnealingLR(optimizer, T_max=100)

for epoch in range(100):
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    output = model(torch.randn(32, 784))
    loss = torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(output, torch.randint(0, 10, (32,)))
    loss.backward()
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm=1.0)
    optimizer.step()
    scheduler.step()

The pattern is always: zero_grad, forward, loss, backward, (clip), step, (schedule). Memorize this order. Getting it wrong (e.g., calling scheduler.step() before optimizer.step()) is a common source of subtle bugs.

For CNNs, many practitioners still prefer SGD + momentum (lr=0.1, momentum=0.9, weight_decay=1e-4) with a step or cosine schedule. SGD finds flatter minima, which often generalize better. For transformers and LLMs, AdamW with warmup + cosine decay is the universal default. Don't fight the consensus without a measured reason.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • AdamW with warmup and decay is the standard recipe for transformers; 8-bit AdamW (bitsandbytes) and paged optimizers cut memory when fine-tuning on one GPU.
  • Muon has entered production. Moonshot AI trained Kimi K2 (1T parameters) with MuonClip, and PyTorch ships torch.optim.Muon (since 2.9); it is applied to 2-D weight matrices while AdamW handles embeddings and norms.

Adam keeps two running averages for each parameter: the mean of the gradient and the mean of the squared gradient. It divides the first by the square root of the second, so every parameter gets its own step size. AdamW adds weight decay that does not pass through these averages. You write one Adam step as a pure function: it takes the state and returns a new state.

Task

Write two functions.

  1. init_adam_state(params) returns the start state: a dict with "m" and "v" as float zero arrays of the shape of params, and "t" equal to the int 0.
  2. adam_step(params, grads, state, lr, beta1, beta2, epsilon, weight_decay) does one update and returns (new_params, new_state).

One step computes, in this order:

t←t+1
m←β1​m+(1−β1​)g,v←β2​v+(1−β2​)g2
m^=1−β1t​m​,v^=1−β2t​v​
θ←θ(1−ηλ)−ηv^​+ϵm^​

The counter grows before the bias correction, so the first step uses t=1. All operations are element by element. The decay factor multiplies the old parameters, as in the AdamW class of the lesson code. With weight_decay = 0 the step is plain Adam.

Reference solution and notes
import numpy as np


def init_adam_state(params):
    return {"m": np.zeros_like(params, dtype=float), "v": np.zeros_like(params, dtype=float), "t": 0}


def adam_step(params, grads, state, lr=0.001, beta1=0.9, beta2=0.999, epsilon=1e-8, weight_decay=0.0):
    t = state["t"] + 1
    m = beta1 * state["m"] + (1 - beta1) * grads
    v = beta2 * state["v"] + (1 - beta2) * grads ** 2
    m_hat = m / (1 - beta1 ** t)
    v_hat = v / (1 - beta2 ** t)
    new_params = params * (1 - weight_decay * lr)
    new_params = new_params - lr * m_hat / (np.sqrt(v_hat) + epsilon)
    return new_params, {"m": m, "v": v, "t": t}

Why bias correction

The moments start at 0. After one step, m=(1−β1​)g, which is only a tenth of the gradient for β1​=0.9. For v with β2​=0.999 the start value is a thousandth of g2. The division by 1−βt removes this bias exactly when the gradient is constant. Without it, the first steps are wrong in size, and the error in v is the larger one.

Why the first step has size lr

At t=1, m^=g and v^=g2. The update is ηg/(∣g∣+ϵ), which is about η times the sign of g. Adam is invariant to the scale of the gradient. This is why one learning rate works for parameters with very different gradient sizes.

Decoupled weight decay

L2 regularization adds λθ to the gradient. In Adam that term then passes through the division by v^​, so parameters with large gradients get almost no decay. AdamW applies the decay directly to the parameters. Every parameter then shrinks by the same factor 1−ηλ at each step.

Where this goes next

AdamW with warmup and cosine decay is the default optimizer for transformers in the later phases. The learning rate schedules lesson changes η at each step around this same update.

Plain gradient descent steps by the current gradient only. On a long narrow valley it moves from side to side and makes slow progress. Momentum keeps a velocity that adds up past gradients, so the steps in a consistent direction grow and the side to side steps cancel. You write the momentum update from the optimizers lesson and use it to minimize a quadratic.

Task

Write two functions.

  1. momentum_step(params, grads, velocity, lr, beta) does one update and returns (new_params, new_velocity).
  2. minimize_quadratic(A, b, x0, lr, beta, steps, nesterov) runs steps momentum updates on f(x)=21​xTAx−bTx and returns every point.

Use the velocity convention of the lesson. The gradient is not scaled by 1−β:

v←βv+g,θ←θ−ηv

The parameters move with the new velocity, not the old velocity.

The gradient of the quadratic is ∇f(x)=Ax−b. The velocity starts at 0. With nesterov=False the gradient is computed at the current point x. With nesterov=True the gradient is computed at the lookahead point x−ηβv, where v is the velocity before the update. The update of v and x is then the same momentum step.

Reference solution and notes
import numpy as np


def momentum_step(params, grads, velocity, lr=0.01, beta=0.9):
    new_velocity = beta * velocity + grads
    new_params = params - lr * new_velocity
    return new_params, new_velocity


def minimize_quadratic(A, b, x0, lr=0.1, beta=0.9, steps=50, nesterov=False):
    x = np.array(x0, dtype=float)
    velocity = np.zeros_like(x)
    trajectory = [x.copy()]
    for _ in range(steps):
        point = x - lr * beta * velocity if nesterov else x
        grad = A @ point - b
        x, velocity = momentum_step(x, grad, velocity, lr, beta)
        trajectory.append(x.copy())
    return np.array(trajectory)

Why momentum helps

With a constant gradient g, the velocity after k steps is g(1−βk)/(1−β). For β=0.9 the step grows to 10 times the plain gradient step in a consistent direction. When the gradient changes sign from step to step, the terms cancel and the velocity stays small. This is why momentum moves fast along a valley and slowly across it.

Two conventions

The lesson adds the raw gradient: v←βv+g. Some texts use an average: v←βv+(1−β)g. The two give the same direction, but the second has a step that is 1−β times smaller. When you copy a learning rate from a paper or a framework, check which convention it uses.

Nesterov

Nesterov momentum looks ahead. It computes the gradient where the velocity will carry the parameters. When the velocity points past the minimum, the lookahead gradient already points back, so the method corrects earlier. On the example quadratic the Nesterov trajectory overshoots less.

Explain the mechanismAdam keeps a running average of the gradient and a running average of its square, and divides each by one minus beta to the power t before the update. A teammate deletes those two divisions to simplify the code. What changes in the first steps of training, and when does the change stop mattering?

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Both averages start at zero, so at step one the first moment is only (1 - beta1) times the gradient, a tenth of it with beta1 at 0.9, and the second moment is a thousandth of the squared gradient with beta2 at 0.999. The divisions rescale both to unbiased estimates. Without them the square root of the second moment is shrunk more than the first moment, so the ratio and the step come out too large while the averages warm up. The first moment's factor is close to 1 after about 50 steps, but the second moment's factor, 1 - 0.999 to the t, stays well below 1 for thousands of steps, so without the division the step stays too large for that long.

The two averages.

m = beta1 * m + (1 - beta1) * g        first moment, the mean gradient
v = beta2 * v + (1 - beta2) * g^2      second moment, the mean squared gradient
m_hat = m / (1 - beta1^t)
v_hat = v / (1 - beta2^t)
w = w - lr * m_hat / (sqrt(v_hat) + eps)

The first moment is momentum: with beta1 at 0.9 it averages about the last ten gradients, from 1 over (1 - 0.9). The second moment sets a per-parameter step size: a weight with large gradients is divided by a large number and moves less, a weight with small gradients moves more.

Why the averages start wrong.

At step one, m is 0.1 times the gradient. The average has not warmed up, and the lesson calls that ten times too small. The same holds for v with a factor of 0.001. Dividing m by (1 - 0.9) at step one gives back the gradient. At step 100 the factor (1 - 0.9 to the 100) is about 1.0, so the correction is gone. The lesson's rule for the first moment: it matters for the first 10 steps or so and is irrelevant after 50. The second moment is slower. With beta2 at 0.999 its factor approaches 1 only after thousands of steps, so its correction lasts much longer.

What the deletion does.

The two moments are shrunk by different factors, and the update takes their ratio after a square root. The first moment is shrunk by 0.1. The second moment is shrunk by 0.001, and its square root by less than 0.1, so the denominator is too small and the step is too large. The early updates take oversized steps on statistics built from one or two gradients. The symptom is a loss that jumps in the first steps and a run that sometimes diverges before it settles.

What else guards the first steps.

Warmup, a small learning rate that ramps up over the first steps, protects the same period for a different reason: the correction fixes the scale of the averages, not their noise. The lesson's advice on tuning: change the learning rate first, then beta2, almost never beta1 or epsilon.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The division is a numerical safety term like epsilon." Epsilon guards a zero denominator; the division by one minus beta to the t corrects the zero initialization of the averages.
  • "Without it the first steps are too small, so training is only slower." The second moment's square root is shrunk more than the first moment, so the ratio and the step come out too large.
  • "Both corrections stop mattering after about 50 steps." That holds for the first moment only. With beta2 at 0.999 the second moment's factor stays well below 1 for thousands of steps.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) What does the first moment estimate and what does the second? Expected: the running mean of the gradient, which is momentum, and the running mean of the squared gradient, which sets a per-parameter step size.
  • (level 2) With beta1 at 0.9, how many past gradients does the first moment roughly average? Expected: about ten, from 1 over (1 - 0.9).
  • (level 3) Why does Adam still want a warmup if bias correction exists? Expected: the correction fixes the scale of the averages, not their noise; a few steps of statistics are a poor estimate, so a small early learning rate limits the damage.
  • (level 3) Which Adam hyperparameter do you change first when training is unstable? Expected: the learning rate, then beta2; almost never beta1 or epsilon.

Weigh a trade-offYou want weight decay on a transformer trained with Adam. One engineer adds an L2 penalty on the weights to the loss. Another uses an AdamW optimizer with a weight decay argument. They say the two are the same thing. Are they, and which do you choose?

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. They are the same only under plain SGD. In Adam, a penalty inside the loss becomes part of the gradient and is divided by the running gradient size like every other term, so weights with large gradient variance get less decay and weights with small variance get more. AdamW subtracts lr times lambda times w after the adaptive update, so every weight shrinks by the same fraction. Choose AdamW; the lesson makes it the default for transformers for this reason.

Where they agree.

The L2 term lambda over 2 times the sum of w squared has gradient lambda w. Under SGD the update subtracts lr times lambda w from each weight, which is weight decay by definition. Both names describe one operation.

Where Adam breaks the equivalence.

Adam does not apply the gradient. It averages it into the first moment, squares it into the second, and divides by the root of the second moment. A penalty that rides along in the gradient is rescaled per parameter by that division. A weight with noisy, large gradients has a large second moment and receives a small share of the decay. A weight with quiet gradients receives a large share. The regularization is uneven, and the lesson reports that it converges to worse solutions than the decoupled form on almost every task.

What AdamW does instead.

w = w - lr * m_hat / (sqrt(v_hat) + eps) - lr * lambda * w

The decay term sits outside the adaptive factor. Every weight loses the same fraction lr times lambda at each step. In the lesson's from-scratch class this is one extra line after the Adam update: subtract lr times weight decay times the parameter.

Values the lessons give.

  • 0.01 for AdamW on transformers; the fine-tuning default is also 0.01.
  • 1e-4 for SGD on CNNs, where the two forms coincide.
  • Up to 0.1 for a model that overfits heavily.

A test that shows the difference.

The lesson's exercise: initialize weights with large magnitudes, train Adam with L2 and AdamW with the same decay, and plot the weight norm over training. AdamW shrinks it faster and more evenly.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Weight decay and L2 are the same by definition." They coincide under SGD; under an adaptive optimizer the penalty is rescaled per parameter.
  • "Use Adam with L2 and a bigger lambda to compensate." A bigger lambda does not fix the unevenness; some weights still receive almost no decay.
  • "Adam adapts, so it does not need weight decay." Adaptation sets step sizes; it does not shrink weights. Overfit transformers still need decay.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why is the decay uniform in AdamW? Expected: it is applied to the weights directly, outside the adaptive division, so the fraction removed per step is lr times lambda for every weight.
  • (level 3) Where exactly does the penalty go wrong inside Adam? Expected: it enters the gradient, so it is averaged into the first moment and squared into the second, and the division by the root of the second moment rescales it per parameter.
  • (level 3) What decay values does the lesson give? Expected: 0.01 for AdamW on transformers, 1e-4 for SGD on CNNs, up to 0.1 for a heavily overfit model.
  • (level 4) What experiment shows the difference in a few lines? Expected: start from weights of large magnitude, train both with the same decay, and plot the weight norm; AdamW shrinks it faster and more evenly.

Check your understanding

Q1What problem does momentum solve in gradient descent?

In narrow valleys, gradients oscillate across the valley while making slow progress along it. Momentum accumulates past gradients: consistent directions amplify while oscillating directions cancel, giving smoother, faster convergence.

Q2What is the key difference between Adam and AdamW?

In Adam + L2, the adaptive learning rate scales the regularization term differently per parameter. AdamW decouples weight decay from the gradient update, applying uniform shrinkage regardless of gradient statistics.

Q3Why does Adam use bias correction in early training steps?

At step 1 with beta1=0.9, m_1 = 0.1 * gradient (10x too small). Dividing by (1 - 0.9^1) = 0.1 corrects this to the actual gradient. The correction becomes negligible after ~50 steps.

Q4What are the standard default hyperparameters for Adam?

The original Adam paper (Kingma & Ba, 2014) recommended lr=0.001, beta1=0.9, beta2=0.999, epsilon=1e-8. These defaults work well for most problems.

Q5Which optimizer is the modern default for training transformers and LLMs?

AdamW (Adam with decoupled weight decay) is used to train BERT, GPT, LLaMA, and virtually all modern transformers. It combines adaptive learning rates with proper weight decay regularization.
