The problem

In plain words first

In Lesson 5.9 the decoder only ever saw one summary vector of the whole input, and long inputs did not fit. Attention removes that limit. At every output step the decoder asks a question ("which input words matter for what I am about to write?"), scores every input position against that question, turns the scores into weights that add up to 1, and takes the weighted average of the encoder's states. The summary is rebuilt fresh for every output word. This lesson writes out the equations for the two classic scoring functions (additive and multiplicative), works through one step by hand, shows why transformers divide the scores by d​, and looks inside a trained translation model at how its attention heads divide the work.

Lesson 5.9 ended on the bottleneck. In the classic copy-task experiment, a GRU encoder-decoder that copies short inputs almost perfectly falls towards chance as the inputs grow long. The reason is structural, not a training bug: every bit of information the encoder gleaned has to fit in one fixed-size hidden state, and the decoder never sees anything else.

Bahdanau, Cho, and Bengio published a three-line fix in 2014. Instead of giving the decoder only the final encoder state, keep every encoder state. At each decoder step, compute a weighted average of encoder states where the weights say "how much does the decoder need to look at encoder position i right now?" That weighted average is the context, and it changes every decoder step.

That is the whole idea. Transformers extended it. Self-attention applied it to a single sequence. Multi-head attention ran it in parallel. But the 2014 version already broke the bottleneck, and once you have it, the pivot to transformers is engineering, not conceptual.

The idea

At each decoder step t:

  1. Use the previous decoder hidden state s_{t-1} as a query.
  2. Score it against every encoder hidden state h_1, ..., h_T. One scalar per encoder position.
  3. Softmax the scores to get attention weights α_{t,1}, ..., α_{t,T} that sum to 1.
  4. Context vector c_t = Σ α_{t,i} * h_i. Weighted average of encoder states.
  5. Decoder takes c_t plus the previous output token, produces the next token.

The weighted average is the point. When the decoder needs to translate "Je" to "I", it weights the encoder state over "Je" high and the others low. When it needs "not", it weights "pas" high. The context vector reshapes each step.

The same idea as a formula

For decoder step t with encoder states h1​,…,hT​:

et,i​=score(st−1​,hi​),αt,i​=∑j=1T​exp(et,j​)exp(et,i​)​,ct​=i=1∑T​αt,i​hi​

Then the decoder uses the context: in Bahdanau's model st​=GRU(st−1​,[embed(yt−1​);ct​]) and P(yt​∣y<t​,x)=softmax(Wo​[st​;ct​;embed(yt−1​)]).

The scoring functions.

name score(s,h) extra weights
additive (Bahdanau) va⊤​tanh(Wa​s+Ua​h) Wa​,Ua​,va​
dot (Luong) s⊤h none, needs ds​=dh​
general (Luong) s⊤Wh W
scaled dot (Transformer) q⊤k/dk​​ projections for q and k

Why it fixes the bottleneck. The context now has a direct, one-step path to every encoder state. In backpropagation, ∂ct​/∂hi​=αt,i​I plus the terms through the weights: the gradient from an output word reaches the source word it attended to without passing through the long product of recurrent Jacobians from Lesson 5.8.

Why softmax. The weights are positive and sum to 1, so ct​ is an average of encoder states and stays on their scale; softmax is also differentiable everywhere, so the "where to look" decision is learned by gradient descent. Adding the same constant to every score changes nothing, which is why implementations subtract the maximum score before exponentiating, to avoid overflow.

Why d​. If the components of q and k are independent with mean 0 and variance 1, then q⊤k=∑j=1d​qj​kj​ has variance d. With d=512 the scores have a standard deviation of about 23, softmax puts almost all the weight on one position, and its gradient almost vanishes. Dividing by d​ brings the standard deviation back to 1.

Cost. One decoder step scores T positions; a whole output of length T′ costs O(T⋅T′⋅d). For self-attention over one sequence that is O(T2d), the quadratic cost that later lessons on long context fight.

Worked example
Additive attention by hand

Two encoder states in 2-D, h1​=(1,0) and h2​=(0,1), decoder state s=(1,1). Take Wa​=I, Ua​=I, va​=(1,−1).

  • Position 1: tanh(s+h1​)=tanh(2,1)=(0.964,0.762), score e1​=0.964−0.762=0.202.
  • Position 2: tanh(s+h2​)=tanh(1,2)=(0.762,0.964), score e2​=0.762−0.964=−0.202.
  • Softmax: α1​=e0.202/(e0.202+e−0.202)=0.600, α2​=0.400.
  • Context: c=0.6(1,0)+0.4(0,1)=(0.6,0.4).

The vector va​ decided what "relevant" means: it rewards the first coordinate of the combined features and penalises the second. In a real model, Wa​, Ua​ and va​ are learned, so the network learns its own notion of relevance.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Attention adds information the encoder did not have."
    ✓ It only re-weights encoder states that already exist. What it removes is the need to squeeze them all into one vector first.
  • ✗ "High attention weight means the model used that word for its decision."
    ✓ The weight says how much a state contributed to an average. That state may itself mix information from many words, and alternative weightings can sometimes give the same output.
  • ✗ "Attention weights are always sharp, one-hot alignments."
    ✓ They are soft. How peaked they are depends on the scale of the scores, which is why the scaling factor matters.

Shapes (the thing that bites everyone)

This is where every attention implementation goes wrong the first time. Read slowly.

Thing Shape Notes
Encoder hidden states H (T_enc, d_h) If BiLSTM, d_h = 2 * d_hidden
Decoder hidden state s_{t-1} (d_s,) One vector
Attention score e_{t,i} scalar One per encoder position
Attention weight α_{t,i} scalar After softmax over all i
Context vector c_t (d_h,) Same shape as an encoder state

Bahdanau (additive) score. e_{t,i} = v_α^T * tanh(W_a * s_{t-1} + U_a * h_i).

  • s_{t-1} has shape (d_s,), h_i has shape (d_h,).
  • W_a has shape (d_attn, d_s). U_a has shape (d_attn, d_h).
  • Their sum inside the tanh has shape (d_attn,).
  • v_α has shape (d_attn,). The inner product with v_α collapses to a scalar. This is what v_α does. It is not magic. It is the projection that turns an attention-dim vector into a scalar score.

Luong (multiplicative) score. Three variants:

  • dot: e_{t,i} = s_t^T * h_i. Requires d_s == d_h. Hard constraint. Skip if your encoder is bidirectional.
  • general: e_{t,i} = s_t^T * W * h_i with W shape (d_s, d_h). Removes the equal-dim constraint.
  • concat: essentially the Bahdanau form. Rarely used since the first two are cheaper.

One Bahdanau / Luong gotcha worth naming. Bahdanau uses s_{t-1} (the decoder state before generating the current word). Luong uses s_t (the state after). Mixing them up gives a model that still trains, just not the one in the paper, and the difference is easy to miss when you compare results. Pick one paper and stick to its convention.

Step 1 · additive (Bahdanau) attention

import numpy as np


def additive_attention(decoder_state, encoder_states, W_a, U_a, v_a):
    projected_dec = W_a @ decoder_state
    projected_enc = encoder_states @ U_a.T
    combined = np.tanh(projected_enc + projected_dec)
    scores = combined @ v_a
    weights = softmax(scores)
    context = weights @ encoder_states
    return context, weights


def softmax(x):
    x = x - np.max(x)
    e = np.exp(x)
    return e / e.sum()

Check your shapes against the table above. encoder_states has shape (T_enc, d_h). projected_enc has shape (T_enc, d_attn). projected_dec has shape (d_attn,) and broadcasts. combined has shape (T_enc, d_attn). scores has shape (T_enc,). weights has shape (T_enc,). context has shape (d_h,). Ship it.

Step 2 · Luong dot and general

def dot_attention(decoder_state, encoder_states):
    scores = encoder_states @ decoder_state
    weights = softmax(scores)
    return weights @ encoder_states, weights


def general_attention(decoder_state, encoder_states, W):
    projected = W.T @ decoder_state
    scores = encoder_states @ projected
    weights = softmax(scores)
    return weights @ encoder_states, weights

Three lines each. This is why Luong's paper landed. Same accuracy on most tasks, a lot less code.

Step 3 · a worked numerical example

Given three encoder states (roughly "cat", "sat", "mat") and a decoder state that aligns most with the first, the attention distribution concentrates on position 0. If the decoder state shifts to align with the last, attention moves to position 2. The context vector tracks.

H = np.array([
    [1.0, 0.0, 0.2],
    [0.5, 0.5, 0.1],
    [0.1, 0.9, 0.3],
])

s_close_to_cat = np.array([0.9, 0.1, 0.2])
ctx, w = dot_attention(s_close_to_cat, H)
print("weights:", w.round(3))
What you should see
weights: [0.464 0.305 0.231]

First row wins, but only mildly: with vectors this short, the dot products differ by less than 1, and softmax turns small differences into similar weights. How sharp attention is depends on the size of the scores, which is exactly the problem the $\sqrt{d}$ scaling in transformers addresses (see the formula box and the first experiment under "Use it"). Then move the decoder state closer to the third encoder state and watch the weights shift. Attention is explicit, soft alignment.

Step 4 · why this is the bridge to transformers

Translate the language above into Q/K/V:

  • Query = decoder state s_{t-1}
  • Key = encoder states (what we score against)
  • Value = encoder states (what we weight and sum)

In classical attention, keys and values are the same thing. Self-attention separates them: you can query a sequence against itself, with different learned projections for K and V. Multi-head attention runs it in parallel with different learned projections. Transformers stack the whole stage many times and drop RNNs.

The math is the same. The shapes are the same. The pedagogical jump from Bahdanau attention to scaled dot-product attention is mostly notation.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

PyTorch and TensorFlow ship attention directly.

import torch
import torch.nn as nn

mha = nn.MultiheadAttention(embed_dim=128, num_heads=8, batch_first=True)
query = torch.randn(2, 5, 128)
key = torch.randn(2, 10, 128)
value = torch.randn(2, 10, 128)

output, weights = mha(query, key, value)
print(output.shape, weights.shape)
What you should see
torch.Size([2, 5, 128]) torch.Size([2, 5, 10])

That is a transformer attention layer. Query batch of 5 positions, key/value batch of 10 positions, 128-dim each, 8 heads. output is the new context-augmented queries. weights is the 5x10 alignment matrix you can visualize.

As the query slides from "cat" to "mat", the weight moves smoothly from the first state to the third: attention is a differentiable, soft selection, which is why it can be learned. Multiplying the same scores by 5 or 20 turns a mild preference into an almost hard choice. The layers do not all look the same. Layers 1 and 2 are almost one-to-one alignments between German and English words (shifted down by one row, because the row for each output token is computed while the decoder is reading the previous output word). The middle layers spread their attention more widely, and the printed entropies confirm it: the first layer is the sharpest. Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • The scoring function is settled; the engineering is not. Every production LLM uses scaled dot-product attention. FlashAttention (2022) and FlashAttention-3 (2024) compute exactly the same result without writing the T×T weight matrix to memory, and grouped-query attention shares keys and values across heads to shrink the cache; both are defaults in vLLM, SGLang and PyTorch's scaled_dot_product_attention.
  • Attention sinks are now an architecture feature. Trained transformers put large weight on the first token or other fixed tokens regardless of content; OpenAI's open-weight gpt-oss models add a learned "sink" term to every attention head, and sliding-window serving keeps the first tokens for the same reason. The columns of attention on the end marker in some heads above are a mild version of the habit.
  • Linear and hybrid attention. Linear-attention and Mamba-style layers replace the softmax over all positions with a fixed-size state; Lesson 5.8's "What's current" box lists production hybrids.

Sources: FlashAttention-3, PyTorch scaled_dot_product_attention, gpt-oss model card, Efficient Streaming Language Models with Attention Sinks.

When classical attention still matters

  • Pedagogy. The single-head, single-layer, RNN-based version makes every concept visible.
  • On-device sequence tasks where transformers do not fit.
  • Any paper from 2014-2017. You will misread it without knowing Bahdanau's convention.
  • Fine-grained alignment analysis in MT. Raw attention weights are an interpretability tool even on transformer models, and reading them requires knowing what they are.

The attention-weight-as-explanation trap

Attention weights look interpretable. They are weights that sum to one across positions; you can plot them; high means "looked at this." Reviewers love them.

They are not as interpretable as they look. Jain and Wallace (2019) showed that attention distributions can be permuted and replaced by arbitrary alternatives without changing model predictions for some tasks. Wiegreffe and Pinter (2019) answered that attention is not useless as explanation either: for some tasks the weights cannot be swapped freely without hurting the model, so it depends on the model and task. Never report attention weights as evidence of reasoning without an ablation or counterfactual check.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Attention: score every encoder state against the decoder's query, softmax the scores, and average the states with those weights; a new context at every step.
  • Additive (Bahdanau) scores use a small network; dot and general (Luong) scores use products; transformers use scaled dot products.
  • Divide dot-product scores by d​, or softmax saturates as the dimension grows; subtract the maximum before exponentiating.
  • Mask padding with −∞ before the softmax so padded positions get zero weight.
  • Attention gives each output a direct path to every input, which fixes the bottleneck and the long gradient path; its weights are a hint, not proof, of what a model used.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Close to 1 without scaling, moderate with it

Without scaling, the scores have a standard deviation of about 512​≈23, so the largest of 10 scores is typically dozens of units above the rest and softmax gives it nearly all the weight (above 0.9 on average in the run). Divided by 512​, the scores have standard deviation 1 and the largest weight is around 0.3, the same as at any other dimension. A saturated softmax has near-zero gradient for every key but one, which is why the scaling is in the transformer's attention formula.

SolutionB1 · nan

np.exp(1000.0) overflows to inf (the largest float64 is about 1.8×10308, and e1000 is about 10434), so the result is inf / inf = nan for every entry, and one nan spreads through the whole network. Subtracting the maximum changes nothing mathematically, since softmax is unchanged when the same constant is added to every score, but it makes the largest exponent e0=1, so nothing overflows: the correct answer is [0.665, 0.245, 0.090].

Debug a failureAn attention module between an encoder and a decoder runs without any exception, but the model's accuracy slowly falls behind the reference implementation. When you print the context tensor, its second dimension is the encoder length instead of the decoder length. Walk me through the diagnosis.

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. A module that runs and still produces the wrong shape is a broadcast bug, and a silent broadcast is the worst attention bug because it degrades accuracy instead of crashing. Write the expected shapes first: scores and weights are batch by decoder length by encoder length, and the context is batch by decoder length by the encoder state width. Then find the matmul whose operands were swapped or not transposed, fix it with one transpose or reshape, and add asserts on the shapes and on the weights summing to one.

The shape table.

Tensor Shape
Encoder states H (T_enc, d_h); d_h is twice the hidden size for a bidirectional encoder
Decoder state s (d_s,)
Score for one encoder position scalar
Weights after the softmax (T_enc,) for one decoder step
Context vector (d_h,), the same shape as one encoder state

Batched over decoder steps, the weights are (batch, T_dec, T_enc) and the context is (batch, T_dec, d_h).

How the wrong shape appears.

The context is weights @ H. If the code built the scores as H @ H.T because the query tensor was replaced by the encoder states, the shapes still multiply and the result has the encoder length where the decoder length belongs. Nothing raises. Downstream layers broadcast or reshape the tensor and the loss keeps falling, just more slowly.

The diagnosis steps.

  1. Print the shape of every intermediate tensor and compare with the table.
  2. Check the score formula. For the additive form the projection vector collapses an attention-dimension vector to a scalar; for the dot form the query and the key must have the same width, and a bidirectional encoder doubles the key width, so the dot form fails unless a matrix W sits between them.
  3. Check which decoder state feeds the query. One classic formulation uses the state before the current step and the other uses the state after it. Mixing them gives subtly wrong gradients that are very hard to see.
  4. Fix with one transpose, reshape or projection. Refuse any fix that only silences the mismatch by broadcasting.

The regression test.

assert output.shape == (batch, T_dec, d_h)
assert weights.shape == (batch, T_dec, T_enc)
assert weights.sum(dim=-1) is close to 1

The third line catches a softmax taken over the wrong axis, which is the other silent bug in this layer.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Reshape the output to the expected shape and continue." The values inside are wrong; a reshape only hides the mismatch.
  • "Lower the learning rate; the model is just unstable." The run is not unstable; it is computing the wrong quantity.
  • "Add a mask to fix the shape." A mask changes which positions get weight, not the layout of the tensor.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Which single test catches most attention bugs? Expected: the weights have shape batch by decoder length by encoder length and sum to one along the last axis.
  • (level 2) The score matrix comes out square in the encoder dimension. What happened? Expected: the keys were scored against themselves; the query tensor was replaced by the encoder states.
  • (level 3) Why does the plain dot score fail with a bidirectional encoder? Expected: the encoder state is twice the hidden width, so it no longer matches the decoder state; use the general form with a matrix between them.
  • (level 3) Why is the state convention a gradient problem and not a shape problem? Expected: both states have the same shape, so nothing fails; the gradient flows to the wrong step and the model learns a slightly wrong alignment.

Where it is taught. The attention mechanism lesson gives the shape table and the shape-debugging prompt, and the self-attention lesson gives the projections.

Explain the mechanismA paper draft shows attention heatmaps as proof that the model "reasons over the right tokens". You are the reviewer. What do you say about the heatmaps, and what would convince you that a given head does something specific?

Level 3 of 4 · about 10 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Attention weights are a distribution over positions, and on their own they are not an explanation: for some tasks they can be permuted or replaced by arbitrary alternatives without changing the prediction, so a heatmap shows where mass went, not that it mattered. Ask for an ablation or a counterfactual, zero or shuffle the head and show that the prediction moves. The head roles that have survived probing are specific and testable: heads that attend to the previous token, copy heads, and induction heads that find an earlier occurrence of the current token and copy what followed it.

The caveat from the literature.

A 2019 study showed that on some tasks the attention distribution can be permuted, or swapped for an adversarial alternative, while the output stays the same. The lesson's rule follows: never report attention weights as evidence of reasoning without an ablation or a counterfactual check. Reviewers like heatmaps because they are easy to read; that is a reason to be careful, not a reason to accept them.

What a head can show you.

The inspection hook in the lesson's block returns the per-head weights of shape (B, H, T, T). In a trained model the patterns differ by head: some attend to the token just before, some to the start of the sequence, some spread almost uniformly. Those are descriptions. Turning a description into a claim takes an intervention.

The evidence that convinces.

  1. Ablation. Zero the head's output or replace its weights with a uniform row and measure the change in loss or in the specific prediction. A head that matters moves the number.
  2. Counterfactual input. Change the token the heatmap points at and show that the output changes in the direction the story predicts.
  3. A behavioral test for the claimed role. For an induction head, feed a random token sequence repeated twice and show that the head attends from each token to the token that followed its first occurrence, and that ablating the head removes the copying.
  4. Replication across seeds and prompts. One heatmap on one example is an anecdote.

What to write in the review.

Ask the authors to keep the heatmaps as illustration and to add the intervention results as the evidence. If the ablation does not move the prediction, the heatmap was decoration.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "A high weight means the token caused the output." Weight shows where the lookup went; causation needs an intervention.
  • "Average the heads to get the model's attention." Heads do different things; the average hides the roles and still is not evidence.
  • "If the heatmap matches human intuition, the model reasons like a human." Agreement with intuition is not a test; the permutation result shows intuitive maps can be irrelevant to the output.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) A head attends almost uniformly across positions. What does that tell you? Expected: little on its own; it may carry an average or nothing, and only an ablation says which.
  • (level 3) Describe a test for an induction head in one sentence. Expected: repeat a random token sequence and check that the head attends from each token to the token after its earlier occurrence, with ablation removing the copy behavior.
  • (level 4) Why can the weights be permuted without changing the output? Expected: the values at the attended positions may carry near-identical information, other heads may be redundant, and later layers can compensate.
  • (level 4) What replaces the heatmap as the headline figure? Expected: the change in loss or in the target probability under ablation and counterfactual edits, with a band across seeds.

Where it is taught. The attention mechanism lesson states the explanation trap, the multi-head lesson lists the probed head roles, and the attention capstone exposes the weights for inspection.

Explain the mechanismA new teammate has read the word "attention" a hundred times and still cannot say what a query, a key or a value is. Explain the three to them in one minute. Then tell me what changed between the 2014 encoder-decoder version and the version inside a transformer.

Level 1 of 4 · about 6 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Attention is a soft lookup. Each token produces a query that asks for information, a key that says what the token holds, and a value that the token hands over when it is picked. The query is scored against every key, the scores go through a softmax, and the output is the weighted sum of the values. In the 2014 version the query was the decoder state and the keys and the values were the same encoder states; a transformer takes all three from one sequence through three learned projections.

The three projections.

For a sequence X of n tokens with d dimensions each:

Q = X @ Wq    (n, dk)   what each token looks for
K = X @ Wk    (n, dk)   what each token contains
V = X @ Wv    (n, dv)   what each token returns when picked
scores = Q @ K.T        (n, n)

Row i of the score matrix is one token's view of the whole sequence. The softmax turns the row into weights that sum to one. The output for token i is the weighted sum of all value vectors.

The database picture.

A real database matches a query to one key and returns one value. Attention matches a query to every key with a similarity score and returns a blend of every value. The blend is what lets the model combine information from several positions in one step.

What the 2014 version had.

The encoder-decoder attention of 2014 used the previous decoder state as the query and scored it against every encoder hidden state. The context vector was the weighted average of those same encoder states. In that design the keys and the values were the same thing. Two score functions were common: an additive score that passes the pair through a tanh and a learned vector, and a multiplicative score that takes a dot product. The three-line fix broke the fixed-size bottleneck of the encoder-decoder.

What a transformer changed.

Self-attention queries a sequence against itself, and it separates keys from values with different learned projections. Multi-head attention runs several of these in parallel. The weighted sum of values is the same idea. The score function changes: the additive tanh score and the plain dot product give way to a dot product divided by the square root of the key width.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The key and the value must be the same vector." They were in the classic version, but a transformer learns separate projections for them.
  • "Attention picks the single best matching token." The softmax gives a weight to every position, and the output blends all of the values.
  • "The query comes from the encoder." In encoder-decoder attention the query comes from the decoder and the keys and the values come from the encoder.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 1) What shape does the output have for one token? Expected: the value dimension, because the output is a weighted sum of value vectors.
  • (level 2) Why give keys and values separate projections? Expected: a token can be matched on one kind of information and return another kind.
  • (level 2) Why does the plain dot-product score need the query and the key to have the same dimension? Expected: a dot product is only defined for vectors of equal length; the general form inserts a matrix W to remove that constraint.
  • (level 3) Name the convention trap between the two classic score functions. Expected: one uses the decoder state before the current step and the other uses the state after it; mixing them produces subtly wrong gradients.
