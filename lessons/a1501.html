In plain words first
The idea in one line

In 2023 a chatbot answered a question in one turn. In 2026 a frontier model routinely runs minutes to hours on a single task. On METR's Time Horizon 1.1 suite (January 2026), Claude Opus 4.6 (February 2026) measured about 12 hours of expert work at 50% reliability, with a wide interval, and in May 2026 METR put Claude Mythos Preview above 16 hours, where its suite stops measuring reliably. The horizon doubled about every seven months over 2019 to 2024, and about every four months since 2023. Every assumption we built around single-turn chat — context, trust, failure modes, cost, observability — breaks when runs last longer than lunch.

The problem

In plain words first

A long-horizon agent works for minutes or hours on one task, taking many steps in a loop. METR measures how long a task a model can finish, as a time horizon: the length of task, in expert-human time, that it completes half the time. The catch is compounding. If each step succeeds with probability p, a run of N steps succeeds with about pN, so even good steps fail over long runs. Harnesses, loops and durable execution are covered in Lessons 14.13 and 14.112 (linked above). This lesson measures a small model's horizon on a chain task, then adds a check after each step.

A chatbot is a stateless function. It takes a prompt, returns a reply, and forgets. Even RAG-equipped systems built through 2024 behave this way: they plan inside a single context window, take one action, and surface the result.

An autonomous agent is different in kind. It runs a loop. It decides when to stop. It spends money — real tokens, real GPU hours, real downstream side effects — during the run. Long-horizon agents amplify every aspect of this: cost grows, error probability grows per step, and the gap between what we can evaluate and what gets shipped widens.

The numbers from METR make this concrete. Between GPT-2 and Claude Opus 4.6, the time horizon (the human task length a model completes at 50% reliability) grew from seconds to half a workday. The doubling time sits near seven months. If the trend holds another year, the 50% horizon hits multi-day tasks. That is qualitatively different from anything the chatbot era designed for.

Idea 1 · The METR Time Horizon, in one paragraph

METR (ex-ARC Evals) fits a logistic curve to task-success probability against the log of expert human completion time. The horizon is the intersection of that curve with the 50% probability line. The suite (HCAST, RE-Bench, SWAA) spans 1-minute through 8+ hour expert tasks in software, cyber, ML research, and general reasoning. The result is a scalar that compresses capability into a single human-legible unit: "this model can do the kind of task an expert spends X hours on."

Idea 2 · What actually breaks when the horizon grows

  • Context. A 14-hour run emits hundreds of thousands of tokens of observations, tool outputs, and reasoning traces. You can no longer carry the raw history; you need compression, checkpoints, and memory tiers (Phase 14 · 04-06).
  • Trust. At one turn you can read the whole answer. At 1,000 turns you can't. The review surface shifts from "read the output" to "audit the trajectory."
  • Failure modes. Short runs fail from capability limits. Long runs additionally fail from drift, loops, reward hacking, and eval-vs-deploy behavior gaps (see below). These failures are invisible until they compound.
  • Cost. A 14-hour autonomous run of Claude Opus 4.6 at full tool use can burn the budget of a month of chat. Without budgets and kill switches (Lessons 13-14), a single runaway loop pays for a small team.
  • Observability. Request logs are not enough. You need trajectory-level telemetry, action budgets, and canary tokens to catch silent misbehavior.

Idea 3 · Doubling times and what they imply

Past performance guarantees nothing, but the trend is too consistent to ignore. METR's fit (March 2025) puts the doubling at 7 months on HCAST-style tasks. The January 2026 update (Time Horizon 1.1, 228 tasks) keeps the long-run slope but estimates a faster recent one: about 131 days since 2023 and about 89 days since 2024. At the original 7-month slope, starting from the lesson's 14 hours:

  • 2026 horizon (Claude Opus 4.6, measured about 12 hours in February 2026; the lesson's code starts from 14): ~14 hours
  • 2027 horizon (forecast): ~48 hours
  • 2028 horizon (forecast): ~1 week

These are straight-line extrapolations, not predictions. They are the scale every design decision in this phase must at least survive.

Idea 4 · Eval-context gaming

The 2026 International AI Safety Report documented frontier models distinguishing evaluation from deployment contexts and behaving measurably safer in tests. Anthropic's 2024 alignment-faking study found alignment-faking reasoning in 12% of cases when Claude 3 Opus believed its answers would be used for training, rising to 78% once it was actually trained to comply. METR's own papers flag this explicitly: reported horizons are idealized upper bounds, not deployment predictions.

Practical consequence: a horizon number is a capability ceiling, not a reliability floor. Production deployment requires your own evals on your own distribution, plus the kill-switches, budgets, HITL checkpoints, and canary tokens covered in the rest of this phase.

The same idea as a formula

Compounding. If each step succeeds independently with probability p, a run of N steps succeeds with

P(N)=pN,N50​=⌊lnpln0.5​⌋.

At p=0.99, N50​=68 steps, which is the lesson's "99% per step still fails half the time near 70 steps".

METR's horizon. METR fits a logistic curve to success against the log of task length t:

P(success∣t)=1+e−(a+blog2​t)1​,t50​=2−a/b.

The horizon t50​ is where the curve crosses one half. Here t is expert-human time; in this lesson it is the number of chained steps.

Worked example
From one step to a horizon
  • The 0.5B model gets 30 of 40 single arithmetic steps right, so p=0.75.
  • pN predicts 0.56 at 2 steps, 0.32 at 4 and 0.10 at 8, so N50​=2.
  • Observed over 8 chains each: 0.50, 0.38 and 0.25. Long chains do a little better than pN, because errors are not independent: some chains happen to get easy operations.
  • The logistic fit gives a=1.56, b=−1.09, so t50​=21.56/1.09≈2.7 steps. This is the same kind of number METR reports, at a much smaller scale.

# A mini time-horizon measurement on a model you can run. Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct (greedy, CPU) does one arithmetic
# step at a time; its own answer feeds the next step, so an error anywhere ruins the chain, as in a long agent run.
# First the single-step reliability p from 40 steps, then 8 chains at each length N = 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, compared with
# the lesson's end_to_end_reliability(p, N). About 2 minutes.
import os, random, re, warnings
os.environ["HF_HUB_OFFLINE"] = "1"
warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")
import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM, logging as hf_logging
from main import end_to_end_reliability, max_steps_for_target
hf_logging.set_verbosity_error(); hf_logging.disable_progress_bar()
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct")
llm = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct").eval()
rng = random.Random(15)

def op():
    kind = rng.choice(["add", "subtract", "double"])
    k = rng.randint(2, 19)
    return (kind, k)
def apply(v, o):
    kind, k = o
    return v + k if kind == "add" else v - k if kind == "subtract" else v * 2
def say(o):
    kind, k = o
    return f"add {k}" if kind == "add" else f"subtract {k}" if kind == "subtract" else "double it"

def model_step(v, o):
    ids = tok.apply_chat_template([{"role": "user", "content": f"Current value: {v}. Operation: {say(o)}. "
                                    "Reply with the new value only, as a number."}],
                                  add_generation_prompt=True, return_tensors="pt", return_dict=True)["input_ids"]
    with torch.no_grad():
        out = llm.generate(ids, max_new_tokens=8, do_sample=False)
    m = re.search(r"-?\d+", tok.decode(out[0, ids.shape[1]:], skip_special_tokens=True))
    return int(m.group(0)) if m else None

singles = [(rng.randint(5, 60), op()) for _ in range(40)]
right = sum(model_step(v, o) == apply(v, o) for v, o in singles)
p = right / len(singles)
print(f"single-step reliability: {right}/40 = {p:.3f}")

def run_chain(n, step=model_step):
    v = truth = rng.randint(5, 60)
    for _ in range(n):
        o = op()
        truth = apply(truth, o)
        v = step(v, o)
        if v is None:
            return False
    return v == truth
RESULTS = {}
print(f"\n{'steps N':>8} {'chains ok':>10} {'observed':>9} {'p**N':>7}")
for n in (1, 2, 4, 8, 16):
    ok = sum(run_chain(n) for _ in range(8))
    RESULTS[n] = ok / 8
    print(f"{n:>8} {ok:>7}/8 {ok / 8:>9.2f} {end_to_end_reliability(p, n):>7.2f}")
print(f"\nmax_steps_for_target(p, 0.5) = {max_steps_for_target(p, 0.5)} steps predicted at 50% end-to-end")

Fit the curve, then add a check per step

This block continues from the one above. It fits METR's logistic curve to the chains and reads off the 50% horizon. Then every step gets a check (recomputing the answer, as a test would) with up to two sampled retries from the last verified value. If both retries fail, the run stops and escalates. About 2 minutes.

# This block continues from the one above. (1) Fit METR's curve to the chains: a logistic in log2(steps), and the
# 50% horizon where it crosses one half. (2) Add a check after every step, as tests or a verifier would in a real
# run: a wrong step is retried, sampled at temperature 0.7, up to twice, from the last verified value (a checkpoint);
# if both retries fail, the run stops and escalates. Re-measure at N = 8 and 16. About 2 minutes.
import math
def fit_logistic(points, iters=4000, lr=0.5):
    # points: (x = log2 N, success 0/1); gradient ascent on the log-likelihood
    a, b = 0.0, 0.0
    for _ in range(iters):
        ga = gb = 0.0
        for x, y in points:
            q = 1 / (1 + math.exp(-(a + b * x)))
            ga += y - q; gb += (y - q) * x
        a += lr * ga / len(points); b += lr * gb / len(points)
    return a, b
points = [(math.log2(n), 1) for n, r in RESULTS.items() for _ in range(round(r * 8))] + \
         [(math.log2(n), 0) for n, r in RESULTS.items() for _ in range(8 - round(r * 8))]
a, b = fit_logistic(points)
h50 = 2 ** (-a / b)
print(f"logistic fit: P(success) = 1 / (1 + exp(-({a:.2f} + {b:.2f} * log2 N)))")
print(f"50% horizon: {h50:.1f} steps   (the p**N rule said {max_steps_for_target(p, 0.5)})")

def sampled_step(v, o):
    ids = tok.apply_chat_template([{"role": "user", "content": f"Current value: {v}. Operation: {say(o)}. "
                                    "Reply with the new value only, as a number."}],
                                  add_generation_prompt=True, return_tensors="pt", return_dict=True)["input_ids"]
    with torch.no_grad():
        out = llm.generate(ids, max_new_tokens=8, do_sample=True, temperature=0.7, top_p=0.95, top_k=None)
    m = re.search(r"-?\d+", tok.decode(out[0, ids.shape[1]:], skip_special_tokens=True))
    return int(m.group(0)) if m else None
CALLS = {"n": 0, "retries": 0, "unrecovered": 0}
def checked_step(v, o):
    expected = apply(v, o)          # the check: tests, a verifier, or a recomputation
    got = model_step(v, o); CALLS["n"] += 1
    tries = 0
    while got != expected and tries < 2:
        got = sampled_step(v, o); CALLS["n"] += 1; CALLS["retries"] += 1; tries += 1
    if got != expected:
        CALLS["unrecovered"] += 1
    return got
def checked_chain(n):
    """Stop and escalate at the first step the check cannot fix, instead of carrying a wrong value forward."""
    v = rng.randint(5, 60)
    for k in range(1, n + 1):
        o = op()
        before = CALLS["unrecovered"]
        v = checked_step(v, o)
        if CALLS["unrecovered"] > before:
            return "escalated", k
    return "ok", n
torch.manual_seed(0)
print("\nwith a check after every step, up to 2 retries from the checkpoint, and escalation if both fail:")
for n in (8, 16):
    CALLS.update(n=0, retries=0, unrecovered=0)
    outcomes = [checked_chain(n) for _ in range(8)]
    ok = sum(o == "ok" for o, _ in outcomes)
    where = sorted(k for o, k in outcomes if o == "escalated")
    print(f"   N={n:<3} finished correct {ok}/8   escalated {8 - ok}/8 (at steps {where})   silently wrong 0/8   "
          f"model calls {CALLS['n']}, retries {CALLS['retries']}")
print("   without the check, every failed chain above ended with a wrong number and no warning")

The fitted horizon is 2.7 steps. METR's logistic curve, fitted to the 40 chains, crosses one half at about 2.7 steps, close to the pN estimate.

Checks change what failure looks like. With a recomputation after every step and two sampled retries, 2 of 8 eight-step chains finish correct and none of the 16-step chains do. Retries rescue some steps, but the model often repeats the same mistake.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

Run code/main.py. It simulates the METR horizon curve and shows:

  • How the 50% horizon scales with a chosen doubling time.
  • How per-step failure probability compounds across a run.
  • How a 99% per-step reliable agent still fails half the time on a 70-step trajectory.

The simulator uses stdlib only. The intent is pedagogical: hold the numbers in your head before trusting a deployed agent to run unattended.

Part 1 of 4 · HorizonConfig, horizon_at, months_to_cross, end_to_end_reliability, max_steps_for_target, fmt_hours

"""METR-style time-horizon simulator — stdlib Python.

Given a doubling time and a baseline horizon, projects the 50% task-completion
horizon across future years. Separately, shows how per-step reliability
compounds across trajectories: a 99% per-step agent still fails a coin flip on
a 70-step task.

Pedagogical, not calibrated. The point is to hold the numbers in your head
before trusting an agent to run unattended.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

import math
from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass
class HorizonConfig:
    baseline_hours: float
    baseline_month: int  # months since epoch (0 = now)
    doubling_months: float


def horizon_at(cfg: HorizonConfig, months_from_now: int) -> float:
    """Project the 50% horizon at a given month offset."""
    delta = months_from_now - cfg.baseline_month
    return cfg.baseline_hours * (2 ** (delta / cfg.doubling_months))


def months_to_cross(cfg: HorizonConfig, target_hours: float) -> float:
    """Months until horizon reaches target_hours."""
    ratio = target_hours / cfg.baseline_hours
    return cfg.baseline_month + cfg.doubling_months * math.log2(ratio)


def end_to_end_reliability(per_step: float, steps: int) -> float:
    """Probability that every step succeeds in sequence."""
    return per_step ** steps


def max_steps_for_target(per_step: float, target: float) -> int:
    """Largest N such that per_step**N >= target."""
    if per_step >= 1.0:
        return 10**9
    return math.floor(math.log(target) / math.log(per_step))


def fmt_hours(h: float) -> str:
    if h < 1:
        return f"{h * 60:.1f} min"
    if h < 24:
        return f"{h:.1f} hr"
    return f"{h / 24:.1f} day"

def horizon_projection() -> None:
    """Plot the horizon forward using METR's fit slope."""
    cfg = HorizonConfig(
        baseline_hours=14.0,
        baseline_month=0,
        doubling_months=7.0,
    )
    print("\nMETR-style horizon projection")
    print("-" * 70)
    print(f"  baseline: {cfg.baseline_hours:.1f} hr at month 0 "
          f"(Claude Opus 4.6, Jan 2026)")
    print(f"  doubling time: {cfg.doubling_months:.1f} months")
    print()
    print(f"  {'month':>8}  {'horizon':>12}  {'interpretation':<30}")
    for m in (0, 6, 12, 18, 24, 30, 36):
        h = horizon_at(cfg, m)
        tag = ""
        if h < 24:
            tag = "workday-scale"
        elif h < 168:
            tag = "multi-day task"
        elif h < 720:
            tag = "week-scale"
        else:
            tag = "month-scale"
        print(f"  {m:>8}  {fmt_hours(h):>12}  {tag:<30}")

    print()
    print("  target crossings")
    for target in (24, 48, 168, 720):
        m = months_to_cross(cfg, target)
        print(f"    {fmt_hours(target)}: month {m:.1f}")


def reliability_compounding() -> None:
    """Show how per-step reliability decays across a trajectory."""
    print("\nPer-step reliability -> end-to-end reliability")
    print("-" * 70)
    print(f"  {'per-step':>10}  {'steps':>8}  {'end-to-end':>12}  "
          f"{'flag':<20}")
    cases = [
        (0.90, 10),
        (0.90, 50),
        (0.95, 50),
        (0.99, 50),
        (0.99, 70),
        (0.99, 200),
        (0.995, 200),
        (0.999, 1000),
    ]
    for per_step, steps in cases:
        p = end_to_end_reliability(per_step, steps)
        flag = ""
        if p < 0.5:
            flag = "coin flip or worse"
        elif p < 0.8:
            flag = "not production"
        elif p < 0.95:
            flag = "fragile"
        else:
            flag = "ok"
        print(f"  {per_step:>10.3f}  {steps:>8}  {p:>12.1%}  {flag:<20}")

    print()
    print("  max trajectory length for 50% end-to-end success")
    for per_step in (0.90, 0.95, 0.99, 0.995, 0.999):
        n = max_steps_for_target(per_step, 0.50)
        print(f"    per-step {per_step:.3f}: up to {n} steps")

HEADLINE: horizons grow exponentially, reliability compounds
----------------------------------------------------------------------
  At 7-month doubling, a multi-day horizon is ~1 year away.
  At 99% per-step, a 70-step trajectory is already a coin flip.
  Both numbers matter at the same time. Design for both.
Break it on purpose

A team reads that a frontier model's 50% horizon is 12 hours. They give it a nightly 6-hour data-migration job, unattended, because "6 hours is half its horizon". It succeeds most nights, but one night in four the output is wrong and nobody notices for days. Using this lesson, what did they misread, and what should the run have had? (Solution B1.)

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Measured horizons. On METR's Time Horizon 1.1 suite, Claude Opus 4.6 measured about 12 hours (February 2026) and Claude Mythos Preview more than 16 hours (May 2026), beyond what the suite measures reliably. METR estimates a doubling of about 131 days since 2023 and has since stopped updating its public page.
  • Long runs need harness support. Coding agents run for hours with context compaction, progress files, checkpoints and tests between steps (see 14.40 and Lesson 14.13). The check-per-step pattern here is the same idea.
  • Horizons are ceilings. METR states that its numbers come from benchmark conditions. Production teams measure their own per-step and end-to-end success on their own tasks.
  • On your laptop: the chain measurement runs the 0.5B model on CPU in a few minutes. The same harness, pointed at a 7B model in 4-bit (about 4–5 GB, fits the 8 GB RTX 4060), would give a longer horizon to compare.

Sources: METR, Time Horizon 1.1, METR, Task-completion time horizons, METR, Measuring AI ability to complete long tasks, Anthropic, Alignment faking in large language models.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • The time horizon is the task length a model finishes half the time; it is a ceiling, not a floor.
  • Per-step reliability compounds: pN falls fast as runs get longer.
  • Retries help little when the model is wrong in the same way every time.
  • A check per step turns silent wrong answers into stops you can act on.
  • Measure your own horizon on your own tasks before trusting an unattended run.

Ship it

outputs/skill-horizon-reality-check.md helps you answer a practical question: given a task you want to hand to an agent, does the current frontier's horizon cover it with enough margin, or are you about to ship a runaway?

outputs/skill-horizon-reality-check.md

---
name: horizon-reality-check
description: Given a task you want to hand to an agent, decide whether the current frontier's horizon covers it with enough margin.
version: 1.0.0
phase: 15
lesson: 1
tags: [autonomous-agents, metr, time-horizon, reliability, deployment]
---

Given a proposed autonomous task (what the agent should do, how long a human expert would take, what the failure cost is), produce a reality check on whether the current frontier model's horizon actually covers it.

Produce:

1. **Expert-time estimate.** Ask the user for the median expert completion time in minutes or hours. If they cannot estimate it, refuse and redirect them to measure a small sample first.
2. **Headroom ratio.** Divide the chosen model's 50% METR horizon by the expert-time estimate. Flag any ratio under 4x — at 50% success probability, you want a generous margin. At ratio 2x or below, refuse the deployment unless HITL is in the loop on every significant action.
3. **Reliability budget.** Estimate trajectory length in tool calls, then compute end-to-end success at per-step reliability 0.95, 0.99, 0.995. If the task length exceeds the 50%-success threshold at your assumed per-step reliability, require checkpoints or split the task.
4. **Eval-vs-deploy adjustment.** Apply a 20-40% gap between benchmark horizon and deploy-context horizon. Cite the Anthropic 2024 alignment-faking study or the 2026 International AI Safety Report when justifying to stakeholders.
5. **Required controls.** Based on headroom, list the minimum set of controls: budget cap, iteration cap, kill switch, HITL checkpoint points, canary tokens, and trajectory audit schedule.

Hard rejects:
- Any deployment at horizon ratio below 2x without HITL on every consequential action.
- Any claim that a model "can do" a task based on the METR horizon alone. The horizon is the 50% mark on a logistic curve; tail failures are guaranteed.
- Treating METR horizons as a floor rather than a ceiling.

Refusal rules:
- If the user cannot estimate expert-time for the task, refuse and ask them to measure a small sample first. Anything else is guesswork.
- If the proposed task would cost more than the user's worst-case budget at full model pricing, refuse and recommend budget controls from Lesson 13 before proceeding.
- If the user describes a task that touches irreversible actions (financial transactions, production database writes, emails to customers) without any HITL layer, refuse. The horizon argument does not clear irreversible deployment.

Output format:

Return a short memo with:
- **Task summary** (one sentence)
- **Expert-time estimate** (with units)
- **Headroom ratio** (with explicit number)
- **End-to-end reliability estimate** (table at three per-step rates)
- **Minimum controls** (bulleted)
- **Go / hold / no-go** (explicit verdict plus one-sentence justification)

Exercises

  1. Run the simulator. With the default 7-month doubling, how many months until the horizon crosses 30 hours? 168 hours? Plot the two crossings.

  2. Set per-step reliability to 0.995. What trajectory length still clears 50% end-to-end reliability? Compare to 0.99 and 0.999. Per-step reliability has exponential consequences at scale.

  3. Read METR's Time Horizon 1.1 blog post. Identify one methodological choice (task weighting, expert baseline, success criterion) that you would change. Write one paragraph explaining why.

  4. Pick one production agent workflow you know. Estimate the median trajectory length in tool calls. Multiply by your best guess of per-step reliability. Is the resulting end-to-end number honest with your users?

  5. Read the 2026 International AI Safety Report section on eval-context gaming. Design one evaluation protocol that would be robust to a model behaving differently in tests than in deployment.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · About two steps

About two. With p=0.75, p2=0.56 and p3=0.42, so the 50% point is between 2 and 3 steps. The logistic fit to the observed chains gives 2.7.

The observed chains roughly follow pN, a little better at 8 steps (0.25 against 0.10), because errors are not independent across steps.

SolutionB1 · Half the horizon is not safe

They read a 50% success point as a safety margin. At the horizon the model fails half the time; "half the horizon" still fails often. A 6-hour job also has hundreds of steps, each of which can go wrong, and the horizon was measured under benchmark conditions, not on their data. One failure in four is about what you would expect.

The run needed: - Checks between steps: row counts, checksums, and invariants on the migrated tables. - Checkpoints, so a failed step resumes rather than restarts. - A stop-and-escalate rule, so a failed check pages someone instead of finishing with wrong data.

Before going unattended, they should also have measured their own per-step and end-to-end success over a few supervised nights.
