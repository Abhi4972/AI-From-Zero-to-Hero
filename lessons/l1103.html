Why this came about

As models multiplied, benchmarks became the way to compare them, then grew saturated and contaminated. At the same time, hallucination emerged as the most visible weakness of language models. Understanding both is necessary to judge models for real tasks.

In plain words first
Exam scores, and why they mislead

Benchmarks are standard exams for models. They help compare models, but scores can mislead: the exam may not resemble your task, questions may have leaked into training data (contamination), top models may all score near the ceiling, and small differences may be noise. Hallucination, a fluent but unsupported answer, comes from how models are trained to always produce plausible text. Your own evaluation set on your task is the score that matters.

Why models hallucinate

A hallucination is fluent, confident text that is false. OpenAI's 2025 paper "Why Language Models Hallucinate" (Kalai, Nachum, Vempala and Zhang) gives a clear statistical account with two parts. In pretraining, the model learns from patterns, and some facts have no pattern: an obscure person's birthday seen once in the data cannot be inferred, so errors on such arbitrary facts are unavoidable. In post-training, the problem is how models are graded: most benchmarks score a right answer 1 and both a wrong answer and "I don't know" 0, so guessing always pays.

# Why models guess (Kalai et al., OpenAI, 2025): grading that gives 0 for "I don't know" rewards guessing
def expected_score(p_correct, wrong_penalty):
    guess = p_correct * 1 + (1 - p_correct) * -wrong_penalty
    abstain = 0.0
    return guess, abstain

for penalty, label in [(0, "binary grading (wrong = 0)"), (1, "wrong answers cost 1 point")]:
    print(label)
    for p in [0.2, 0.5, 0.8]:
        g, a = expected_score(p, penalty)
        print(f"   {p:.0%} sure: guess scores {g:+.1f}, abstain scores {a:+.1f} -> {'guess' if g > a else 'either' if g == a else 'abstain'}")
What you should see
binary grading (wrong = 0)
   20% sure: guess scores +0.2, abstain scores +0.0 -> guess
   50% sure: guess scores +0.5, abstain scores +0.0 -> guess
   80% sure: guess scores +0.8, abstain scores +0.0 -> guess
wrong answers cost 1 point
   20% sure: guess scores -0.6, abstain scores +0.0 -> abstain
   50% sure: guess scores +0.0, abstain scores +0.0 -> either
   80% sure: guess scores +0.6, abstain scores +0.0 -> guess

Under binary grading, guessing beats abstaining at any confidence, so models trained and selected on such benchmarks learn to bluff. Penalize wrong answers and abstaining becomes the right move below the break-even confidence. The paper's proposal is to state a confidence threshold in evaluation instructions and score accordingly.

The engineering mitigations follow from the causes: ground answers in retrieved sources and require citations (the Retrieval (RAG) section), let the model say it does not know and reward that in your evals, check claims against tools, and measure calibration (does 80% confidence mean right 80% of the time?). Hallucination in agents also includes invented tool names, arguments and results, which schema validation and tool-result checks catch (Lesson 14.139).

In-context learning and emergent abilities

In-context learning is a model learning a task from examples in the prompt, with no weight updates: give three labeled tickets and it classifies the fourth. It was the headline finding of GPT-3 (2020) and is why few-shot prompting works (Lesson 11.25). Emergent abilities are skills that seem to appear suddenly above some model size (Wei et al., 2022). Schaeffer et al. (2023, "Are Emergent Abilities of Large Language Models a Mirage?") showed that many such jumps come from all-or-nothing metrics such as exact match: measured with a smooth metric, ability improves gradually. Both views are worth knowing; the practical lesson is that metric choice shapes what you see.

Text-overlap metrics are still common for summarization and translation. BLEU measures n-gram precision against a reference (translation); ROUGE measures overlap, often via the longest common subsequence (summarization); BERTScore compares contextual embeddings instead of exact words, so paraphrases score better. Their blind spot is meaning:

import math
from collections import Counter
def ngrams(words, n): return Counter(tuple(words[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(words) - n + 1))

def bleu(candidate, reference, max_n=2):
    """Simplified BLEU: geometric mean of clipped n-gram precisions, times a brevity penalty."""
    c, r = candidate.lower().split(), reference.lower().split()
    precisions = []
    for n in range(1, max_n + 1):
        cn, rn = ngrams(c, n), ngrams(r, n)
        precisions.append(max(sum((cn & rn).values()), 1e-9) / max(sum(cn.values()), 1))
    bp = 1.0 if len(c) > len(r) else math.exp(1 - len(r) / len(c))
    return bp * math.exp(sum(math.log(p) for p in precisions) / max_n)

def rouge_l(candidate, reference):
    """ROUGE-L F1: based on the longest common subsequence of words."""
    c, r = candidate.lower().split(), reference.lower().split()
    dp = [[0] * (len(r) + 1) for _ in range(len(c) + 1)]
    for i in range(len(c)):
        for j in range(len(r)):
            dp[i + 1][j + 1] = dp[i][j] + 1 if c[i] == r[j] else max(dp[i][j + 1], dp[i + 1][j])
    lcs = dp[-1][-1]
    p, rc = lcs / len(c), lcs / len(r)
    return 0 if lcs == 0 else 2 * p * rc / (p + rc)

ref = "your refund was issued and will arrive in five days"
for cand in ["your refund was issued and will arrive in five days",
             "the money is on its way back to you within a week",       # same meaning, different words
             "your refund was not issued and will not arrive"]:          # opposite meaning, same words
    print(f"BLEU {bleu(cand, ref):.2f}  ROUGE-L {rouge_l(cand, ref):.2f} | {cand}")
What you should see
BLEU 1.00  ROUGE-L 1.00 | your refund was issued and will arrive in five days
BLEU 0.00  ROUGE-L 0.00 | the money is on its way back to you within a week
BLEU 0.56  ROUGE-L 0.74 | your refund was not issued and will not arrive

A correct paraphrase scores zero and an answer saying the opposite scores well. That is why LLM output is judged with task checks, rubric-based LLM judges calibrated against people, and human review (Lesson 14.143), and overlap metrics are used only as a rough signal.

Contamination is benchmark questions leaking into training data, which inflates scores. The usual check is n-gram overlap between test items and the training corpus (GPT-3's paper used 13-word windows):

# Contamination check: does a benchmark question appear (nearly) verbatim in the training data?
def shingles(text, n=8):
    w = text.lower().split()
    return {tuple(w[i:i + n]) for i in range(len(w) - n + 1)}

training_docs = [
    "forum post: a train leaves the station at 3 pm travelling at 60 km per hour and another train leaves at 4 pm",
    "recipe blog: preheat the oven to 180 degrees and grease the tin before you start",
]
benchmark = [
    "A train leaves the station at 3 pm travelling at 60 km per hour and another train leaves at 4 pm at 80 km per hour. When does it catch up?",
    "A cyclist sets off at 9 am at 15 km per hour; a second cyclist follows at 10 am at 20 km per hour. When do they meet?",
]
train_shingles = set().union(*(shingles(d) for d in training_docs))
for q in benchmark:
    s = shingles(q)
    overlap = len(s & train_shingles) / len(s)
    print(f"{overlap:4.0%} of 8-word windows seen in training -> {'CONTAMINATED' if overlap > 0.2 else 'clean'}: {q[:50]}...")
What you should see
 58% of 8-word windows seen in training -> CONTAMINATED: A train leaves the station at 3 pm travelling at 6...
  0% of 8-word windows seen in training -> clean: A cyclist sets off at 9 am at 15 km per hour; a se...

For your own evals the defense is the same: keep a private test set, refresh it from recent production traffic, and be suspicious of a model that scores far better on public benchmarks than on your cases.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "The model with the highest benchmark score is best for me."
    ✓ Shortlist with benchmarks; decide with your own evaluation set.
  • ✗ "Hallucination is a bug that will be fixed soon."
    ✓ It follows from how models generate; reduce it with grounding, checks and permission to abstain.
  • ✗ "BLEU and ROUGE measure answer quality."
    ✓ They measure word overlap; correct answers in different words score low.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Benchmarks are a filter

Not on that alone: benchmarks may be contaminated, may not match the task, and 2 points can be noise. Shortlist with benchmarks, decide with your own eval set, cost and latency.

SolutionB1 · Overlap is not truth

A summary can share words with the reference and still state wrong facts, or be correct with different words. Add faithfulness checks and human or calibrated-judge review.

SolutionD1 · Eval set mismatch

Production questions are harder, rarer or outside the knowledge the eval covers, or the eval's judge misses certain errors. Sample production answers, label them, and update the eval.

SolutionM1 · Metrics with methods

Correctness against labeled answers, faithfulness with a calibrated judge comparing claims to sources, abstention rate on unanswerable questions. Report each with intervals and segments.

Check your understanding

Explain the mechanismHow do you evaluate an LLM for a specific application?

Worked answer. Build a task-specific eval set from real inputs with labels or rubrics, choose metrics that match the goal (exact match, rubric scores, faithfulness, abstention), use human labels to calibrate any LLM judge, report intervals and segments, and track cost and latency. Public benchmarks only help shortlist models.

A plausible wrong answer. "Use the leaderboard." It measures other tasks.

Follow-ups. How big should the eval set be?

Debug a failureAn LLM judge rates a new model higher, but humans prefer the old one. Why?

Worked answer. Judge biases: preference for longer answers, for its own model family, or for confident style. Check agreement with human labels, control for length, randomize order in pairwise comparisons, and use a different judge model.

A plausible wrong answer. "Humans are inconsistent." Check the judge first.

Follow-ups. How would you calibrate the judge?

Defend a designHuman evaluation or LLM-as-judge?

Worked answer. Humans for the core labeled set, rubrics and calibration; LLM judges for scale and fast iteration, with measured agreement. High-stakes decisions keep human review on samples.

A plausible wrong answer. "LLM judges are as good as humans." Sometimes close, but only measured agreement tells you.

Follow-ups. What agreement level would you require?
