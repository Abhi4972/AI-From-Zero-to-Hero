In plain words first
The idea in one line

Stop estimating value. Parameterize the policy directly, compute the gradient of expected return, step uphill. Williams (1992) wrote it in one theorem. It is why PPO, GRPO, and every LLM RL loop exist.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
Policy gradient "Train the policy directly" $\nabla J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[G \cdot \nabla \log \pi_\theta(a \mid s)]$; derived from the log-derivative trick.
REINFORCE "The original PG algorithm" Williams (1992); Monte Carlo returns multiplied by log-policy gradient.
Log-derivative trick "Score function estimator" $\nabla P(\tau;\theta) = P(\tau;\theta) \cdot \nabla \log P(\tau;\theta)$; makes gradients of expectations tractable.
Baseline "Variance reduction" Any $b(s)$ subtracted from $G$; unbiased because $\mathbb{E}[b \cdot \nabla \log \pi] = 0$.
Reward-to-go "Only future returns count" $G_t^{\text{from } t}$ instead of the full $G_0$; correct and lower-variance.
Entropy bonus "Encourage exploration" $+\beta \cdot H(\pi(\cdot \mid s))$ term keeps the policy from collapsing.
On-policy "Train on what you just saw" Gradient expectation is w.r.t. the current policy — cannot reuse old data directly.
Advantage "How much better than average" $A(s, a) = G(s, a) - V(s)$; the signed quantity REINFORCE-with-baseline multiplies.

The problem

In plain words first

Value-based methods learn how good each action is and then pick the best. Policy-gradient methods skip that step: the policy itself is a function with adjustable numbers that outputs probabilities for each action, and learning nudges those numbers so that actions which led to high reward become more likely. REINFORCE, the simplest version, plays an episode, then raises the probability of every action taken in proportion to the reward that followed it. It works with any kind of action, including continuous ones, and it is the ancestor of the methods used to fine-tune language models with reinforcement learning. Its weakness is noise: the estimated direction of improvement varies wildly from one batch of episodes to the next. This lesson runs the lesson's REINFORCE on the grid world (under a second, on a table of preferences), then measures that noise exactly, and with a real PyTorch policy on Gymnasium's CartPole, showing how baselines and reward-to-go cut it. No neural network is trained.

Q-learning and DQN parameterize the value function. You pick actions by argmax Q. That is fine for discrete actions and discrete states. It breaks when actions are continuous (which argmax over a 10-dimensional torque?) or when you want a stochastic policy (argmax is deterministic by construction).

Policy gradients parameterize the policy instead. πθ​(a∣s) is a neural net that outputs a distribution over actions. Sample from it to act. Compute the gradient of expected return with respect to θ. Step uphill. No argmax. No Bellman recursion. Just gradient ascent on J(θ)=Eπθ​​[G].

The REINFORCE theorem (Williams 1992) tells you this gradient is computable: ∇J(θ)=Eπ​[G⋅∇θ​logπθ​(a∣s)]. Run an episode. Compute the return. Multiply by ∇logπθ​(a∣s) at every step. Average. Gradient-ascent. Done.

The on-policy RL algorithms used for language models (PPO, GRPO, RLOO) are refinements of REINFORCE, and DPO is derived from the same KL-regularised objective. Understanding it in your fingers is the prerequisite for the rest of this phase, and for Phase 10 · 07 (RLHF implementation) and Phase 10 · 08 (DPO).

The idea

The policy gradient theorem. For any policy πθ​ parameterized by θ:

∇J(θ)=Eτ∼πθ​​[t=0∑T​Gt​⋅∇θ​logπθ​(at​∣st​)]

where Gt​=∑k=tT​γk−trk+1​ is the discounted return from step t. The expectation is over full trajectories τ sampled from πθ​.

The proof is short. Differentiate J(θ)=∑τ​P(τ;θ)G(τ) under the expectation. Use ∇P(τ;θ)=P(τ;θ)∇logP(τ;θ) (the log-derivative trick). Factor logP(τ;θ)=∑logπθ​(at​∣st​)+environment terms that do not depend on θ. The environment terms vanish. Two lines of algebra give you the theorem.

Variance reduction tricks. Vanilla REINFORCE has murderous variance — returns are noisy, ∇logπ is noisy, their product is very noisy. Two standard fixes:

  1. Baseline subtraction. Replace Gt​ with Gt​−b(st​) for any baseline b(st​) that does not depend on at​. Unbiased because E[b(st​)⋅∇logπ(at​∣st​)]=0. Typical choice: b(st​)=V^(st​) learned by a critic → actor-critic (Lesson 9.7).
  2. Reward-to-go. Replace ∑t​Gt​⋅∇logπθ​(at​∣st​) with ∑t​Gtfrom t​⋅∇logπθ​(at​∣st​). Only future returns matter for a given action — past rewards contribute zero-mean noise.

Combined, you get:

∇J≈(1/N)i=1∑N​t=0∑Ti​​[Gt(i)​−V^(st(i)​)]⋅∇θ​logπθ​(at(i)​∣st(i)​)

which is REINFORCE with a baseline — the direct ancestor of A2C (Lesson 9.7) and PPO (Lesson 9.8).

Softmax policy parameterization. For discrete actions, the standard choice:

πθ​(a∣s)=exp(fθ​(s,a))/a′∑​exp(fθ​(s,a′))

where fθ​ is any neural net that outputs a score per action. The gradient has a clean form:

∇θ​logπθ​(a∣s)=∇θ​fθ​(s,a)−a′∑​πθ​(a′∣s)∇θ​fθ​(s,a′)

i.e., score of the taken action minus its expected value under the policy.

Gaussian policy for continuous actions. πθ​(a∣s)=N(μθ​(s),σθ​(s)). ∇logN(a;μ,σ) has a closed form. That is all Phase 9 · 07's SAC needs.

The same idea as a formula

Objective. J(θ)=Eτ∼πθ​​[G(τ)], the expected return of trajectories τ produced by the policy.

Log-derivative trick. ∇θ​P(τ;θ)=P(τ;θ)∇θ​logP(τ;θ), and since the environment's probabilities do not depend on θ, ∇θ​logP(τ;θ)=∑t​∇θ​logπθ​(at​∣st​). Hence

∇θ​J=Eτ​[t∑​∇θ​logπθ​(at​∣st​)(Gt​−b(st​))]

with reward-to-go Gt​ (rewards before t cannot be caused by at​, so dropping them removes noise, not signal) and any baseline b(st​) that does not depend on at​.

Why a baseline costs nothing. Ea∼π​[∇θ​logπθ​(a∣s)]=∑a​∇θ​πθ​(a∣s)=∇θ​1=0, so subtracting b(s) leaves the expectation unchanged and only changes the variance. The variance-minimising baseline is close to Vπ(s).

Softmax score. For logits z=θ⊤x: ∇z​logπ(a∣s)=ea​−π(⋅∣s); for a one-step problem the exact gradient is ∂J/∂zk​=πk​(rˉk​−J).

Gaussian policy for continuous actions: ∇μ​logN(a;μ,σ2)=(a−μ)/σ2.

Worked example
One REINFORCE update by hand

Three actions with probabilities π=(0.5,0.3,0.2); the agent took action 1 (the second) and received return G=4; baseline b=3; learning rate 0.1; the logits are the parameters.

  • Score: e1​−π=(−0.5,0.7,−0.2).
  • Advantage: G−b=1.
  • Update: z←z+0.1×1×(−0.5,0.7,−0.2), i.e. (−0.05,+0.07,−0.02): the taken action's logit rises, the others fall in proportion to their probability.
  • Without the baseline the advantage would be 4: the same direction, four times the step. If every return were around 100, every action taken would be pushed up hard and the signal, which action was better, would be buried in noise (the run after Step 5).
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "REINFORCE needs a differentiable environment."
    ✓ Only the policy's log-probabilities are differentiated; the environment is sampled.
  • ✗ "A baseline biases the gradient."
    ✓ Its expected contribution is exactly zero; it only reduces variance, often enormously (the run after Step 5).
  • ✗ "Higher return always means a larger, better gradient."
    ✓ Adding a constant to all rewards leaves the true gradient unchanged but inflates the estimate's noise unless a baseline removes it.

Step 1 · softmax policy network

def policy_logits(theta, state_features):
    return [dot(theta[a], state_features) for a in range(N_ACTIONS)]

def softmax(logits):
    m = max(logits)
    exps = [exp(l - m) for l in logits]
    Z = sum(exps)
    return [e / Z for e in exps]

Use a linear policy (one weight vector per action) for a tabular env. For Atari, swap in a CNN and keep the softmax head.

Step 2 · sampling and log-probability

def sample_action(probs, rng):
    x = rng.random()
    cum = 0
    for a, p in enumerate(probs):
        cum += p
        if x <= cum:
            return a
    return len(probs) - 1

def log_prob(probs, a):
    return log(probs[a] + 1e-12)

Step 3 · rollout with log-probs captured

def rollout(theta, env, rng, gamma):
    trajectory = []
    s = env.reset()
    while not done:
        logits = policy_logits(theta, s)
        probs = softmax(logits)
        a = sample_action(probs, rng)
        s_next, r, done = env.step(s, a)
        trajectory.append((s, a, r, probs))
        s = s_next
    return trajectory

Step 4 · REINFORCE update

def reinforce_step(theta, trajectory, gamma, lr, baseline=0.0):
    returns = compute_returns(trajectory, gamma)
    for (s, a, _, probs), G in zip(trajectory, returns):
        advantage = G - baseline
        grad_log_pi_a = [-p for p in probs]
        grad_log_pi_a[a] += 1.0
        for i in range(N_ACTIONS):
            for j in range(len(s)):
                theta[i][j] += lr * advantage * grad_log_pi_a[i] * s[j]

The gradient ∇logπ(a∣s)=ea​−π(⋅∣s) (onehot of a minus probabilities) is the heart of softmax policy gradients. Burn it into muscle memory.

Baselines, measured exactly

A one-step problem with three actions and rewards near 100, where the exact gradient is known: 2,000 batches of 32 REINFORCE samples each, without a baseline and with the average reward as baseline. Nothing is updated.

import numpy as np

# A one-step problem where the exact policy gradient is known: 3 actions, softmax policy, noisy rewards.
theta = np.array([0.5, 0.0, -0.5]); pi = np.exp(theta) / np.exp(theta).sum()
mean_r = np.array([101.0, 100.0, 99.0])               # every reward sits around 100; differences are small
J = pi @ mean_r
exact = pi * (mean_r - J)                               # d/dtheta_k of sum_a pi_a r_a for a softmax policy
print("policy", pi.round(3), "| exact gradient", exact.round(4))
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
def estimate(n, baseline):
    a = rng.choice(3, size=n, p=pi); r = mean_r[a] + rng.normal(0, 1, n)
    grad_logpi = np.eye(3)[a] - pi                      # e_a - pi: the softmax score function
    return ((r - baseline)[:, None] * grad_logpi).mean(0)
for name, b in (("no baseline", 0.0), ("baseline = average reward", J)):
    est = np.array([estimate(32, b) for _ in range(2000)])
    err = np.linalg.norm(est - exact, axis=1)
    print(f"{name:<26}: batches of 32, mean estimate {est.mean(0).round(3)}, typical error {np.median(err):.2f} "
          f"(exact gradient's size {np.linalg.norm(exact):.2f})")

Reading this output

Both estimators are unbiased: averaged over 2,000 batches, the baseline version matches the exact gradient (0.344,−0.098,−0.246) to three decimals, and the no-baseline average is close but still visibly off even after 64,000 samples. Per batch the difference is dramatic: with rewards around 100, the typical error of a 32-sample estimate is 11.7, twenty-seven times the size of the gradient itself, so a single update points almost at random. Subtracting the average reward cuts the typical error to 0.14, about 80 times smaller, for free. Every action's log-probability gradient is multiplied by roughly 100 without a baseline; with one, it is multiplied by the small difference that actually distinguishes the actions.

Pitfalls

  • Exploding gradients. Returns can be huge. Always normalize G to ~N(0, 1) across the batch before multiplying by ∇logπ.
  • Entropy collapse. The policy converges to a near-deterministic action too early, stops exploring, gets stuck. Fix: add entropy bonus β⋅H(π(⋅∣s)) to the objective.
  • High variance. Vanilla REINFORCE needs thousands of episodes. A critic baseline (Lesson 9.7) or TRPO/PPO's trust region (Lesson 9.8) is the standard fix.
  • Sample inefficiency. On-policy means you throw away every transition after one update. Off-policy corrections via importance sampling bring back data, at the cost of variance (PPO's ratio is a clipped IS weight).
  • Non-stationary gradients. The same gradient from 100 episodes ago uses old π. On-policy methods update every few rollouts for this reason.
  • Credit assignment. Without reward-to-go, past rewards contribute noise. Always use reward-to-go.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

In 2026, REINFORCE is rarely run directly but its gradient formula is everywhere:

Use case Derived method
Continuous control PPO / SAC with Gaussian policy

When you read loss = -advantage * log_prob in a 2026 training script, that is REINFORCE with a baseline. Entire papers (GRPO, RLOO) are variance-reduction tricks on top of this one line.

See it drawn
Gradient noise with PyTorch on CartPole

A 226-parameter PyTorch policy (untrained) plays Gymnasium's CartPole; for 30 independent batches of 8 episodes, autograd computes the gradient of -(weight * log_prob).mean() with three weightings: the full episode return, the reward-to-go, and the reward-to-go minus its batch mean. No optimiser step is taken.

import numpy as np, torch, gymnasium as gym

# REINFORCE gradients for a small, untrained PyTorch policy on Gymnasium's CartPole (+1 per step the pole stays up).
torch.manual_seed(0)
policy = torch.nn.Sequential(torch.nn.Linear(4, 32), torch.nn.Tanh(), torch.nn.Linear(32, 2))
env = gym.make("CartPole-v1")
def batch(n_episodes, seed):
    logps, rtg, full = [], [], []
    for ep in range(n_episodes):
        obs, _ = env.reset(seed=seed + ep); done = False; lp, rew = [], []
        while not done:
            dist = torch.distributions.Categorical(logits=policy(torch.as_tensor(obs, dtype=torch.float32)))
            a = dist.sample(); lp.append(dist.log_prob(a))
            obs, r, te, tr, _ = env.step(int(a)); rew.append(r); done = te or tr
        g = np.cumsum(rew[::-1])[::-1].copy()            # reward-to-go (gamma = 1)
        logps += lp; rtg += list(g); full += [g[0]] * len(g)
    return torch.stack(logps), torch.tensor(rtg), torch.tensor(full)
def grad(weights, logps):
    policy.zero_grad()
    (-(weights.float() * logps).mean()).backward(retain_graph=True)     # loss = -advantage * log_prob
    return torch.cat([p.grad.flatten() for p in policy.parameters()]).clone()
results = {k: [] for k in ("full return", "reward-to-go", "reward-to-go - baseline")}
for b in range(30):
    logps, rtg, full = batch(8, seed=1000 * b)
    results["full return"].append(grad(full, logps))
    results["reward-to-go"].append(grad(rtg, logps))
    results["reward-to-go - baseline"].append(grad(rtg - rtg.mean(), logps))
print(f"policy: {sum(p.numel() for p in policy.parameters())} parameters; 30 batches of 8 episodes each "
      f"(episodes last {len(rtg) / 8:.0f} steps on average)")
for k, gs in results.items():
    G = torch.stack(gs); mean = G.mean(0)
    cos = torch.nn.functional.cosine_similarity(G, mean[None].expand_as(G)).mean()
    print(f"{k:<24}: gradient size {G.norm(dim=1).mean():7.2f}, spread across batches {(G - mean).norm(dim=1).mean():7.2f}, "
          f"agreement of one batch with the average (cosine) {cos:.2f}")

reward-to-go            : gradient size    1.44, spread across batches    1.15, agreement of one batch with the average (cosine) 0.63

Reading this output

The untrained policy keeps the pole up for about 18 steps per episode. With the full episode return as the weight, independent batches disagree badly: the spread across batches (2.60) is almost as large as the gradients themselves (2.72), and one batch's gradient points only loosely toward the average (cosine 0.27). Weighting each action by its reward-to-go removes the rewards that came before it, which it could not have caused: the spread falls to 1.15 and agreement rises to 0.63. Subtracting the batch mean as a baseline lowers the spread further (0.95, cosine 0.68). Fewer episodes are then needed per reliable update, which is the practical meaning of variance reduction.

Break it on purpose

Make the baseline depend on the action: use b(s,a)=Q(s,a) estimated from the same batch, so the advantage Gt​−b becomes tiny for every action actually taken. The variance drops dramatically. What has gone wrong with the gradient? (Solution B1.)

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • REINFORCE-style training of language models. RLOO (leave-one-out baselines) and GRPO (group-normalised rewards, used for DeepSeek-R1 and many open reasoning models) are REINFORCE with sampled baselines instead of a critic; TRL, verl and OpenRLHF implement them.
  • Policy-gradient defaults in control. PPO (Lesson 9.8) and SAC remain the standard algorithms in Stable-Baselines3, CleanRL and RLlib, and in robotics training on GPU simulators (Isaac Lab, MuJoCo Playground).
  • Variance tools. Advantage normalisation, reward-to-go, GAE (Lesson 9.7) and entropy bonuses are defaults in every modern implementation.

Practice problem · Discounted returns with episode ends

easy15 minimplement

REINFORCE scales the gradient of each action by the return that follows it. This return is the reward to go: the discounted sum of the rewards from that step to the end of the episode. A rollout buffer often holds several episodes in one array, with a done flag at the last step of each episode.

Task

Write three functions.

  1. returns_to_go(rewards, gamma) returns the reward to go of one episode. For an episode of length T, GT−1​=rT−1​ and Gt​=rt​+γGt+1​ for t<T−1.
  2. batch_returns(rewards, dones, gamma) returns the reward to go for a batch of episodes that are joined in one array. When dones[t] is True, step t is the last step of its episode, so Gt​=rt​. Otherwise Gt​=rt​+γGt+1​. The last step of the batch has no next step. Use Gt+1​=0 there, also when its done flag is False.
  3. normalize(returns) returns (G−μ)/(σ+10−8). Here μ is the mean and σ is the population standard deviation (ddof = 0). When the array has fewer than 2 values, return a copy of the values unchanged.

Input and output

Name Type Shape Meaning
rewards float array (T,) the reward at each step
dones bool array (T,) True at the last step of each episode
gamma float scalar the discount factor, from 0 to 1
returns float array (T,) returns to normalize

Each function returns a float array of shape (T,).

Constraints

  • 1 <= T <= 1000 and 0 <= gamma <= 1.
  • Do not change the input arrays.
  • Use numpy only.

Your code
Common mistakes the checks catch
  • Computes G = gamma * (r + G), so the reward of the current step is also discounted.
  • Sums the discounted rewards from the start of the episode, not from step t to the end.
  • Divides by the standard deviation without 1e-8, so equal returns give NaN.
  • Ignores the done flags, so the return of one episode includes rewards of the next episode.
  • Resets the return after it stores the done step, so the reset cuts the wrong episode.
  • Uses the sample standard deviation (ddof = 1), not the population standard deviation.
Hint 1

Go through the steps from the last step to the first step. The return of a step needs the return of the step after it.

Hint 2

Keep one running value G. At each step, set G to the reward plus gamma times G, and store it.

Hint 3

In the batch version, a done flag at step t means that no reward after t belongs to this episode. Set the running value to 0 before you add the reward of step t.

Hint 4

For normalization, np.std gives the population standard deviation by default. Add 1e-8 to it so that equal returns do not divide by 0.

Reference solution and notes
import numpy as np


def returns_to_go(rewards, gamma):
    rewards = np.asarray(rewards, dtype=float)
    out = np.zeros_like(rewards)
    G = 0.0
    for t in reversed(range(rewards.shape[0])):
        G = rewards[t] + gamma * G
        out[t] = G
    return out


def batch_returns(rewards, dones, gamma):
    rewards = np.asarray(rewards, dtype=float)
    dones = np.asarray(dones, dtype=bool)
    out = np.zeros_like(rewards)
    G = 0.0
    for t in reversed(range(rewards.shape[0])):
        if dones[t]:
            G = 0.0
        G = rewards[t] + gamma * G
        out[t] = G
    return out


def normalize(returns):
    returns = np.asarray(returns, dtype=float)
    if returns.shape[0] < 2:
        return returns.copy()
    mean = np.mean(returns)
    std = np.std(returns)
    return (returns - mean) / (std + 1e-8)

Why the backward pass works

The return obeys Gt​=rt​+γGt+1​. A pass from the end to the start computes every return in O(T) time. A sum for each step from scratch takes O(T2) time.

Why the reset goes before the add

At a done step the episode ends after the reward. The reward of the done step is part of its own return. The rewards after it belong to the next episode. So the running value goes to 0 first, and then the reward of the done step goes in. The mutant that resets after the store cuts the wrong episode.

Truncation

This problem treats the end of the batch as the end of an episode. A real rollout often stops in the middle of an episode. Then the sum misses the future rewards. The actor critic and PPO lessons fix this with a bootstrap value from the critic. The problem on generalized advantage estimation uses it.

Why normalize

Returns can be large and change scale during training. The lesson on REINFORCE scales them to mean 0 and standard deviation 1 across the batch before the gradient step. This keeps the step size stable. The same normalize returns in the actor critic and PPO lessons, where it acts on advantages.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · About 10

About 10: the typical error is 11.7 against a true gradient of size 0.43, so each 32-sample estimate is almost pure noise. Each sample's contribution is r×(ea​−π), and with r≈100 every sampled action is pushed up strongly; the useful information is in the differences of about 1 between actions, buried under the common offset of 100. Subtracting the average reward removes the offset: the error drops to 0.14 while the average estimate stays the same. This is why every practical policy-gradient method subtracts a baseline (a running mean, a learned value, or the group mean in GRPO).

SolutionB1 · A biased gradient

The zero-expectation argument requires the baseline not to depend on the action: Ea​[b(s)∇logπ(a∣s)]=b(s)Ea​[∇logπ]=0. With b(s,a)=Q(s,a) the term Ea​[Q(s,a)∇logπ(a∣s)] is not zero; it is the policy gradient itself. Subtracting it removes the signal along with the noise, and what remains is essentially sampling error around zero; the policy stops improving even though the numbers look calm. The legitimate version subtracts a state value, giving the advantage Q(s,a)−V(s), which is what actor-critic methods estimate (Lesson 9.7).
