In plain words first
The idea in one line

Video is not a stack of photos. A 5-second clip has causal ordering, action verbs, and event timing that an image model cannot represent. Video-LLaMA (Zhang et al., June 2023) shipped the first open video-LLM with audio-visual grounding. VideoChat and Video-LLaVA scaled the pattern. By 2025 Qwen2.5-VL's time-aligned M-RoPE, and Qwen3-VL's text timestamps, made second-level grounding a standard open-model skill. Each system solved temporal tokens differently — Q-former per clip, concat-pool per frame, time-aligned M-RoPE per token. This lesson reads the patterns, builds a unifo

The problem

In plain words first

A video model has to understand order and timing as well as what is in each frame: what happened before what, and at which second. Early video LLMs (Video-LLaMA, Video-LLaVA) squeezed a short, fixed number of frames into the language model and could only say "early in the clip". Newer models attach each frame's real time: Qwen2.5-VL through its position code, Qwen3-VL as written timestamps. They can then answer "at 4.2 seconds". Two practical questions decide quality and cost: which frames you sample, and how many tokens each frame gets. This lesson covers the architectures, frame sampling, per-frame pooling, benchmarks and answer formats. It checks a frame sampler that finds a brief event that uniform sampling misses, and scores grounding answers with temporal IoU. No model is trained.

A 1-minute video at 30 FPS is 1800 frames. At 196 visual tokens per frame (ViT-B at 224), that is 352k tokens — larger than any 2024-era LLM context.

Three reduction strategies exist:

  1. Subsample frames (1-8 FPS depending on content).
  2. Pool each frame's patch tokens aggressively (3x3 or 4x4 bilinear pool).
  3. Compress via a Q-former that takes a 16-frame clip and outputs 64 tokens.

Each trade-off is different. Subsampling loses temporal detail. Pooling loses spatial detail. Q-former loses both a little but saves tokens.

Temporal position encoding is the other axis: how does the model know frame 5 came before frame 6? Options include simple 1D temporal RoPE (Video-LLaMA), learned temporal embeddings (Video-LLaVA), and time-aligned M-RoPE (Qwen2.5-VL, full 3D).

Idea 1 · Video-LLaMA: Q-former per clip + audio branch

Video-LLaMA (2023) was the first open video-LLM. Architecture:

  • A short clip with a fixed, small number of frames (8 in its default setup), so long videos are heavily subsampled.
  • Per-frame ViT features -> Video Q-former that cross-attends over all 16 frames -> 32 learned queries -> LLM.
  • Parallel audio branch: waveform -> ImageBind audio encoder -> Audio Q-former -> 32 queries -> LLM.

Strength: audio-visual joint reasoning. Weakness: fixed clip length, no arbitrary time grounding.

Idea 2 · VideoChat and Video-LLaVA

VideoChat kept the Video-LLaMA idea but dropped audio and simplified. Video-LLaVA (Lin et al., 2023) trained a single visual encoder on both images and video frames ("alignment before projection"), giving a unified representation. Both are frozen-CLIP-encoder + MLP + LLM.

Neither handles long video. Both are 8-16 frame systems.

Idea 3 · Qwen2.5-VL and time-aligned M-RoPE

Qwen2.5-VL keeps Qwen2-VL's M-RoPE (Multimodal Rotary Position Embedding) and aligns its temporal axis with absolute time. Each patch token carries a (t, h, w) position where t follows the actual timestamp (not frame index). Qwen2.5-Omni (Lesson 12.20) later named its audio and video version of the same idea TMRoPE, Time-aligned Multimodal RoPE.

Key differences from simple temporal embedding:

  • Absolute time, not index. The model sees "at 4.2 seconds" not "at frame 15."
  • Per-token rotation, not per-clip. Each visual token rotates independently by its timestamp.
  • Compatible with dynamic FPS. If you sample at 2 FPS here and 4 FPS there, time-aligned M-RoPE handles the uneven spacing natively.

Time-aligned M-RoPE enables "at what second does the cat jump?" queries. The model can output "at 4.2 seconds." Video-LLaMA could only say "early in the clip."

Idea 4 · Frame sampling strategies

Uniform: sample N frames evenly over duration. Simple, loses motion peaks.

Motion-adaptive: sample more densely where frames change most, using optical flow or frame differencing (measured below). Note that Qwen2.5-VL's "dynamic FPS" is different: it trains on videos sampled at varying frame rates and encodes absolute time, so you can choose the rate per request; it does not pick frames by motion.

Event-driven: run a lightweight detector, sample more where action happens. Used by VideoAgent.

Keyframe + context: sample at shot boundaries + a few adjacent frames. Used for cinematic content.

Idea 5 · Pooling per frame

At 1 FPS and 576 tokens per frame, a 5-minute clip is 172,800 tokens. That overflows Qwen2.5-VL-72B's 128k context, so you must drop the frame rate or pool tokens per frame first.

3x3 bilinear pool reduces to 64 tokens per frame -> 19,200 tokens for 5 minutes. Sweet spot for most tasks.

Pool more aggressively (6x6 -> 16 tokens per frame) for agent workflows where spatial detail matters less.

Worked example
A 5-minute clip on a budget
  • 1 fps, 576 tokens per frame: 300×576=172,800 tokens, too many for most contexts.
  • 3 × 3 pooling: 576/9=64 per frame, 19,200 for 5 minutes. 6 × 6 pooling: 16 per frame, 4,800.
  • A 0.16 s event sampled uniformly at 16 frames over 10 s (one frame every 0.67 s) is caught only if a sample lands inside it: about a 1-in-4 chance.
  • Predicted [4.2, 4.4] s against labelled [4.20, 4.36] s: overlap 0.16, union 0.20, tIoU 0.80.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "More uniform frames always catch more events."
    ✓ A brief event between two samples is missed at any spacing wider than the event (measured below).
  • ✗ "Qwen2.5-VL picks frames by motion."
    ✓ Its dynamic FPS means training at varied sampling rates with absolute time; the sampling policy is yours.
  • ✗ "An answer that mentions the right second is correct."
    ✓ Grounding is scored by interval overlap; a point or a vague phrase scores zero at standard thresholds.

Finding a brief event: uniform against motion-weighted sampling

A synthetic 10-second clip at 25 fps (72 × 128 pixels): a ball rolls slowly, then jumps between 4.20 and 4.36 s. 8, 12 and 16 frames are picked uniformly and by frame differencing with a coverage floor, and the run reports which samples land inside the jump. Instant.

import numpy as np
# A 10-second synthetic clip at 25 fps: a ball rolls slowly, then jumps between 4.20 s and 4.36 s.
fps, T, H, W = 25, 250, 72, 128
frames = np.zeros((T, H, W), np.float32)
for i in range(T):
    t = i / fps
    x = int(10 + 8 * t)                                     # slow roll to the right
    y = 50 - (30 if 4.2 <= t < 4.36 else 0)                  # the jump
    frames[i, y - 4:y + 4, x - 4:x + 4] = 1.0

def uniform(n):
    return np.linspace(0, T - 1, n).round().astype(int)
def motion(n, floor=0.25):
    diff = np.r_[0, np.abs(np.diff(frames, axis=0)).mean(axis=(1, 2))]   # frame differencing
    w = diff + floor * diff.mean()                         # keep some coverage of quiet stretches
    cdf = np.cumsum(w) / w.sum()
    return np.unique(np.searchsorted(cdf, (np.arange(n) + 0.5) / n))
jump = lambda idx: [f"{i / fps:.2f} s" for i in idx if 4.2 <= i / fps < 4.36]

for n in [8, 12, 16]:
    u, m = uniform(n), motion(n)
    print(f"{n:>2} frames  uniform at " + ", ".join(f"{i / fps:.1f}" for i in u) + " s")
    print(f"{'':>10}frames inside the jump: {jump(u) or 'none'}")
    print(f"{n:>2} frames  motion-weighted, {len(m)} distinct, inside the jump: {jump(m) or 'none'}\n")
print("each sampled frame keeps its real timestamp (frame index / fps); that is what time-aligned positions encode")

Reading this output

Uniform sampling catches the jump only by luck. With 8 frames, one sample (4.28 s) happens to fall inside the 0.16-second jump. With 12 and with 16 frames the samples land at 3.6/4.5 and 4.0/4.6 s, on both sides of it: more frames, and the event is missed. Whether uniform sampling sees a brief event depends on where the grid falls, not on how many frames you use, until the spacing is shorter than the event.

Motion-weighted sampling finds it every time. Frame differencing gives large weights to the frames where the ball leaves and returns to the ground. Sampling proportionally to those weights puts a frame at 4.20 or 4.28 s with 8, 12 and 16 samples. The coverage floor keeps some frames in quiet stretches, so the rest of the clip is not ignored.

Timestamps come for free. Every chosen frame keeps its real time (index ÷ 25 fps). A model with time-aligned positions or text timestamps can then say "4.2 s", even though the frames are unevenly spaced. Without explicit time, uneven sampling would confuse a model that assumes evenly spaced frames.

Scoring grounding answers

Six possible model answers for "when does the ball jump?" (JSON, free text, a point, a vague phrase, a wrong interval) are parsed and scored by temporal IoU against [4.20, 4.36] s, with recall at three thresholds. Instant.

import json, re
# Scoring temporal grounding: parse the model's answer, then temporal IoU against the labelled interval.
def tiou(a, b):
    inter = max(0.0, min(a[1], b[1]) - max(a[0], b[0]))
    union = max(a[1], b[1]) - min(a[0], b[0])
    return inter / union if union > 0 else 0.0
def parse(answer):
    try:                                                   # structured JSON first
        d = json.loads(answer); return float(d["start"]), float(d["end"])
    except (ValueError, KeyError, TypeError):
        pass
    nums = [float(x) for x in re.findall(r"(\d+(?:\.\d+)?)\s*(?:s|sec|seconds)\b", answer)]
    if len(nums) >= 2: return nums[0], nums[1]
    if len(nums) == 1: return nums[0], nums[0]             # a point is a zero-length interval
    return None

gold = (4.20, 4.36)
answers = ['{"event": "jump", "start": 4.2, "end": 4.4}',
           '{"event": "jump", "start": 3.9, "end": 4.9}',
           "The ball jumps from 4.1 s to 4.3 s.",
           "The ball jumps at about 4 seconds.",
           "The ball jumps early in the clip.",
           '{"event": "jump", "start": 6.0, "end": 6.5}']
hits = {0.3: 0, 0.5: 0, 0.7: 0}
for a in answers:
    p = parse(a)
    s = tiou(p, gold) if p else 0.0
    for k in hits: hits[k] += s >= k
    print(f"  {a:<46} -> {str(p):<14} tIoU {s:.2f}")
print("\nrecall at tIoU thresholds over these 6 answers: " + ", ".join(f"R@{k} = {v}/6" for k, v in hits.items()))

Reading this output

Parsing comes first. JSON answers parse exactly. Free text needs a pattern ("4.1 s to 4.3 s"), a single time becomes a zero-length interval, and "early in the clip" cannot be parsed at all. Asking the model for JSON, as Qwen-VL models can do, removes most parsing failures.

tIoU rewards precision and punishes vagueness. [4.2, 4.4] overlaps the labelled [4.20, 4.36] s with tIoU 0.80. A generous [3.9, 4.9] contains the whole jump but scores only 0.16, because the union is large. [4.1, 4.3] gets 0.38; a point, a vague phrase and a wrong interval all get 0.

Report recall at several thresholds. Over these six answers, R@0.3 = 2/6, R@0.5 = 1/6 and R@0.7 = 1/6. Charades-STA-style benchmarks report R@0.5 and R@0.7 because a loose threshold flatters imprecise models.

Idea 6 · The four video benchmarks

  • VideoMME: comprehensive video understanding, short + medium + long.
  • TempCompass: fine-grained temporal reasoning, "before" / "after" questions.
  • EgoSchema: long-horizon first-person video.
  • Video-MMMU: multimodal multi-discipline video questions.

A full video-VLM evaluation hits all four. They stress different axes — TempCompass is all about ordering, EgoSchema is about 3+ minute reasoning, VideoMME spans durations.

Idea 7 · Grounding output formats

Output formats for temporal grounding:

  • Free text: "The cat jumps around the 4-second mark." Easy to parse but imprecise.
  • Structured JSON: {"event": "jump", "start": 4.1, "end": 4.3}. Qwen2.5-VL can localise events to the second and answer in text or JSON when asked.
  • Timestamp tokens in the sequence: Qwen3-VL writes each frame group's time into the input as text (<4.2 seconds>, Lesson 12.9), so the model reads and repeats times as ordinary tokens; other video models use special time tokens. Qwen2.5-VL instead encodes time in its M-RoPE position IDs and answers in text or JSON.

Whatever the format, parse it strictly and score it with temporal IoU against labelled intervals (measured below); a vague answer like "early in the clip" scores zero.

Idea 8 · 2026 best practice

For video VLMs in 2026:

  • Model: a native-resolution video VLM with explicit time (Qwen2.5-VL's time-aligned M-RoPE or Qwen3-VL's text timestamps).
  • Frame sampling: dynamic FPS (1-4 depending on motion) with max-frame cap.
  • Per-frame pooling: 3x3 bilinear.
  • Output: structured JSON with time + event fields.
  • Benchmarks: VideoMME + TempCompass for general; EgoSchema for long-horizon.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Time-aware video VLMs: Qwen2.5-VL (time-aligned M-RoPE) and Qwen3-VL (text timestamps, 256K context), InternVL3.5 and Gemini-class hosted models localise events to the second.
  • Benchmarks: Video-MME, TempCompass, EgoSchema, Video-MMMU and grounding sets like Charades-STA (scored with R@tIoU) remain the standard suite.
  • Token budgets per frame are explicit settings: fps, maximum frames, maximum pixels per frame and pooling or merging factors.
  • Laptop reality: video is token-heavy; on an 8 GB GPU use a 2B-class video VLM with low fps (0.5–1) and a small per-frame pixel cap, or process segments one at a time.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Video needs order and time, not just frames.
  • Early video LLMs used a few frames and no explicit time; current models attach real timestamps.
  • Budget tokens: fps × duration × tokens per frame; pool or merge per frame.
  • Sampling decides what the model can see: brief events need dense or motion-aware sampling.
  • Score grounding with temporal IoU on strictly parsed intervals.

Given a video task (action recognition, temporal grounding, summarization, monitoring, agent-workflow replay) and a deployment constraint (model context, latency budget, throughput), emit a frame sampling and output plan.

Produce:

1. Frame sampler pick. Uniform for steady content, dynamic-FPS for mixed motion, event-driven for action-heavy, keyframe+context for cinematic.
2. Per-frame pooling. 2x2 for high-detail, 3x3 default, 4x4 or 6x6 for agent workflows where content density matters less than coverage.
3. Temporal encoding. Time-aligned M-RoPE for Qwen2.5-VL-family (TMRoPE in Qwen2.5-Omni); learned temporal embedding for smaller models; no encoding for single-clip tasks.
4. Output format. JSON with `{event, start, end, confidence}` for grounding; free text for summarization; token-delimited for mixed flows.
5. Benchmark plan. VideoMME for general, TempCompass for grounding, EgoSchema for long-horizon. Specify expected accuracy tier.
6. Context / latency budget. Total tokens = duration * fps * tokens_per_frame. Warn if exceeds 40% of context.

Hard rejects:
- Proposing uniform sampling for action-heavy video. Loses peak events.
- Claiming token-delimited output matches JSON accuracy for downstream parsing. JSON is more robust.
- Recommending Video-LLaMA for any project starting in 2026. Older architectures no longer competitive.

Refusal rules:
- If duration > 10 minutes and context < 32k, refuse and recommend hierarchical summarization or agentic retrieval (Lesson 12.18).
- If target accuracy is frontier (within 2 points of Gemini 2.5 Pro on VideoMME), refuse open 7B models and require 32B+ or proprietary.
- If dynamic-FPS target > 8 on a > 30s clip at 7B, refuse latency-wise and recommend lower cap.

Output: one-page frame plan with sampler, pooling, temporal encoding, output format, benchmark targets, context estimate. End with arXiv 2502.13923 (Qwen2.5-VL) and 2306.02858 (Video-LLaMA) for comparison reading.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Only 8 frames, by luck; motion sampling every time

Only the 8-frame grid catches it, with one frame at 4.28 s. The 12- and 16-frame grids put samples just before and after the jump (3.6 and 4.5 s; 4.0 and 4.6 s) and miss it, though they use more frames. Motion-weighted sampling lands a frame inside the jump (4.20 or 4.28 s) at all three budgets, because the frames where motion changes get the most weight.

SolutionB1 · A loose metric hides imprecise answers

"Start within 2 seconds" ignores the end of the interval and allows large errors. On a 10-second clip, a ±2 s window covers 40% of the video, so even random guesses would often pass. A model that always answers "from 3 to 9 seconds" could score highly and still be useless for finding the moment. The number says little about precision, and it cannot be compared with published results, which use temporal IoU (for example R@0.5 and R@0.7 on Charades-STA). Score with tIoU on parsed intervals at several thresholds. Check how a trivial baseline scores (a fixed interval, or the middle of the clip), and look at errors in seconds by clip length.
