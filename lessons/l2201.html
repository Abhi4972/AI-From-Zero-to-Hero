Why this came about

Computers can only do arithmetic, so anything an AI system works with (a word, a photo, a customer, a support ticket) has to become numbers first. The most useful form turned out to be a vector, a fixed-length list of numbers, because geometry then gives meaning to arithmetic: things that are alike end up close together. From word vectors in 2013 to the "embeddingslater · 3.19" behind every RAGlater · 10.37 system today, how similar are these two vectors? is one of the most-asked questions in AI. This lesson gives you the two answers everyone uses: distance and cosine similarity.

In plain words first
Describing things with numbers

Describe snacks with two numbers: how sweet (0 to 1) and how salty (0 to 1). Masala chips are [0.1, 0.9], chocolate is [0.9, 0.1]. That pair of numbers is a vector. Draw it as an arrow from the corner of a graph to the point (0.1, 0.9), and similar snacks become arrows pointing in similar directions. Now imagine 768 numbers instead of 2, each capturing some aspect of meaning that a model learned. You cannot draw 768 directions, but the arithmetic is exactly the same, and that is what this lesson teaches.

Step 1 · A vector is a list of numbers

A vector is an ordered list of numbers that describes one thing. The example describes five snacks by three numbers each: how sweet, how salty and how spicy, from 0 to 1. Once things are lists of numbers, a computer can compare them with arithmetic, and that single idea is behind every embedding and every search in this course. Make the vectors first:

import numpy as np

# each snack described by [sweet, salty, spicy], from 0 to 1
snacks = {
    "masala chips":     np.array([0.1, 0.9, 0.6]),
    "salted peanuts":   np.array([0.0, 0.8, 0.1]),
    "namkeen mix":      np.array([0.2, 0.8, 0.7]),
    "chocolate":        np.array([0.9, 0.1, 0.0]),
    "chilli chocolate": np.array([0.7, 0.1, 0.6]),
    "gulab jamun":      np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0]),
}
chips = snacks["masala chips"]
print(chips, chips.shape, chips[1])
What you should see
[0.1 0.9 0.6] (3,) 0.9

Each number is one component (or dimension) of the vector; this vector has 3 dimensions: sweet, salty, spicy. The order matters and is fixed: component 1 is always "salty" for every snack. Everything that follows works the same whether a vector has 2, 3 or 3,072 components.

Step 2 · Adding and scaling vectors

Vectors add component by component, and multiplying by a number scales every component:

peanuts = snacks["salted peanuts"]
print(chips + peanuts)              # add matching components
print(2 * chips)                    # scale: twice as much of everything
mix = (chips + snacks["chocolate"]) / 2
print(mix.round(2))                 # the average: a sweet-and-salty "in between"
What you should see
[0.1 1.7 0.7]
[0.2 1.8 1.2]
[0.5 0.5 0.3]

As arrows, adding means "walk along the first arrow, then the second"; scaling means making an arrow longer or shorter without turning it. The average of two vectors lands halfway between them. A famous result from word vectors (Lesson 5.7) uses exactly this arithmetic: the vector for king minus man plus woman lands near queen.

Step 3 · Length and distance

The length (also called the norm or magnitude) of a vector comes from Pythagoras: square each component, add them up, take the square root. The distance between two vectors is the length of their difference.

print(np.sqrt((chips ** 2).sum()).round(3), np.linalg.norm(chips).round(3))   # the same thing, two ways

def distance(a, b):
    return np.linalg.norm(a - b)

for name in ["salted peanuts", "namkeen mix", "chocolate"]:
    print(f"{name:<15} distance from masala chips: {distance(chips, snacks[name]):.3f}")
What you should see
1.086 1.086
salted peanuts  distance from masala chips: 0.520
namkeen mix     distance from masala chips: 0.173
chocolate       distance from masala chips: 1.281

Namkeen mix is closest to masala chips, chocolate is far away: distance matches our sense of "alike". This Euclidean distance (straight-line distance) is one way to measure similarity. It has a weakness, though: it cares about how much as well as what kind. Step 5 shows when that matters.

The same idea as a formula

For vectors a and b with n components:

  • length: |a| = √(a₁² + a₂² + … + aₙ²)
  • distance: |a − b| = √((a₁ − b₁)² + … + (aₙ − bₙ)²)

Step 4 · The dot product

The dot product multiplies matching components and adds the results. It is the single most important operation in this course: neural network layers (Lesson 3.4) and attention (Lesson 7.3) are built from it.

print(round(chips @ peanuts, 3))                # @ is the dot product for 1-D arrays
print(round((chips * peanuts).sum(), 3))        # the same, spelled out: multiply pairs, then add
print(round(0.1 * 0.0 + 0.9 * 0.8 + 0.6 * 0.1, 3))   # the same, by hand
print(round(chips @ snacks["chocolate"], 3))
What you should see
0.78
0.78
0.78
0.18

(The code rounds to 3 decimals because, as Lesson 0.9 showed, decimal arithmetic leaves tiny errors such as 0.7800000000000001.) The dot product is large when the two vectors have big values in the same components (both salty), and small when their big values are in different places (one salty, one sweet). Geometrically, it combines the two lengths with the angle between the arrows. Drag the arrows to feel it:

Things to try in the figure: point both arrows the same way (large positive dot product); make them perpendicular (dot product exactly 0); point them in opposite directions (negative); keep the angle fixed but make one arrow longer (the dot product grows even though the direction did not change).

The same idea as a formula

a · b = a₁b₁ + a₂b₂ + … + aₙbₙ = |a| |b| cos θ, where θ is the angle between the arrows.

Step 5 · Cosine similarity: direction, not size

The last experiment shows the dot product's weakness as a similarity measure: longer arrows get bigger scores. Often you care only about direction, the kind of thing, not the amount. Divide the dot product by both lengths and you get the cosine of the angle between the vectors: cosine similarity, always between −1 and 1.

def cosine(a, b):
    return (a @ b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b))

mild_chips = np.array([0.05, 0.45, 0.3])        # same taste profile as masala chips, half as intense
print(f"distance {distance(chips, mild_chips):.3f}   cosine {cosine(chips, mild_chips):.3f}")
print(f"distance {distance(chips, peanuts):.3f}   cosine {cosine(chips, peanuts):.3f}")
What you should see
distance 0.543   cosine 1.000
distance 0.520   cosine 0.891
Reading this output
  • Mild chips is the same snack profile at half strength. By distance it looks further from masala chips than peanuts are, because the arrow is shorter. By cosine it is a perfect match, 1.000: same direction.
  • Cosine 1 means "same direction", 0 means "unrelated" (perpendicular), −1 means "opposite".
  • When the lengths are not meaningful (a long document versus a short one about the same topic), cosine is the better measure. This is why text search with embeddings almost always uses cosine similarity (the Retrieval (RAG) section).

Step 6 · Ranking by similarity: vector search in miniature

Put it together: given a query vector, score every item and sort. This is, in essence, what a vector database does for millions of items (Lesson 14.26).

def most_similar(query, items, k=3):
    scores = {name: cosine(query, vec) for name, vec in items.items()}
    return sorted(scores.items(), key=lambda kv: kv[1], reverse=True)[:k]

craving = np.array([0.6, 0.2, 0.7])             # "something sweet but spicy"
for name, score in most_similar(craving, snacks):
    print(f"{score:.3f}  {name}")
What you should see
0.983  chilli chocolate
0.755  namkeen mix
0.656  chocolate

A common speed-up: normalize every vector to length 1 once, when you store it. Then cosine similarity is just the dot product, and one matrix multiplication (Lesson 1.5) scores a query against every item at once:

names = list(snacks)
M = np.array([snacks[n] for n in names])                       # shape (6, 3): one row per snack
M_unit = M / np.linalg.norm(M, axis=1, keepdims=True)           # every row now has length 1
q_unit = craving / np.linalg.norm(craving)
scores = M_unit @ q_unit                                        # 6 cosines in one operation
print(scores.round(3))
print(names[int(scores.argmax())])
What you should see
[0.644 0.302 0.755 0.656 0.983 0.636]
chilli chocolate

keepdims=True keeps the lengths as a column of shape (6, 1), so broadcasting (Lesson 0.14) divides each row by its own length.

Step 7 · Many dimensions

Real embeddings have hundreds or thousands of components. You cannot picture 768 directions, but every formula above is unchanged. One surprising fact about high dimensions is worth knowing: two random vectors are almost always nearly perpendicular.

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
for dims in [2, 10, 768]:
    a, b = rng.normal(size=(2, 1000, dims))                    # 1000 random pairs
    cos = (a * b).sum(axis=1) / (np.linalg.norm(a, axis=1) * np.linalg.norm(b, axis=1))
    print(f"{dims:>4} dimensions: typical |cosine| between random vectors = {np.abs(cos).mean():.3f}")
What you should see
   2 dimensions: typical |cosine| between random vectors = 0.644
  10 dimensions: typical |cosine| between random vectors = 0.244
 768 dimensions: typical |cosine| between random vectors = 0.029

In 2 dimensions random arrows often point roughly the same way; in 768 dimensions they almost never do. So when two embeddings have a cosine of 0.6, that is a strong signal: it did not happen by chance. It also means similarity scores from different embedding modelslater · 7.13 are not comparable with each other: what counts as "high" depends on the model, a point the Retrieval (RAG) section returns to.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "A vector is an arrow."
    ✓ It is a list of numbers; an arrow is one way to picture it, useful in 2 or 3 dimensions.
  • ✗ "A bigger dot product always means more similar."
    ✓ It also grows with length. Cosine similarity removes length and compares direction only.
  • ✗ "Cosine similarity of 0 means the opposite."
    ✓ 0 means unrelated (perpendicular); −1 means opposite.
  • ✗ "High-dimensional math needs different formulas."
    ✓ Length, distance, dot product and cosine are the same formulas with more terms.

Exercises

Predict before you run

Without running code: what is the dot product of [1, 2, 3] and [4, 0, -1]? And the cosine similarity of [2, 0] and [0, 5]? (Solution P1.)

Make it your own

Add two snacks of your own to snacks (for example pani puri and kaju katli), choose their three numbers, and run most_similar with each as the query. Do the neighbours make sense? Which number would you add as a fourth dimension to separate snacks that still look too similar? (Solution M1.)

Debugging exercise

A colleague computes M @ q with M of shape (6, 3) and gets correct-looking scores, but the ranking favours snacks with large numbers in every component. What did they forget? (Solution D1.)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · By hand

Dot product: 1×4 + 2×0 + 3×(−1) = 4 + 0 − 3 = 1. Cosine of [2, 0] and [0, 5]: the dot product is 0 (one points along the first axis, the other along the second), so the cosine is 0: perpendicular, whatever their lengths.

SolutionM1 · Your own snacks

Any reasonable numbers work, for example pani puri = [0.3, 0.5, 0.8] (its nearest neighbour is likely namkeen mix) and kaju katli = [0.9, 0.1, 0.0] (chocolate and gulab jamun). Three hand-picked numbers can only separate snacks along three directions; a fourth such as crunchy would split chips from gulab jamun-like sweets more finely. Learned embeddings solve this by letting a model choose hundreds of such directions from data instead of a person choosing three (Lesson 5.7).

SolutionD1 · Not normalized

They used raw vectors, so M @ q is the dot product, which grows with each row's length. Normalize the rows of M (and the query) to length 1 first, or divide each score by np.linalg.norm(M, axis=1) * np.linalg.norm(q). Then the scores are cosines and compare direction only.

Recap
  • A vector is a fixed-length list of numbers; each position always means the same thing.
  • Vectors add component by component and scale by multiplying every component.
  • Length uses Pythagoras; distance is the length of the difference.
  • The dot product multiplies matching components and adds them; it combines lengths and angle.
  • Cosine similarity divides out the lengths and compares direction only; with unit-length vectors it is just the dot product, and ranking by it is vector searchlater · 14.26.

Check your understanding

Q1Two document embeddings point in exactly the same direction, but one is three times longer. What is their cosine similarity?

Cosine ignores length; same direction gives 1.

Q2What is the dot product of [1, 0] and [0, 1]?

They are perpendicular: 1×0 + 0×1 = 0.

Q3Why do vector search systems often normalize vectors to length 1 when storing them?

With unit vectors, a single matrix multiplication gives every cosine.
