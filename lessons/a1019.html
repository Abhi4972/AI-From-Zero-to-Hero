In plain words first
The idea in one line

DeepSeek-V3 was trained on 2,048 H800 GPUs with MoE experts scattered across nodes. Cross-node expert all-to-all communication cost 1 GPU-hour of comm for every 1 GPU-hour of compute. GPUs were idle half the time. DualPipe (DeepSeek, Dec 2024) is a bidirectional pipeline that overlaps forward and backward computation with the all-to-all comms they trigger. Bubbles drop, throughput climbs, and the keeping of two model-parameter copies (the "dual" that gives the name) is cheap once Expert Parallelism is already spreading experts across ranks anyway. The problem

In plain words first

A model too big for one GPU can be cut into consecutive slices (pipeline stages), one per GPU, with the batch split into small micro-batches that flow through the GPUs like cars on an assembly line. At the start and end of every step some GPUs sit idle waiting for work: the pipeline bubble. DeepSeek-V3 had a second problem: its mixture-of-experts layers send every token across the network to the GPUs holding its experts, and that communication took about as long as the computation itself. DualPipe feeds micro-batches in from both ends of the pipeline and pairs each forward pass with another micro-batch's backward pass, so that one's computation hides the other's communication. This lesson computes the published bubble formulas, checks the 1:1 communication claim, and runs PyTorch's real DualPipeV schedule on four CPU processes. No model is trained.

Training a 671B MoE model on 2k H800 GPUs runs into three compounding bottlenecks:

  1. Memory pressure. Each GPU holds a slice of the model. Activation memory for 4K-token sequences across 61 layers with 128 attention heads is enormous.
  2. Pipeline bubbles. Traditional pipeline parallelism (GPipe, 1F1B) leaves GPUs idle while they wait for their stage's input or gradient. At 8 stages, roughly 12% of GPU time can be bubble even with 1F1B scheduling.
  3. Cross-node all-to-all. MoE with expert parallelism scatters experts across nodes. Every forward pass triggers an all-to-all to dispatch tokens to their experts, and another to combine. At 2k GPUs this easily becomes a 1:1 compute-to-comm ratio.

Each of these has separate solutions: gradient checkpointing for memory, Zero Bubble (Sea AI Lab, 2023) for pipeline bubbles, expert-parallel comm kernels for all-to-all. What DualPipe does is make them play together. The schedule overlaps compute and comm within a single forward-backward chunk, injects micro-batches from both ends of the pipeline simultaneously, and uses the resulting schedule to hide all-to-all inside the compute windows.

Reported result: fewer pipeline bubbles than 1F1B and Zero Bubble, and the all-to-all communication almost fully hidden behind computation. DeepSeek-V3's training took 2.788M H800 GPU-hours in total; the report gives no overall utilisation figure.

Idea 1 · Pipeline parallelism refresher

Split an N-layer model across P devices. Device i holds layers i * N/P .. (i+1) * N/P - 1. A micro-batch flows forward through devices 0 to P-1, then backward from P-1 to 0. Each device can only start its forward stage when the prior device sends its output and can only start backward when the downstream device sends the upstream gradient.

GPipe (Huang et al., 2019) schedules one micro-batch at a time, which wastes most GPU time. 1F1B (Narayanan et al., 2021) interleaves forward and backward passes for multiple micro-batches. Zero Bubble (Qi et al., 2023) splits the backward pass into two parts — backward-for-input (B) and backward-for-weights (W) — and schedules them to fill the bubble. After Zero Bubble, the pipeline is almost tight.

DualPipe is the next step. It adds two ideas on top:

Idea 2 · Idea 1: chunk decomposition

Each forward chunk is split into four components:

  • Attention. Q/K/V projections, attention, output projection.
  • All-to-all dispatch. Cross-node communication that sends tokens to their experts.
  • MLP. The MoE expert computation.
  • All-to-all combine. Cross-node communication that brings expert outputs back.

A backward chunk adds gradient versions of each of these. DualPipe schedules them so that all-to-all dispatch happens in parallel with the attention compute of the next chunk, and all-to-all combine happens in parallel with the MLP compute of the following chunk.

Idea 3 · Idea 2: bidirectional scheduling

Most pipeline schedules inject micro-batches from stage 0 and flow toward stage P-1. DualPipe injects micro-batches from BOTH ends. Stage 0 sees forward micro-batches originating there; stage P-1 sees forward micro-batches originating there too. The two streams meet in the middle.

For this to work, device i must hold BOTH the early-pipeline layer i AND the late-pipeline layer P - 1 - i. That is the "dual" part of DualPipe: each device keeps two copies of the model layers it needs to serve (one for each direction). At DeepSeek-V3's scale, this is a 2x parameter replication cost. It is affordable because Expert Parallelism already spreads the MoE experts so thin that replicating the non-expert layers twice is small potatoes.

Crucially, the forward stream in one direction and the backward stream in the other direction overlap exactly where the bubbles would be in a single-direction schedule. The bubbles vanish.

Idea 4 · A hand-traced schedule

Consider P = 4 ranks, 8 micro-batches, divided 4 forward / 4 reverse. Time moves left to right; rows are device ranks.

           Time →
rank 0:  F1 F2 F3 F4  F5R F6R F7R F8R  B1 B2 B3 B4  ...
rank 1:     F1 F2 F3  F4/F5R F6R F7R   B1 B2 ...
rank 2:        F1 F2  F3/F5R F4/F6R    B1 ...
rank 3:           F1  F2/F5R F3/F6R    ...

Reading the "F4/F5R" notation: rank 1 is running forward of micro-batch 4 (going left-to-right in the pipeline) AND forward of micro-batch 5 (going right-to-left) in the same time slot. That is what "bidirectional" means operationally.

At rank 2 the cross streams overlap sooner, at rank 0 and P-1 they overlap latest. In the stable middle phase of the schedule, every rank runs forward-of-X-direction overlapped with backward-of-Y-direction. Compute is busy. All-to-all dispatches for the forward pass hide inside backward compute. All-to-all combines hide inside forward compute. The bubbles are squeezed out.

Idea 5 · Bubble accounting

Standard 1F1B pipeline bubble (time wasted per rank):

bubble_1F1B = (P - 1) * forward_chunk_time

Zero Bubble refinement brings it down but not to zero. DualPipe needs the number of stages and the number of micro-batches to be even (but, unlike the earlier bidirectional schedule Chimera, not the micro-batches to be divisible by the stages). Its bubble is (PP/2 − 1)(F&B + B − 3W), against (PP − 1)(F + B) for 1F1B and (PP − 1)(F + B − 2W) for Zero Bubble, where F&B is a forward and a backward chunk executed together. Outside the stable phase (warmup and cooldown), there is some bubble but it does not grow with the number of micro-batches — a key property the paper highlights.

In marketing terms: "bubble-free". In technical terms: a smaller fixed bubble that, like 1F1B's and Zero Bubble's, does not grow with the micro-batch count, plus all-to-all communication hidden inside computation. Sea AI Lab's follow-up (Cut-in-half) notes that part of DualPipe's bubble comes from its specialisation for Expert Parallelism: without the EP overlap, the same V-shape becomes the zero-bubble ZB-V schedule.

Idea 6 · DualPipeV — the refinement

Sea AI Lab (2025) observed that the second parameter copy is not needed at all. Cutting DualPipe's schedule in half gives a V-shape schedule on half as many devices: each device holds two different stages (one on the way down the pipeline, one on the way back), so no parameter is duplicated, at the cost of twice the pipeline communication, which is small next to EP communication. The bubble is about the same, ((PP − 1)/2)(F&B + B − 3W) with PP now counting the devices after cutting. DeepSeek added it to the open-source DualPipe repository as DualPipeV, and PyTorch ships it as ScheduleDualPipeV (run after Use it).

The tradeoff:

Feature DualPipe DualPipeV 1F1B Zero Bubble
Param copies per device 2 1 1 1
Bubble (fixed, independent of micro-batch count) (PP/2−1)(F&B+B−3W) ((PP−1)/2)(F&B+B−3W), PP = devices (PP−1)(F+B) (PP−1)(F+B−2W)
Compute-comm overlap full partial minimal partial
Use when EP-heavy MoE dense or EP-light baseline any pipeline

Idea 7 · What it means for a 14.8T-token run

DeepSeek-V3's pre-training consumed 14.8T tokens on 2,048 H800 GPUs in 2.664M GPU-hours (2.788M including context extension and post-training). With 1F1B at 16 stages, the bubble would take 10% of each step with 128 micro-batches and more with fewer: hundreds of thousands of GPU-hours, the compute of several 7B models trained on 2T tokens (computed after Use it). The report does not quantify DualPipe's saving; its main gain is hiding the all-to-all communication, which otherwise would have cost about as much time as the computation.

For smaller runs (under 1k GPUs), DualPipe is overkill — pipeline bubbles are smaller relative to total cost, and dense-model training rarely hits the all-to-all bottleneck. For frontier MoE training at multi-thousand GPU scale, it is effectively required.

Idea 8 · Where it sits in the stack

  • Complementary to FSDP (Phase 10 · 05). FSDP shards the model parameters across ranks; DualPipe schedules the compute across ranks. They combine.
  • Compatible with ZeRO-3 gradient sharding. The bookkeeping for the two-copy replication needs to cooperate with ZeRO's sharded gradients.
  • Requires custom all-to-all kernels tuned for the specific cluster topology. DeepSeek's open-source kernels are the reference implementation.

The same idea as a formula

Let F be the time of one forward chunk on one stage, B of one full backward chunk, W of its weight-gradient part (B minus the input-gradient part), and F&B of a forward and a backward chunk of two micro-batches executed together. With P pipeline stages:

Schedule Bubble per step Parameters per device Activations (micro-batches)
1F1B (P−1)(F+B) 1× P
ZB1P (P−1)(F+B−2W) 1× P
DualPipe (P/2−1)(F&B+B−3W) 2× P+1
DualPipeV (d devices) 2d−1​(F&B+B−3W) 1× d+21​

Share of a step with M micro-batches: bubble+M(F+B)bubble​. The bubble is fixed, so the share falls as M grows.

Communication to hide, per token and MoE layer: bytes≈nnodes​h(1+2) for an FP8 dispatch and BF16 combine; it is hidden when F&B≤F+B leaves room for it.

Worked example
16 stages, F = 1, B = 2, W = 1
  • 1F1B: 15×(1+2)=45 units of idle time per step.
  • ZB1P: 15×(1+2−2)=15: weight gradients fill most of the bubble.
  • DualPipe with F&B=F+B=3 (no gain from overlapping): 7×(3+2−3)=14.
  • With M=64 micro-batches a step has 64×3=192 units of work, so the shares are 45/237=19%, 15/207=7.2% and 14/206=6.8%.
  • With the same assumption DualPipe barely beats Zero Bubble: its real gain is that F&B also hides the all-to-all communication, which would otherwise add about as much time as the computation.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "1F1B's bubble grows with the number of micro-batches; DualPipe's does not."
    ✓ Every schedule here has a fixed bubble; adding micro-batches shrinks its share for all of them.
  • ✗ "DualPipe's 'dual' is a second copy of the whole model."
    ✓ Each device keeps two copies of its own stage's parameters; with large expert parallelism that is affordable, and DualPipeV removes even that.
  • ✗ "Pipeline schedules change the result."
    ✓ They only reorder work: the run after Use it gets the single-process loss and gradients from all three schedules.
Predict before you run

PyTorch's ScheduleDualPipeV runs an 8-stage model on 4 processes. On rank 0, which holds the first and the last stage, will its very first actions be forward passes of stage 0, of stage 7, or a mix? And will the three schedules produce exactly the same gradients? (Solution P1.)

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

code/main.py is a pipeline schedule simulator. It takes (P, n_micro_batches, schedule) and prints the stable-phase utilization for each of 1F1B, Zero Bubble, DualPipe, and DualPipeV. It is a teaching tool — the numbers match the qualitative claims in the papers, they are not a claim about production measured speedup.

The simulator's value: run it with different P and micro-batch counts and watch the bubble fraction shrink for every schedule as micro-batches are added, with DualPipe starting lower.

Integration considerations for a real training run:

  • Pick a pipeline-parallel depth that divides cleanly into your micro-batch count.
  • Ensure your expert-parallel mesh supports bidirectional all-to-all. DeepSeek's kernels are the reference.
  • Expect to burn a week of debugging time on the schedule itself the first time. The bookkeeping is fiddly.
  • Monitor GPU utilization per rank, not just aggregate. DualPipe's benefit comes from tightening the stragglers.

Reading this output

The simulator does not schedule anything; each schedule is a one-line formula with made-up costs. The 1F1B line is the standard result, (P−1)/(M+P−1): 30.4% at 8 stages and 16 micro-batches. The others are guesses: Zero Bubble's and DualPipe's formulas are rough, and DualPipeV is simply DualPipe times 1.2, a number not taken from any source (Sea AI Lab's analysis gives it about the same bubble on half the devices, with no duplicated parameters; the next run uses the published formulas).

Step 2 also contradicts the lesson's earlier claim: the bubble fraction shrinks with more micro-batches for every schedule, 1F1B included (30% at 16, 5% at 128), because every bubble here is a fixed cost per step. What grows with pipeline depth (Step 3) is the bubble, for all of them.

Step 4's "roughly the cost of a full 70B dense pre-training run" is wrong by a factor of about two: 245K H800 GPU-hours is closer to four 7B models on 2T tokens (next run, part C).

Reading this output

A. With the published formulas at 16 stages, 1F1B idles for 45 units per step, Zero Bubble for 15 and DualPipe for 14 (with the cautious assumption that running a forward and a backward together is no faster than running them in turn). At 64 micro-batches that is 19%, 7.2% and 6.8% of the step. B. Without overlap, a step would take about twice as long; this, not the bubble, is what DualPipe's paired forward and backward chunks are for: one micro-batch computes while another's tokens travel.

PyTorch's real schedules on four processes

torch.distributed.pipelining with four CPU processes (gloo backend): one training step of an 8-block MLP with 8 micro-batches under ScheduleGPipe, Schedule1F1B (4 stages, one per process) and ScheduleDualPipeV (8 stages, two per process in a V). Loss and gradients are compared with the same model in one process, and DualPipeV's computed schedule is printed. About 10 seconds.

import subprocess, sys, textwrap
from pathlib import Path

# A real pipeline run: 4 processes on this CPU (gloo), PyTorch's own schedules (torch.distributed.pipelining).
# One step of an 8-block MLP; gradients checked against the same model in one process. No training.
Path("pp_demo.py").write_text(textwrap.dedent('''
    import torch, torch.distributed as dist, torch.nn as nn
    from torch.distributed.pipelining import PipelineStage, ScheduleGPipe, Schedule1F1B, ScheduleDualPipeV
    from torch.distributed.pipelining.schedules import _format_pipeline_order
    dist.init_process_group("gloo"); rank, P = dist.get_rank(), dist.get_world_size()
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    blocks = [nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(64, 64), nn.GELU()) for _ in range(8)]           # the whole model, on every rank
    X, Y, M = torch.randn(32, 64), torch.randn(32, 64), 8                             # one batch in 8 micro-batches
    loss_fn = nn.MSELoss()
    ref = nn.Sequential(*[nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(64, 64), nn.GELU()) for _ in range(8)])
    ref.load_state_dict(nn.Sequential(*blocks).state_dict())
    loss_ref = sum(loss_fn(ref(X[i * 4:(i + 1) * 4]), Y[i * 4:(i + 1) * 4]) for i in range(M)) / M
    loss_ref.backward()
    ref_grads = {id(b): [p.grad for p in r.parameters()] for b, r in zip(blocks, ref)}
    def piece(ids):                                                                    # a stage = some of the blocks
        m = nn.Sequential(*[blocks[i] for i in ids]); return m
    for name in ["GPipe", "1F1B", "DualPipeV"]:
        for b in blocks: b.zero_grad()
        if name == "DualPipeV":                       # 8 stages of 1 block; rank r holds stages r and 7-r (a V)
            own = [rank, 2 * P - 1 - rank]
            stages = [PipelineStage(piece([s]), s, 2 * P, torch.device("cpu")) for s in own]
            sched = ScheduleDualPipeV(stages, n_microbatches=M, loss_fn=loss_fn)
        else:                                         # 4 stages of 2 blocks; rank r holds stage r
            own = [rank]
            stage = PipelineStage(piece([2 * rank, 2 * rank + 1]), rank, P, torch.device("cpu"))
            sched = (ScheduleGPipe if name == "GPipe" else Schedule1F1B)(stage, n_microbatches=M, loss_fn=loss_fn)
        losses = []
        first, last = (0 in own), ((2 * P - 1 if name == "DualPipeV" else P - 1) in own)
        args = (X,) if first else ()
        kw = dict(target=Y, losses=losses) if last else {}
        sched.step(*args, **kw)
        mine = [blocks[i] for i in (own if name == "DualPipeV" else [2 * rank, 2 * rank + 1])]
        err = max((p.grad - g).abs().max().item() for b in mine for p, g in zip(b.parameters(), ref_grads[id(b)]))
        errs = [None] * P; dist.all_gather_object(errs, err)
        if last:
            print(f"{name:<10} loss {sum(l.item() for l in losses) / M:.6f} (one process: {loss_ref.item():.6f}); "
                  f"largest gradient difference on any rank {max(errs):.1e}", flush=True)
        if rank == 0 and name == "DualPipeV":
            order = getattr(sched, "pipeline_order", None)
            if order:
                print(f"\\n{name} schedule as PyTorch computes it (stage, then F = forward, B = backward, I/W = input/weight gradient, then micro-batch;"
                      f" (a;b)OVERLAP_F_B = forward and backward of two micro-batches at once):", flush=True)
                print(_format_pipeline_order(order), flush=True)
    dist.destroy_process_group()
'''))
out = subprocess.run([sys.executable, "-m", "torch.distributed.run", "--nproc_per_node=4", "--master_port=29533",

Reading this output

All three schedules compute exactly the same thing: the loss over 8 micro-batches equals the single-process loss (1.034468), and every gradient on every rank matches to 5×10−10, rounding error. Pipeline schedules only reorder work.

The printed grid is DualPipeV's plan as PyTorch computes it, one column per process. Each entry names the stage, the action and the micro-batch: 3F1 is stage 3 running the forward pass of micro-batch 1. Rank 0 holds stages 0 and 7, rank 3 holds stages 3 and 4: the V. During warm-up, rank 0 runs forwards of stage 0 for micro-batches 0 to 6 while their activations travel down to rank 3 and back up through stages 4 to 7. Backward passes are split into input gradients (I) and weight gradients (W), and from step 10 the entries (4F4;3B0)OVERLAP_F_B are DualPipe's key move: the forward of one micro-batch and the backward of another executed together, which on GPUs lets the computation of one hide the all-to-all communication of the other. At the end, the leftover weight gradients fill what would otherwise be the cool-down bubble. The grid lists actions in order, not wall-clock time, so idle time is not shown.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • DualPipe and DualPipeV: open source from DeepSeek; DualPipeV is also in PyTorch (torch.distributed.pipelining.ScheduleDualPipeV).
  • Zero-bubble schedules (ZB-V, interleaved zero bubble) are in PyTorch's pipelining library next to GPipe and 1F1B.
  • MoE communication kernels: DeepEP, DeepSeek's open all-to-all library, is used for expert parallelism by Megatron-Core, SGLang and vLLM.

Sources: DeepSeek-V3 report, DualPipe repository, Sea AI Lab: Cut-in-half, Zero Bubble, PyTorch pipelining docs, DeepEP.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Pipeline bubbles are a fixed cost per step; more micro-batches shrink their share.
  • Zero Bubble fills bubbles with weight gradients; DualPipe feeds both ends and overlaps forward with backward.
  • DualPipe's main win at DeepSeek scale is hiding all-to-all communication that cost as much as computation.
  • DualPipe keeps 2× stage parameters; DualPipeV gets the same schedule with 1× on half the devices.
  • Schedules reorder work; the gradients are identical.

Given a training cluster specification (total GPU count, interconnect topology, accelerator model, memory per GPU), a model shape (total params, active params, MoE or dense, expected layer count), and a target training-data volume, recommend a pipeline parallelism strategy and confirm the expected bubble fraction.

Produce:

1. Pipeline depth P. Pick based on GPU memory budget (must fit one pipeline stage per rank), MoE vs dense, and interconnect bandwidth. Range: 4 for small clusters, 16-32 for frontier MoE training.
2. Micro-batch count M. Must be divisible by 2 for DualPipe and DualPipeV. Typical ratio M/P between 8 and 16. Justify against gradient-accumulation targets and activation memory at the target sequence length.
3. Schedule choice. Pick from 1F1B, Zero Bubble, DualPipe, DualPipeV. Decision table: dense training under 500 GPUs -> Zero Bubble. MoE with expert parallelism -> DualPipe. Dense training above 500 GPUs without heavy all-to-all -> DualPipeV. Small runs under 100 GPUs -> 1F1B is fine.
4. Expected bubble fraction. Compute for the chosen schedule at the target P and M. Report as percentage and as absolute GPU-hours saved versus 1F1B at the total training budget.
5. Parameter replication plan (DualPipe only). Confirm the 2x parameter replication fits in available VRAM. Report the effective parameter density per GPU given the chosen P.

Hard rejects:
- DualPipe without Expert Parallelism. The 2x replication is not justified without EP-heavy comms to hide.
- P > 64 on any training run. Bubble fraction grows linearly with P regardless of schedule.
- Micro-batch count not divisible by 2 for DualPipe/DualPipeV. The schedule will not close.
- Pipeline parallelism at all when the model fits in one GPU's memory. Use data parallelism only.

Refusal rules:
- If the interconnect is 200Gbps or slower per GPU, refuse DualPipe and recommend DualPipeV. The all-to-all overlap window is too narrow to justify the replication.
- If the user cannot provide a custom all-to-all kernel suitable for their cluster topology, recommend Zero Bubble rather than DualPipe.
- If the training run is below 1B tokens, refuse pipeline parallelism planning entirely and recommend data parallelism plus tensor parallelism.

Output: a one-page plan listing P, M, schedule, expected bubble fraction, parameter replication cost (if DualPipe), and an all-to-all kernel recommendation. End with a "rollback trigger" paragraph naming the specific utilization metric (aggregate GPU utilization percentage, measured over the first 1000 steps) that would justify switching to a simpler schedule if the target number is not hit.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Stage 0 first, and identical gradients

Stage 0 first: rank 0 starts with forward passes of stage 0 for micro-batches 0 to 6 (0F0 to 0F6), because stage 7, also on rank 0, has nothing to do until a micro-batch has travelled through stages 1 to 6 on the other ranks; its first forward (7F0) comes at step 7. And yes, identical gradients: all three schedules match the single-process gradients to 5×10−10, since a schedule changes the order of the forward and backward chunks, never what they compute.

SolutionB1 · Too few micro-batches to fill the pipeline

The pipeline never fills. With fewer micro-batches than stages, the last stage receives its first micro-batch only after the first stage has run out of new ones, so warm-up and cool-down overlap and most devices are idle most of the time: for 1F1B the bubble share would be 15/(15+8)=65%. PyTorch's ScheduleDualPipeV refuses outright: it raises an error when there are fewer micro-batches than stages. The fix is more micro-batches per step (smaller micro-batches or gradient accumulation) or fewer pipeline stages, trading for more data or expert parallelism instead.
