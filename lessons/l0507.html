Why this came about

Once GraphRAG moved from demos to production, teams faced the usual operational questions with a graph twist: storage at scale, queries that explode through highly connected nodes, permissions that must hold along paths, and facts that change over time.

In plain words first
Keeping the map accurate and safe

A knowledge graph in production needs the usual operations plus graph-specific ones: choosing a store that fits the scale and queries, preventing traversals that explode through hub nodes, enforcing who may see which nodes and edges, keeping facts current as sources change, and evaluating whether answers stay correct.

Choosing a store

Workload Good fits What to check
Transactional traversals for an application or agent tool Neo4j, Memgraph, Amazon Neptune Database, Spanner Graph, Azure Cosmos DB for Apache Gremlin Query language, latency at your depth, high availability, backups

Supernodes and traversal explosion

Real graphs are not uniform. A few nodes connect to a huge share of the rest: a default category, a popular product, a shared office address, a "country: India" node. A traversal that passes through one of them suddenly touches a large part of the graph. The simulation below shows how quickly k-hop neighborhoods grow on a graph with that shape.

import random, statistics
import networkx as nx

# Real graphs have a few very connected nodes ("supernodes"): a popular product, a default category,
# a shared address. A preferential-attachment graph reproduces that shape.
G = nx.barabasi_albert_graph(20_000, 3, seed=42)
deg = sorted((d for _, d in G.degree()), reverse=True)
print(f"nodes {G.number_of_nodes():,} | median degree {statistics.median(deg)} | max degree {deg[0]} | "
      f"top 0.1% of nodes hold {sum(deg[:20]) / sum(deg):.0%} of edge endpoints")

rng = random.Random(1)
seeds = rng.sample(list(G), 50)
for depth in [1, 2, 3]:
    sizes = sorted(len(nx.single_source_shortest_path_length(G, s, cutoff=depth)) for s in seeds)
    print(f"  {depth}-hop neighborhood: median {statistics.median(sizes):>7,.0f} nodes, worst {sizes[-1]:>7,} "
          f"({sizes[-1] / G.number_of_nodes():.0%} of the graph)")

# Mitigation: do not expand through supernodes (or sample their edges), and cap depth.
HUB = deg[int(len(deg) * 0.001)]
def bounded(s, depth):
    seen, frontier = {s}, [s]
    for _ in range(depth):
        frontier = [m for n in frontier if G.degree(n) <= HUB or n == s for m in G[n] if m not in seen]
        seen.update(frontier)
    return len(seen)
sizes = sorted(bounded(s, 3) for s in seeds)
print(f"  3-hop, skipping nodes with degree > {HUB}: median {statistics.median(sizes):,.0f}, worst {sizes[-1]:,}")
What you should see
nodes 20,000 | median degree 4.0 | max degree 554 | top 0.1% of nodes hold 4% of edge endpoints
  1-hop neighborhood: median       5 nodes, worst      56 (0% of the graph)
  2-hop neighborhood: median      56 nodes, worst   1,390 (7% of the graph)
  3-hop neighborhood: median     964 nodes, worst  10,444 (52% of the graph)
  3-hop, skipping nodes with degree > 109: median 473, worst 5,124

One hop is tiny, two hops are manageable, and three hops can reach half the graph for an unlucky start: the context for a GraphRAG answer, or the work for an agent's tool call, explodes. Skipping supernodes halves the worst case here. In production combine several controls: a depth limit (two hops covers most questions), filtering by relation type, sampling or ranking the edges of high-degree nodes, and modeling fixes (a country is better as a property than as a node that everything connects to).

Security for graphs

Graphs need the same controls as any data store (Lesson 14.138), plus two of their own: relationships are data, and paths reveal things that single records do not. Two harmless facts ("Priya raised ticket T-88" and "T-88 mentions a medical device") join into a sensitive one.

# Access control on a shared graph: every node carries a tenant, sensitive relationships need a role,
# and the filter is applied by the query layer, never left to the model's prompt.
EDGES = [  # (tenant, source, relation, target)
    ("acme", "Priya", "BOUGHT", "Phone X"), ("acme", "Priya", "RAISED_TICKET", "T-88"),
    ("acme", "T-88", "MENTIONS", "medical device interference"), ("acme", "Priya", "SAME_ADDRESS_AS", "Rahul"),
    ("globex", "Priya", "BOUGHT", "Blender 3000"),          # a different tenant's customer with the same name
]
SENSITIVE = {"MENTIONS": "support_lead", "SAME_ADDRESS_AS": "fraud_team"}

def neighbors(entity, tenant, roles, hops=2):
    out, frontier = [], {entity}
    for _ in range(hops):
        nxt = set()
        for t, a, r, b in EDGES:
            if t != tenant or a not in frontier:
                continue                                    # tenant isolation: other tenants' data is invisible
            if r in SENSITIVE and SENSITIVE[r] not in roles:
                continue                                    # role-gated relationship
            out.append((a, r, b)); nxt.add(b)
        frontier = nxt
    return out

print("support agent, tenant acme:", neighbors("Priya", "acme", roles={"support"}))
print("support lead,  tenant acme:", neighbors("Priya", "acme", roles={"support", "support_lead"}))
print("support agent, tenant globex:", neighbors("Priya", "globex", roles={"support"}))
What you should see
support agent, tenant acme: [('Priya', 'BOUGHT', 'Phone X'), ('Priya', 'RAISED_TICKET', 'T-88')]
support lead,  tenant acme: [('Priya', 'BOUGHT', 'Phone X'), ('Priya', 'RAISED_TICKET', 'T-88'), ('T-88', 'MENTIONS', 'medical device interference')]
support agent, tenant globex: [('Priya', 'BOUGHT', 'Blender 3000')]

The support agent sees purchases and tickets but not the ticket's medical content or the shared-address link used for fraud work; the support lead sees the medical mention; and a customer with the same name in another tenant's data is invisible. Enforce this in the query layer or the database (separate databases per tenant, or row and label security), never by asking the model to ignore what it should not see.

Other rules that matter:

  • Parameterize every query. Cypher injection is SQL injection with different syntax; values from users or documents go in as parameters (Lesson 14.88).
  • Read-only users for agents, restricted to the labels their purpose needs, with query timeouts.
  • Inference review. Before adding a relationship type, ask what it reveals when joined with the others (health, location, relationships between people).
  • Erasure through edges. Deleting a person must remove their node, their edges and the facts extracted from their documents; provenance (Lesson 14.86) is what makes that possible.

Keeping the graph current

Source Sync method Watch for
Systems of record Change data capture or scheduled diff, keyed by source IDs Deletes and merges in the source
Documents Hash chunks; re-extract new and changed ones; retire facts whose only source was removed Facts supported by several documents survive one deletion
Derived data (communities, summaries, centrality) Recompute on a schedule or when enough of the graph changed Stale summaries citing removed facts

Evaluate and operate

  • Retrieval: evidence recall and context size by question type, against a vector baseline (Lesson 14.88).
  • Graph quality: fact precision on a labeled sample, duplicate rate, share of single-source facts, schema violations rejected per run (Lesson 14.85).
  • Cost: extraction cost per changed document, summary refresh cost, query latency at p95 by depth.
  • Operations: traces that include the generated query, the traversal depth and the number of nodes touched (Lesson 14.146), and alerts on queries that hit limits.
From the field
Managed GraphRAG on a cloud

Amazon Bedrock Knowledge Bases GraphRAG (generally available since March 2025) creates the graph from your documents automatically and stores it with embeddings in Amazon Neptune Analytics, combining vector search with graph traversal, with no charges beyond the underlying services (AWS). Managed services remove the construction code but not the decisions in this part: schema scope, entity resolution quality, refresh, security and evaluation are still yours.

Exercises

Predict before you run

bounded(s, depth) limits traversal depth. For a supplier connected to a logistics hub with 100,000 edges, what does depth 2 return without a degree limit? (Solution P1.)

Break it on purpose

Check permissions only on the starting entity, not on the nodes reached by traversal. What leaks? (Solution B1.)

Debugging exercise

An incident was resolved and removed from the source, but GraphRAG still mentions it. Which part of "Keeping the graph current" was skipped? (Solution D1.)

Make it your own

Add a per-query budget to traversal: at most 200 nodes visited, and report when the budget cut the result short. (Solution M1.)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Supernodes explode traversals

Everything connected to the hub, possibly most of the graph, because the second hop goes through the hub. Limit fan-out per node, skip or sample supernodes, or filter edge types.

SolutionB1 · Permissions on every hop

Traversal can reach nodes the user may not see, such as another tenant's suppliers or confidential incidents, and their facts enter the answer. Filter every node and edge by permissions during traversal, as neighbors does.

SolutionD1 · Deletes and summaries

The delete did not propagate to the graph, or the community summary was not regenerated. Track provenance so facts from a removed source are removed, and mark summaries stale when their community changes.

SolutionM1 · Budgets make queries predictable

Count visited nodes, stop at the budget, and return a flag so the answer can say results may be incomplete. Tune the budget on real questions.

Check your understanding

Explain the mechanismWhat are the main operational concerns of a production knowledge graph?

Worked answer. Choosing a store that fits scale and query patterns, controlling supernodes and traversal explosion, enforcing permissions on every node and edge, keeping the graph and its summaries fresh with provenance-based updates and deletes, and evaluating answers and data quality continuously.

A plausible wrong answer. "Once built, it maintains itself." Extraction errors and stale facts accumulate without operations.

Follow-ups. What would you monitor?

Debug a failureGraph queries time out at peak hours only. What do you check?

Worked answer. Concurrent heavy traversals hitting supernodes, missing query timeouts and budgets, and cache misses. Look at the slowest queries' plans and fan-out, add budgets, and precompute common traversals.

A plausible wrong answer. "Scale the database up." Bounding the expensive queries is cheaper.

Follow-ups. Which queries would you precompute?

Defend a designManaged graph database or self-hosted?

Worked answer. Managed when the team is small and the graph is a component, not the product; self-hosted for strict residency, cost at large scale, or features only available there. Evaluate on your query patterns and data size.

A plausible wrong answer. "Self-hosted is always cheaper." Not after operations time.

Follow-ups. What would you load-test?
