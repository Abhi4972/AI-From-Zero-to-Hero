In plain words first
The idea in one line

The 2020 Kaplan paper said: bigger model, lower loss. The 2022 Hoffmann paper said: you were under-training. Compute goes into two buckets — parameters and tokens — and the split is not obvious.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
Parameters (N) "Model size" Non-embedding weight count; determines capacity.
Tokens (D) "Training data" Number of training tokens seen; determines how well the parameters get used.
Compute (C) "FLOPs spent" Approximately 6 × N × D for a standard transformer.
Chinchilla-optimal "D/N ≈ 20" Ratio that minimizes loss per FLOP of pretraining.
Over-training "Past Chinchilla" Spend extra training FLOPs to save inference FLOPs; D/N >> 20.
Irreducible loss "The floor" The $E$ term in the scaling law; the entropy of the data itself.
Emergent capability "Sudden jumps at scale" Often a scorer artifact; continuous loss is smooth.
Effective compute "Training-efficiency multiplier" Better data / optimizer / architecture multiplies how far a FLOP goes.

The problem

In plain words first

Training a language model costs compute, and compute can be spent two ways: a bigger model, or more training text. Scaling laws are formulas, fitted to many training runs, that predict the final loss from those two numbers. The 2022 Chinchilla study found the best split for a fixed training budget is roughly 20 training tokens per parameter, far more data than earlier models used. Today's models are trained on hundreds or thousands of tokens per parameter anyway, because a smaller model is cheaper every time it answers a question. This lesson runs the lesson's Chinchilla calculator, then measures real losses of EleutherAI's Pythia models (several sizes trained on the same data, with checkpoints from during training) on the same Wikipedia text, and works out when it pays to train a smaller model for longer. Nothing is trained.

When you have C FLOPs of training compute and want the best model, you face two knobs:

  1. How many parameters (N)? Bigger model, higher capacity.
  2. How many training tokens (D)? More data, better use of capacity.

FLOPs scale approximately as 6 × N × D. You can push N up and D down, or D up and N down. Which is better?

Before 2022, the answer was "push N hard." GPT-3 (2020) was 175B parameters trained on ~300B tokens. A ratio of about 1.7 tokens per parameter. The Kaplan scaling laws backed this up.

Hoffmann et al. (2022), training over 400 models and then a 70B model called Chinchilla, found something different: optimal ratio is closer to 20 tokens per parameter. GPT-3 was 10× undertrained. Chinchilla (70B params, 1.4T tokens) beat the much larger Gopher (280B) and GPT-3 (175B, 300B tokens) on most benchmarks, at a fraction of their inference cost.

2026 is Chinchilla's world — with one important twist. Llama 3 8B was trained on 15 trillion tokens, a ratio of 1,875 tokens per parameter. Ninety-four times past Chinchilla-optimal. Inference cost matters more than training cost for models that will be used at scale, so over-training (past Chinchilla) for a smaller deployable footprint is the 2026 default.

The idea

Idea 1 · The Hoffmann law

From the Chinchilla paper, loss follows:

L(N,D)=NαA​+DβB​+E

  • N = parameters (non-embedding).
  • D = training tokens.
  • α≈0.34, β≈0.28 (roughly symmetric).
  • E≈1.69, the irreducible loss ceiling.
  • A≈406, B≈411.

Two terms trade against each other as you scale. Take the derivative w.r.t. N at fixed compute (C=6ND) and solve. With the α and β above, the exponents come out at about 0.45 for N and 0.55 for D, so the exact optimal ratio drifts slowly with budget; code/main.py finds it by grid search. The paper's first two fitting approaches, which measure the optimum directly, put both exponents at about 0.5. That gives the empirical rule of a fixed ratio. Set D=20N in C=6ND and the rule of thumb reads:

Nopt​Dopt​Dopt​/Nopt​​≈0.22×(C/6)0.5≈4.5×(C/6)0.5≈20​

Compute-optimal: 20 tokens per parameter.

Idea 2 · Why over-training anyway

Chinchilla-optimal minimizes training loss per training FLOP. But you pay training cost once; inference cost forever.

For a chatbot that serves a trillion tokens per month, inference dominates total cost. Llama's approach: train smaller, longer. 8B at 15T tokens is deeply inference-optimized:

  • Fits on consumer GPUs.
  • Latency is a fraction of 70B Chinchilla-optimal.
  • Quality is close enough for most tasks.

Sardana and Frankle (2024, "Beyond Chinchilla-Optimal") formalized this: once the tokens a model will serve are counted, the cheapest model for a given quality is smaller and trained on more tokens per parameter, and the more it will serve, the further past 20 the best ratio moves.

Idea 3 · Emergence vs smoothness

Claim: certain abilities (arithmetic, multi-step reasoning, chain-of-thought following) "emerge" suddenly at some scale.

Schaeffer et al. (2023) argued this is a measurement artifact: emergent metrics use discontinuous scoring (exact match, accuracy at threshold) that hide smooth improvement in the underlying logits. Continuous metrics (cross-entropy) show smooth curves.

In 2026 the consensus is: predictions via continuous loss are reliable. Benchmark jumps are often scorer artifacts. Plan budgets against continuous metrics.

Idea 4 · The 2026 picture

Scaling laws still work, but:

Factor Changed how
Data quality Curating "good" tokens (Phi-style) shifts curves by >2× effective compute
MoE Total params decouple from active FLOPs; scaling laws per-active-FLOP
Post-training Some capabilities (instruction following, code) shift with SFT+RLHF more than pretraining
Multimodality Image + text tokens scale together; separate curves per modality
Synthetic data Models generate training data; effective compute can compound

The Muon optimizer: Moonshot AI's Moonlight report (2025) measured about 2× the compute efficiency of AdamW in its scaling experiments, and Kimi K2 was trained with a Muon variant. Changes the absolute constant in the scaling law, not its shape.

The same idea as a formula

Training compute. A forward pass costs about 2 FLOPs per parameter per token (one multiply, one add), the backward pass about twice that, so

C≈6ND

for N parameters and D training tokens (attention adds a little, more at long context).

The Chinchilla fit. Final loss as a function of size and data:

L(N,D)=E+NαA​+DβB​,E=1.69, A=406.4, B=410.7, α=0.34, β=0.28

E is the loss no model can beat (the text's own randomness); the other terms are what finite size and finite data cost.

The compute-optimal split. Minimise L subject to 6ND=C. Setting the derivative of AN−α+B(C/6N)−β to zero gives αAN−α=βBD−β at the optimum, so

Nopt​∝Cβ/(α+β)=C0.45,Dopt​∝Cα/(α+β)=C0.55

With these fitted exponents the best ratio D/N drifts upwards with C; the paper's other two methods give exponents near 0.5 each and a constant ratio of about 20.

Counting inference. A model that will serve Dinf​ tokens in its lifetime costs about

Ctotal​=6ND+2NDinf​

For a target loss, a smaller N needs a larger D (from the formula above); if Dinf​ is large, the saving in 2NDinf​ outweighs the extra training.

Worked example
A training budget by hand

Llama 3 8B: N=8×109, D=15×1012 tokens.

  • C=6×8×109×15×1012=7.2×1023 FLOPs.
  • On H100s at 40% of their 989 TFLOP/s bf16 peak (about 4×1014 FLOP/s each): 7.2×1023/4×1014=1.8×109 GPU-seconds, about 500,000 GPU-hours. Meta's model card lists 1.46 million H100-hours for Llama 3.1 8B; the difference is lower utilisation and everything around the main run.
  • Tokens per parameter: 15×1012/8×109=1,875, about 94 times Chinchilla's 20.
  • A Chinchilla-style split of the same 7.2×1023 FLOPs at D=20N: $N = \sqrt{C/120} \approx 77$B parameters on 1.5T tokens, a model ten times more expensive to serve.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Chinchilla says every model should see 20 tokens per parameter."
    ✓ It says that minimises loss for a fixed training budget; when inference dominates, smaller models trained much longer are cheaper overall.
  • ✗ "More training tokens always lower the loss."
    ✓ The data term shrinks ever more slowly, and loss on a particular kind of text can stall or rise late in training (the Pythia run under "Use it").
  • ✗ "Abilities appear suddenly at a certain size."
    ✓ Many such jumps come from all-or-nothing metrics; the underlying loss usually improves smoothly.

Step 1 · Chinchilla loss

See code/main.py. We implement the Chinchilla loss equation and solve for compute-optimal (N,D) at each of several compute budgets.

def chinchilla_loss(N, D, A=406.4, B=410.7, alpha=0.34, beta=0.28, E=1.69):
    return A / N ** alpha + B / D ** beta + E

Reading this output

With the fitted exponents the best ratio D/N is not a constant 20: it grows from 27 at 1018 FLOPs to 120 at 1025, the drift the formula box predicts, and the script itself notes that the paper's direct methods disagree. Over-training by 10× (a tenth of the parameters, ten times the data) costs only 0.06 nats of loss at 1024 FLOPs while making every answer ten times cheaper. The last table's "loss" values are the formula's predictions, not measured results: the real models used different tokenizers and data, so their actual losses are not on this scale.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

You're unlikely to train a frontier model yourself. But scaling laws tell you:

  1. Whether your fine-tune has enough data. If your task-specific data is below 20 tokens per param of the base model, expect saturation at some loss floor.
  2. Whether to pick a bigger base model. If you're spending all your budget on inference, prefer a smaller, longer-trained model.
  3. Where the returns diminish. Each further 10× of tokens buys a smaller drop in loss: the data term shrinks only like D^(−0.28).

The research trajectory in 2026:

  • Data-constrained regime. Public web data after filtering is on the order of tens of trillions of tokens (Hugging Face's FineWeb holds 15 trillion English tokens), and frontier runs already train on that much. Frontier pretraining is approaching this ceiling. Synthetic data, multilingual, multimodal, and RLHF-scaled fine-tuning are the next levers.
  • Compute-multiplier tricks. Muon optimizer, MoE, better data curation — each shifts the absolute constants, not the asymptote.
  • Scaling laws for RL. Open question. Early evidence suggests power-law in RL samples but with very different exponents than pretraining.

Measured losses of a real model family

Three Pythia models (trained on the same 300 billion tokens of the Pile, in the same order) and five training checkpoints of Pythia-70m, all scored on the same 4,096 tokens of WikiText-103 that none of them was trained on.

import time, numpy as np, torch
from datasets import load_dataset
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, AutoTokenizer

# EleutherAI's Pythia suite: models of several sizes trained on the same data (the Pile) in the same order,
# with checkpoints saved during training. Score them all on the same held-out text: no training needed.
torch.set_num_threads(2)
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("EleutherAI/pythia-160m")
wiki = load_dataset("Salesforce/wikitext", "wikitext-103-raw-v1", split="test")
ids = tok("".join(wiki["text"][:2000]), return_tensors="pt").input_ids[0, : 4 * 1024].view(4, 1024)

def loss(repo, revision="main"):
    m = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(repo, revision=revision, dtype=torch.float32).eval()
    with torch.no_grad():
        out = m(ids, labels=ids).loss.item()
    n = sum(p.numel() for name, p in m.named_parameters() if "embed" not in name)     # non-embedding parameters
    del m
    return out, n

print("Final checkpoints (300B tokens each):")
sizes = []
for repo in ["EleutherAI/pythia-70m", "EleutherAI/pythia-160m", "EleutherAI/pythia-410m"]:
    L, n = loss(repo)
    sizes.append((n, L))
    print(f"  {repo:24s} {n / 1e6:6.1f}M non-embedding parameters   loss {L:.3f} nats/token")

print("\nPythia-70m during training (2.1M tokens per step):")
steps = []
for step in [1000, 4000, 16000, 64000, 143000]:
    L, _ = loss("EleutherAI/pythia-70m", f"step{step}")
    steps.append((step * 2_097_152, L))
    print(f"  step {step:6d}: {step * 2_097_152 / 1e9:6.1f}B tokens   loss {L:.3f}")

print("  loss change between checkpoints:", ", ".join(f"{b[1] - a[1]:+.2f}" for a, b in zip(steps, steps[1:])))

# Fit L = E + A / N^a to the three sizes, with E fixed at Chinchilla's 1.69 (three points cannot fix three constants).
x, y = np.array(sizes, float).T
a, _ = np.polyfit(np.log(x), np.log(y - 1.69), 1)
print(f"\nfitted exponent for parameters: {-a:.2f}   (Chinchilla's fit: 0.34)")

Reading this output

Size helps steadily: from 45M to 124M to 354M non-embedding parameters, each about 2.8 times the last, the loss drops from 3.87 to 3.34 to 2.89 nats per token. With the irreducible loss fixed at Chinchilla's 1.69, the three points give an exponent of 0.29, close to Chinchilla's 0.34, although Pythia used a different dataset, tokenizer and evaluation text. Data shows diminishing returns: for Pythia-70m the first 4× of tokens (2.1B to 8.4B) cuts the loss by 0.89 nats, the next 4× by 0.19, the next by 0.09, and the last 2.2× (134B to 300B tokens, about 6,700 tokens per parameter) makes it slightly worse on this text, +0.05. A 70M-parameter model is saturated long before 300B tokens, and the Pile is not Wikipedia, so late training can drift away from it. Scaling laws describe averages on the training distribution; they do not promise that every domain improves.

When does training longer pay off?

The lesson's Chinchilla formula, used the other way round: for the loss of the compute-optimal model at 1024 FLOPs, how many tokens a smaller model needs to match it, and the total cost of training plus serving 1, 10 or 100 trillion tokens.

import numpy as np

A, B, alpha, beta, E = 406.4, 410.7, 0.34, 0.28, 1.69          # the lesson's Chinchilla constants
target = A / 42.5e9 ** alpha + B / 3.9e12 ** beta + E          # loss of the compute-optimal model at 1e24 FLOPs

def tokens_needed(N):                                          # D that reaches the target loss with N parameters
    gap = target - E - A / N ** alpha
    return (B / gap) ** (1 / beta) if gap > 0 else np.inf

print(f"target loss {target:.3f} (the compute-optimal 42.5B model trained on 3.9T tokens)\n")
print(f"{'parameters':>10} {'tokens':>9} {'D/N':>6} {'training FLOPs':>15} |  total FLOPs when serving this many tokens:")
print(f"{'':>10} {'':>9} {'':>6} {'':>15} |  {'1 trillion':>12} {'10 trillion':>12} {'100 trillion':>13}")
for N in [42.5e9, 30e9, 20e9, 15e9, 10e9, 8e9]:
    D = tokens_needed(N)
    train = 6 * N * D
    totals = [train + 2 * N * served for served in (1e12, 1e13, 1e14)]   # inference: about 2N FLOPs per token
    print(f"{N / 1e9:9.1f}B {D / 1e12:8.1f}T {D / N:6.0f} {train:15.2e} |  " + " ".join(f"{t:12.2e}" for t in totals))

Matching the loss of the 42.5B compute-optimal model with 20B parameters takes 10.2T tokens instead of 3.9T, 22% more training compute. If the model will serve only a trillion tokens, that never pays back: the 42.5B model is cheapest. At 10 trillion served tokens, 20–30B models are cheapest; at 100 trillion, a 15B model trained on 17.5T tokens (1,167 per parameter) costs half as much in total as the "optimal" one. Shrinking too far backfires: 8B parameters need 121T tokens, more text than exists in filtered public data. This is the reasoning behind Llama 3 8B's 15T tokens and every small model trained far past 20 tokens per parameter.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Over-training is standard. Llama 3 8B saw 15T tokens (about 1,900 per parameter), Qwen2.5 models up to 18T and Qwen3 about 36T tokens; small models are trained far past the Chinchilla ratio because they are served at scale.
  • Data quality as a multiplier. Filtered datasets such as FineWeb-Edu and DCLM, and synthetic data from larger models, buy the same loss with fewer tokens; most open model reports now describe data filtering in more detail than architecture.
  • Scaling at inference time. Reasoning models (OpenAI's o-series, DeepSeek-R1, Qwen3's thinking mode) improve by spending more tokens per answer, so serving cost per query, not only model size, enters the budget.

Sources: Llama 3 paper, Qwen3 technical report, FineWeb, DCLM, DeepSeek-R1, Beyond Chinchilla-Optimal.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Training compute ≈6ND; the Chinchilla fit predicts loss as E+A/Nα+B/Dβ.
  • For a fixed training budget, grow N and D together, roughly 20 tokens per parameter.
  • Counting serving cost 2NDinf​ moves the optimum to smaller, longer-trained models.
  • Real models follow the trends, but loss on a given domain can stall; measure on your own text.

Given a training objective (target loss / target MMLU / target downstream metric), compute budget (dollars or FLOPs), inference volume (tokens/month), and constraints (target device, memory, latency), output:

1. Compute regime. Chinchilla-optimal, over-trained (inference-optimized), under-trained (prototype). One-sentence reason tied to inference volume.
2. N and D. Concrete values. Print the `D/N` ratio. If over-trained, note the loss penalty vs Chinchilla-optimal.
3. Training wall-clock. Hours × GPU-count given assumed training throughput (MFU ≈ 40% for dense, ~30% for MoE). Budget the precision (bf16 / fp8) and optimizer (AdamW / Muon).
4. Data sources. Named corpora or synthetic budget. Flag if the required `D` exceeds available high-quality tokens.
5. Risk note. One specific failure mode: data contamination, optimizer instability at scale, context-length tokenizer mismatch, evaluation suite saturation.

Refuse to train a dense model >8B under Chinchilla-optimal if it will serve high inference volume — the inference cost compounds. Refuse to set target loss without a held-out evaluation suite defined. Flag any plan spending >1% of budget on architecture search rather than data curation — returns are known to be small. Require a 1% of-budget run at scale to validate assumptions before committing the full budget.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Not at all, on this text

Not at all on this text: the loss goes from 3.819 at 134 billion tokens to 3.872 at 300 billion, slightly worse. The early checkpoints improve fast (4.995 at 2.1B tokens, 4.104 at 8.4B), then each 4× of data helps less (−0.19, then −0.09). By the end, the 70M-parameter model has seen about 6,700 tokens per parameter and has little capacity left to use more data, and the Pile's mix differs from Wikipedia, so continued training on it can shift the model away from this kind of text. The scaling law's smooth curve is an average on the training distribution, not a guarantee for every domain.

SolutionB1 · A plan three times over budget

With C=2ND the same budget appears to buy three times as many parameter-tokens. The optimiser therefore recommends an N and D whose product is three times too large (about 30.45 times the parameters and 30.55 times the tokens). Launched as planned, the run would need three times the GPU-hours you had: it would be cut off a third of the way through, with a model trained on a third of its intended tokens, worse than the correctly planned smaller model. The forward pass is only a third of training compute; the backward pass computes gradients for both the activations and the weights, about twice the forward cost.

Short answer. Compute is about 6 times the parameter count times the training tokens, so the budget fixes the product and leaves the split open. The compute-optimal split is about 20 tokens per parameter; the first plan has about 1.7 tokens per parameter, which the lesson calls ten times undertrained, and a 70B model trained on 1.4T tokens beat that 175B model on every benchmark at 2.5 times less inference cost. If the model will serve a large volume, go past the optimum on purpose: train smaller and longer, because training is paid once and inference is paid forever.

The law.

L(N, D) = A / N^alpha + B / D^beta + E
alpha about 0.34, beta about 0.28, E about 1.69, A about 406, B about 411

N is the non-embedding parameter count, D the training tokens, E the irreducible loss of the data itself. Minimize L under C = 6 N D and the two optimal quantities grow with about the square root of the budget:

N_opt about 0.22 * (C / 6)^0.5
D_opt about 4.5  * (C / 6)^0.5
D_opt / N_opt about 20

The fitted exponents drift slowly, about 0.45 for N and 0.55 for D with the constants above, so the ratio moves with the budget; the lesson's code finds the optimum by grid search.

What the two plans look like.

Plan N D Tokens per parameter
Large and short 175B 300B about 1.7
Compute-optimal 70B 1.4T 20
Inference-optimized 8B 15T 1,875

The rows do not share one budget: the second and third rows use about twice the compute of the first, so they compare ratios, not alternatives at the same cost. The last row is a production model family that trained a small model ninety-four times past the compute-optimal ratio. For a service that answers a trillion tokens a month, inference dominates total cost, and a model that fits consumer hardware with a fraction of the latency wins even though its training loss per training FLOP was worse. The lesson's range for inference-dominated workloads is 100 to 500 tokens per parameter depending on serving volume.

How to defend it.

  1. Show the budget as 6 N D and the loss contours over N and D; the first plan sits far from the valley.
  2. Show the inference bill at the expected volume for each N; the small model's saving repeats every month.
  3. Show the loss gap you accept by over-training, read from the law at the chosen N and D.

What changes the constants but not the shape.

Data curation can shift the curves by more than 2 times effective compute. A mixture-of-experts model decouples total parameters from active FLOPs, so the law applies per active parameter. A better optimizer shifted the constant by about 2 times in one 2024 result. None of these change the power-law shape.

The fine-tuning corollary.

The lesson's rule of thumb: if your task data holds fewer than 20 tokens per parameter of the base model, expect saturation at some loss floor. Treat it as a warning to measure, not as a rule for the base size.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The bigger model always wins at fixed compute." The 70B model trained on 1.4T tokens beat the 175B model trained on 300B.
  • "Compute-optimal is also the cheapest model to serve." It minimizes training loss per training FLOP; serving cost scales with N, which is why production models over-train.
  • "A loss prediction is useless because abilities appear suddenly." The continuous loss is smooth and predictable; sudden benchmark jumps are mostly scorer artifacts.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) What is the E term and why can no budget remove it? Expected: the irreducible loss, the entropy of the data itself; the law approaches it but never crosses it.
  • (level 4) How does a sparse expert architecture change the plan? Expected: FLOPs follow the active parameters, so the optimal D is set against active N while total N sets memory.
  • (level 4) The web runs out of high-quality tokens for your budget. What are the levers? Expected: the lesson puts filtered English at about 5 to 10 trillion tokens; the levers are synthetic data, multilingual and multimodal data, and better curation.
  • (level 4) How would you check the law on your own stack before betting the budget? Expected: fit it on five small models from 100K to 10M parameters on the same data and compare the exponents with the published ones.

Explain the mechanismBetween two model sizes a benchmark accuracy goes from near zero to high. The team calls it an emergent ability and asks for a larger budget to find the next jump. How do you evaluate the claim before you sign?

Level 4 of 4 · about 12 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Ask what the scorer is. Exact match and thresholded accuracy are discontinuous: a model that gets most digits right scores zero until it gets all of them right, so a smooth gain in the underlying probabilities shows up as a cliff. Re-score the same checkpoints with a continuous metric such as the cross-entropy of the target answers; if that curve is smooth, the jump belongs to the scorer and not to the model. Plan the budget against the continuous curve, which the scaling law predicts reliably.

The argument.

The 2023 analysis of emergence showed that the abilities usually cited, arithmetic and multi-step reasoning among them, were measured with discontinuous scoring. Under a continuous metric the same models improve smoothly with scale. By 2026 the working consensus is: predictions from continuous loss are reliable, and benchmark jumps are often artifacts of the scorer.

What to do with the checkpoints you have.

  1. Keep the prompts and the targets fixed.
  2. For each checkpoint, log the per-token probability of the correct answer and the cross-entropy over the answer tokens.
  3. Plot both against the parameter count and against the training compute.
  4. Add a partial-credit scorer, such as the share of correct digits, to see where the cliff disappears.

If the probability curve is smooth, the model was getting closer all along and the benchmark hid it.

What it means for the budget.

Fit the loss on the checkpoints and read where the next size lands on the continuous curve. Then calibrate the discontinuous benchmark against that curve: at which loss does accuracy cross the product threshold? Sample more sizes near that point instead of betting on a surprise. A product threshold can be real for the user and still be predictable for the planner.

Where real changes of regime do show up.

In-context learning, following a few examples with no fine-tuning, is usually described as appearing around the 6B to 13B range. The lesson's position is still to measure it with a continuous metric before you call it a discontinuity, because the same scorer problem applies.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Accuracy is the ground truth and loss is a proxy." Accuracy with a hard threshold throws away the information that shows the trend; the loss is what the model optimizes and what the law predicts.
  • "Emergence means the scaling law broke." The law describes the continuous loss, which keeps its shape while the thresholded score jumps.
  • "Train the bigger model and see." That is the expensive experiment the analysis lets you avoid; re-score what you already have first.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) Give an example of a discontinuous scorer and its continuous counterpart. Expected: exact match on a multi-digit answer against the log-probability or the digit-level accuracy of the same answer.
  • (level 4) How does this change the way you plan a training budget? Expected: predict the loss from the law, calibrate the benchmark against the loss, and place runs near the threshold instead of hoping for a jump.
  • (level 4) The product threshold really is binary for the user. Does the artifact argument still matter? Expected: yes; the threshold is real for the product, but its position is predictable from the smooth curve, which is what you need to budget.
  • (level 4) What would convince you that a jump is not a scorer artifact? Expected: the continuous metric also shows a kink at the same scale, replicated across seeds and data orderings.
