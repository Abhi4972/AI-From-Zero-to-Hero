In plain words first
The idea in one line

This course builds AI from the ground up: every idea first written out in small code you can run, then used the way production teams use it, from a first training loop to agents that work in teams.

Why this came about

Most AI courses pick one end of the subject. Some start with a working chatbot and never explain what is inside it; others start with calculus and lose people before anything works. This course does both, in order: every idea is built from scratch in small code you can run, the maths is shown with pictures and real numbers, and only then do the lessons reach for the libraries and the production practice that teams use.

Who this course is for

Anyone who wants to understand AI properly, from the ground up: engineers, analysts, data scientists, product people and students. You do not need to know Python, statistics or machine learning before you start. The course teaches each of them, and each lesson names what it assumes, so you can skip what you already know.

By the end you will be able to:

  • explain, with the right technical terms, how a model learns from data, how a neural network is trained, and how a transformer turns text into predictions;
  • build the core pieces yourself in Python: a training loop, a convolutional network, a tokenizer, attention, a small language model, retrieval and an agent loop;
  • use the libraries and protocols that production teams use, and evaluate, secure, serve and cost what you build;
  • design agents and multi-agent systems that hold up outside a demo.

How the parts connect

The first six parts follow the history of the field: each one exists because the step before it ran into a limit. The seventh and eighth are practice.

  1. Part 1 · Foundations (Phases 0 to 3). Tools, the maths you actually need, classic machine learning and the core of deep learning, written out by hand. Everything later stands on these four phases.
  2. Part 2 · Perception (Phases 4 to 6). How models see, read and listen: convolutional vision, language from tokens to sequence models, and speech. Each modality introduces an idea the rest of the course reuses.
  3. Part 3 · Models (Phases 7 to 10). Attention and transformers in depth, generative models, reinforcement learning, and finally a small language model built end to end.
  4. Part 4 · Building with LLMs (Phases 11 to 13). Using large models well: prompting, retrieval, evaluation and serving; models that mix text and images; and the tools and protocols, such as MCP, that connect them to the world.
  5. Part 5 · Agents (Phases 14 to 16). The agent loop and everything around it: planning, memory, tools, frameworks and production engineering, then autonomous systems and teams of agents.
  6. Part 6 · Serving, Safety & Capstones (Phases 17 to 19). Running models for real users (serving engines, GPUs, caching, routing, observability, releases, security, compliance and cost), the safety and alignment work that keeps systems trustworthy, and capstone projects that build complete systems step by step.
  7. Part 7 · Projects and Case Files (Phases 20 and 21). 79 smaller projects you build and test stage by stage, from starter tools to frontier systems, and 60 real incidents from serving and agent systems to diagnose the way an interview debugging round asks you to.
  8. Part 8 · Project Library (Phases 22 to 30). 427 projects, recipes and samples from other open-licence repositories: classic machine learning and build-from-scratch projects that run here with their real output, then RAG, application, agent, multi-agent and MCP projects, Claude API recipes and Snowflake samples that you run with your own keys.
  9. Part 9 · Forward Deployed Engineering (Phase 31). A 20-lesson intensive on the forward deployed engineer's job with Claude, modelled on Anthropic's Claude FDE learning path and Frontier Academy and on other FDE programmes: discovery, scoping, building inside a customer's systems, security review, evals, cost, pilots, adoption and handover, ending in a four-day simulated deployment with a graded practical.
  10. Part 10 · MLOps and LLMOps (Phase 32). Running models as systems: reproducibility, experiment tracking and a model registry with MLflow, data contracts, point-in-time features, pipelines, CI/CD, serving, canary releases, monitoring, retraining and governance, Snowflake-native MLOps, and the LLMOps practices for prompts, evaluation data, monitoring and model upgrades.

Inside a lesson

  • Guided and Scroll. This switch is in the top bar. Guided shows a lesson one part at a time: a briefing, the idea in steps, practice, a check and a finish. Scroll shows the whole lesson as one page. The words are the same in both.
  • The parts list. In Guided view the parts of the lesson are listed on the left. Click a part to jump to it, or use Back and Continue or the left and right arrow keys. On a phone, the bar at the bottom shows where you are.
  • In one line. This coloured box closes most parts. It states the one thing to carry forward. Read it, then say it in your own words before you continue.
  • Show output. This button, under a code block, reveals what the code printed when it was run for this course, with a note on what to notice. Predict the output first.
  • Code map. Longer code blocks come with a list of their functions and classes. Click a name to see what it does and to highlight its lines.
  • New words in this part. This row lists the terms a part introduces. Click a word to see its meaning.
  • True or false? Common misunderstandings are shown as statements. Decide whether each is true, then open it to check.
  • Pictures. Replay draws an animated diagram again, and the enlarge button opens a picture at full size. Where several pictures show the same idea, tabs switch between them.
  • Show solution. This button, on each exercise card, opens the worked answer. Try the exercise first.

At the end of a lesson

  • Replay. After the teaching parts, most lessons show a few of their own code runs again. Say what you expect each one to print, then reveal what really happened. Lessons with no code ask recall questions here.
  • Carry forward. The last part lists the In one line of every part, hidden. Say each from memory, then tap to check.
  • Mark complete and continue. This button, on the last part of each lesson, records the lesson and moves you on. Quiz scores are recorded when you answer every question.

The course remembers your choice of view, and the part of each lesson you were on.

When a lesson mentions something it has not taught yet

No course can teach everything in a single straight line: a lesson on prompts has to mention tools, and a lesson on agents has to mention retrieval. So that this never leaves you guessing, the course marks every such mention.

  • A dotted underline under a term means the term is taught in another lesson. Tap or hover over it to see a one-line meaning and a link to the lesson that teaches it.
  • A small "later" pill after a term, such as later · 14.60, means the lesson that teaches it comes later in the course. The one-line meaning is all you need for now; you are not expected to follow the link.
  • "Mentioned here, taught later", near the top of a lesson, lists every such term in that lesson with its one-line meaning and where it is taught. Open it if you want the list in one place.
  • "How this connects" boxes appear where a lesson returns to something an earlier lesson taught, or prepares something a later one will. They say what you already know, and what is new here.

If you are skipping around, the same marks tell you where to go back to: a dotted term with no pill was taught earlier, and its link takes you there.

How every lesson is built

Every lesson of the course follows the same shape, so you always know where you are:

  • Goals and prerequisites. What you will be able to do, what you need first, and how long it takes.
  • In plain words first. The idea in everyday language before any jargon, then Terms you'll hear, which explains the words practitioners use.
  • The problem and the ideas. Why the topic exists, then the concepts one at a time, with a formula, a worked example by hand, and a prediction to make before you run anything.
  • See it drawn. Real runs on small models and real data, with the exact output and a note on how to read it. Diagrams animate and can be stepped through one part at a time.
  • Build it, then use it. The mechanism written from scratch, then the same idea with the libraries teams use.
  • Practice. A deliberate mistake to find, how industry does it today, exercises with solutions, practice problems you can check in the browser, and interview questions with model answers.

How to study so it sticks

These habits come from learning research and they make a real difference:

  • Predict before you run. Before running a code block, write down what you expect it to print. Being wrong and then seeing why is the fastest way to learn.
  • Recall, do not reread. At the end of a lesson, close it and write the three main ideas from memory. Then check. The quizzes and check your understanding questions exist for this.
  • Space it out. An hour a day for a week beats seven hours on Sunday. Come back to the quiz of the previous lesson before starting the next.
  • Explain it to someone. If you can explain attention or RAG to a colleague without notes, you understand it.
  • Change the code. After each example works, change one thing (a number, a word, a step) and predict the effect. The Make it your own exercises guide this.
  • Do not wait to feel ready. You will not understand every detail the first time. Keep going; later lessons revisit earlier ideas from new angles.

How this course was designed

  • Build it before you use it. Each mechanism is written from scratch in a few dozen lines first, so that when a library does it for you, you know what it is doing.
  • Real runs, not made-up output. Every figure and every printed number comes from code that was actually run, on small models that fit on a laptop, so you can reproduce it.
  • Pictures before equations. Diagrams build themselves step by step, and many lessons have an interactive figure you can drag or slide; the formula comes after the picture.
  • Practice that checks itself. Predictions, true-or-false cards, quick checks that come back in Review, exercises with solutions, practice problems checked in the browser, and interview questions with model answers.
  • The whole field, in order. The phases run from tooling and maths to agents in production, with gentle on-ramp lessons and the applied agent engineering teams use today placed inside the phases where they fit.

The stack in one picture

When you reach the building blocks, every agentic system, whatever the vendor, is made of the same six layers, plus concerns that cut across all of them. You will learn each layer in turn; this picture is the map.

Dates and trust

AI tools change monthly. Version-sensitive material carries the date it was checked, and Lesson 0.3 sets up the environment the code was run in. When something in a lesson disagrees with a library's official documentation, trust the documentation.
