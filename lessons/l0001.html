Why this came about

Most AI courses pick one end of the subject. Some start with a working chatbot and never explain what is inside it; others start with calculus and lose people before anything works. Teams that need to use generative AI responsibly need both: a working mental model of how these systems behave, and the hands-on skill to build with them. This course was written to be the one place where a complete beginner can go from "what is a model?" to designing an agent that is safe to put in front of customers.

Who this course is for

Anyone who is new to generative AI and AI agents, whatever their background. You might be a data engineer, an analyst, a product manager, a consultant, a tester or a student. You do not need to know Python, statistics or machine learning. The course teaches each of these from zero, at the moment you need it, and it never uses a term before explaining it.

If you already know some of it, the course is still for you: each chapter opens with what it assumes, so you can skip what you know and lose nothing.

By the end you will be able to:

  • explain, in plain words and with the right technical terms, how a language model turns text into numbers, predicts the next word, and becomes an assistant that follows instructions;
  • write Python that calls models, gets structured answers back, and connects them to your own data and tools;
  • build retrieval (RAG), agents and multi-agent systems by hand first, then with frameworks such as LangChain and LangGraph;
  • evaluate, secure, monitor and cost these systems the way production teams do.

How the pieces connect

Each part of this course exists because the previous step ran into a limit. Read this once, and every lesson's Why this came about box will slot into it.

  1. Programs that learn (Chapters F1 to F3). Ordinary programs follow rules a person wrote. For problems too fuzzy for rules, such as "is this email spam?", machine learning lets a program find the rules from examples. Python is the language almost all of this is written in, and a little math (vectors, probability, slopes) is the language the ideas are written in.
  2. Neural networks (Chapter F4). Simple models cannot learn complicated patterns. Neural networks stack many simple units in layers and learn by nudging millions of numbers. Learning to train them reliably, from about 2012, started the deep learning era.
  3. Language as numbers (Chapter F5). To learn from text, words must become numbers. Counting words, then learning word vectors (2013), then models that read word by word (RNNs) each solved part of the problem. They still struggled with long text, which led to attention (2014).
  4. Transformers (Chapter F6). In 2017, the transformer replaced reading word by word with attention everywhere. It trains fast on huge amounts of text, and it is the architecture inside every large language model you use today.
  5. From language model to assistant (Chapter F7). A transformer trained to predict the next word on most of the internet becomes a large language model (LLM). Further training on instructions and human preferences turns it into an assistant. Its limits (no access to your data, no actions, occasional confident mistakes) explain everything that follows.
  6. Using models well (Chapters 1 and 2). Calling models from code brought practical concerns: tokens and cost, speed, randomness, structured output, prompts and context.
  7. Agents (Chapter 3). Text alone cannot look things up or act, so tool calling (2023) let models request actions, and a loop around it made agents.
  8. Knowledge (Chapters 4 and 5). Models do not know your private, current information, so retrieval (RAG) fetches it for each question. Knowledge graphs help when the question is about connections.
  9. Frameworks and protocols (Chapters 6 to 8). Everyone was rebuilding the same plumbing, so frameworks (LangChain, LangGraph and others) and protocols (MCP) packaged it.
  10. Beyond text, safety and model building (Chapters 9 to 11). Models learned to see and speak; agents with real permissions made security central; and knowing how models are trained explains when customizing one is worth it.
  11. Production (Chapters 12 to 14). Demos that worked failed at scale, so production practice grew around them: evaluation, observability, reliability, cost, and the cloud platforms that package these pieces. A capstone puts it all together.

Finding your way around

The course

  • Course home. Click the course name at the top left, or Course home in the contents. It shows every chapter as a card, your overall progress, and a Continue button that takes you to your next lesson.
  • Choose your path. Pick a path on the home page. Your progress is then counted against that path, and lessons outside it are shown faded but stay available.
  • Chapters. A chapter groups lessons. Open one to see its lessons, how long each takes, and which you have finished.
  • Contents. This button, at the top left of a lesson, opens the list of all chapters and lessons. Search every lesson in that list looks through the full text of the course, and a result opens the lesson at the part that contains your words.
  • Your progress. It is saved in your browser. The home page offers to keep it with your Claude account, so that it follows you to other devices.

Inside a lesson

  • Guided and Scroll. This switch is in the top bar. Guided shows a lesson one part at a time: a briefing, the idea in steps, practice, a check and a finish. Scroll shows the whole lesson as one page. The words are the same in both.
  • The parts list. In Guided view the parts of the lesson are listed on the left. Click a part to jump to it, or use Back and Continue or the left and right arrow keys. On a phone, the bar at the bottom shows where you are.
  • In one line. This coloured box closes most parts. It states the one thing to carry forward. Read it, then say it in your own words before you continue.
  • Show output. This button, under a code block, reveals what the code printed when it was run for this course, with a note on what to notice. Predict the output first.
  • Code map. Longer code blocks come with a list of their functions and classes. Click a name to see what it does and to highlight its lines.
  • New words in this part. This row lists the terms a part introduces. Click a word to see its meaning.
  • True or false? Common misunderstandings are shown as statements. Decide whether each is true, then open it to check.
  • Pictures. Replay draws an animated diagram again, and the enlarge button opens a picture at full size. Where several pictures show the same idea, tabs switch between them.
  • Show solution. This button, on each exercise card, opens the worked answer. Try the exercise first.

At the end of a lesson

  • Replay. After the teaching parts, most lessons show a few of their own code runs again. Say what you expect each one to print, then reveal what really happened. Lessons with no code ask recall questions here.
  • Carry forward. The last part lists the In one line of every part, hidden. Say each from memory, then tap to check.
  • Mark complete and continue. This button, on the last part of each lesson, records the lesson and moves you on. Quiz scores are recorded when you answer every question.

The course remembers your choice of view, and the part of each lesson you were on.

When a lesson mentions something it has not taught yet

No course can teach everything in a single straight line: a lesson on prompts has to mention tools, and a lesson on agents has to mention retrieval. So that this never leaves you guessing, the course marks every such mention.

  • A dotted underline under a term means the term is taught in another lesson. Tap or hover over it to see a one-line meaning and a link to the lesson that teaches it.
  • A small "later" pill after a term, such as later · 4.6, means the lesson that teaches it comes later in the course. The one-line meaning is all you need for now; you are not expected to follow the link.
  • "Mentioned here, taught later", near the top of a lesson, lists every such term in that lesson with its one-line meaning and where it is taught. Open it if you want the list in one place.
  • "How this connects" boxes appear where a lesson returns to something an earlier lesson taught, or prepares something a later one will. They say what you already know, and what is new here.

If you are skipping around, the same marks tell you where to go back to: a dotted term with no pill was taught earlier, and its link takes you there.

How every lesson is built

Every lesson follows the same shape, so you always know where you are:

  1. Goals and prerequisites. What you will be able to do, what you need first, and how long it takes.
  2. Why this came about. The problem that led to the idea, so it connects to the story above.
  3. In plain words first. The idea in everyday language, usually with an analogy, before any jargon or code.
  4. The idea, one step at a time. Each step adds one thing: a picture, a small runnable example, its exact output, and an explanation of every line you did not write yourself.
  5. Exercises, with solutions folded away. Try first, then open the answer.
  6. Questions to check your understanding, and a short quiz. Answer in your own words before opening the model answer.

How to study so it sticks

These habits come from learning research and they make a real difference:

  • Predict before you run. Before running a code block, write down what you expect it to print. Being wrong and then seeing why is the fastest way to learn.
  • Recall, do not reread. At the end of a lesson, close it and write the three main ideas from memory. Then check. The quizzes and check your understanding questions exist for this.
  • Space it out. An hour a day for a week beats seven hours on Sunday. Come back to the quiz of the previous lesson before starting the next.
  • Explain it to someone. If you can explain attention or RAG to a colleague without notes, you understand it.
  • Change the code. After each example works, change one thing (a number, a word, a step) and predict the effect. The Make it your own exercises guide this.
  • Do not wait to feel ready. You will not understand every detail the first time. Keep going; later lessons revisit earlier ideas from new angles.

How this course was designed

The structure borrows what works from the most respected courses and books, so that a beginner gets the best of each:

  • The whole game first, then the parts. fast.ai's Practical Deep Learning starts with a working system and digs into how it works, rather than starting with theory. Lesson 0.22 does the same: you see a language model, retrieval and an agent working before learning how each is built.
  • Build small versions from scratch. Andrej Karpathy's Neural Networks: Zero to Hero and Sebastian Raschka's Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch) teach by building a tiny training engine, a language model, a GPT and a tokenizer in code. This course does the same in Chapters F4 to F6, with smaller code, every line explained, and nothing that needs a GPU.
  • Pictures before equations. Jay Alammar's illustrated guides and book, and 3Blue1Brown's videos, made transformers understandable to millions through pictures. Polo Club's Transformer Explainer showed that an overview you can drill into, plus live sliders such as temperature, helps people see that a model is a sequence of operations rather than magic. Every key mechanism here has a diagram, and many have a Try it figure.
  • Just enough math, just in time. fast.ai teaches the math as it is needed, and practitioners warn that weeks of up-front theory stall beginners. Chapter F2 gives four short, visual math lessons; every formula is explained again where it is used.
  • Learn and Build lessons. Microsoft's Generative AI for Beginners alternates concept lessons with build lessons; Hugging Face's courses pair each concept with runnable notebooks. Here every concept comes with code you run.
  • The agent patterns, in the order teachers converge on. DeepLearning.AI's Agentic AI and Microsoft's AI Agents for Beginners both teach what an agent is, then reflection and tool use, then evaluation, then planning and multi-agent systems, then production. Chapters 3, 6 and 12 follow that arc.
  • Coverage checked against the field. The list of topics was compared with Maxime Labonne's LLM Course roadmap (the scientist and engineer tracks), the Hugging Face Agents Course, published AI-engineering syllabi, and LangChain's 2026 State of Agent Engineering survey of what teams run in production. That comparison is why the course includes a full fine-tuning workshop (Chapter 11), convolutional networks (Lesson 3.20), generative image models built from scratch (Lesson 4.31), and retrieval taught with real embedding and reranking models (Chapter 4).
  • Evidence from learning science. Richard Mayer's principles of multimedia learning (name the parts before showing the process, short segments, labels placed on the picture, leave out what is not needed) and research on retrieval practice and spacing shape every lesson's layout and its exercises.

The stack in one picture

When you reach the building blocks, every agentic system, whatever the vendor, is made of the same six layers, plus concerns that cut across all of them. You will learn each layer in turn; this picture is the map.

Layer The question it answers Examples you will use in this course
Interfaces How do people (or events) reach the agent? Chat UIs, voice, Slack bots, IDE plugins, webhooks
Protocols How do agents connect to tools, other agents and UIs? MCP, A2A, AG-UI
Frameworks and harnesses How is the agent's control flow written? LangGraph, OpenAI Agents SDK, Strands, Google ADK, Microsoft Agent Framework, CrewAI, Pydantic AI
Knowledge and memory What does the agent know, and how does it look things up? Vector databases, keyword search, knowledge graphs, SQL, memory stores
Model access Which models, through what door? Provider APIs, open-weight models, an AI gateway in front of all of them
Runtime Where does it run and how does it survive failures? Containers, serverless, durable execution, sandboxes
Cross-cutting How is it kept safe, observable and compliant? Guardrails, tracing, evaluations, cost controls, governance

Dates and trust

AI tools change monthly. Version-sensitive material carries the date it was checked, and library versions are pinned in Lesson 0.23. When something in a lesson disagrees with a library's official documentation, trust the documentation, and note the difference for your team.
