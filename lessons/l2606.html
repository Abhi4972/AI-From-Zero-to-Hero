Why this came about

The 2017 transformer had both an encoder and a decoder, for translation. In 2018 the two halves went separate ways. OpenAI's GPT kept the decoder and trained it to predict the next word. Google's BERT kept the encoder and trained it to fill in hidden words using context on both sides, which made it excellent at understanding tasks: classification, question answering, search. In 2019 Nils Reimers and Iryna Gurevych's Sentence-BERT fine-tunedlater · 10.36 such encoders so that whole sentences with similar meanings get similar vectors, the basis of today's embedding models and vector search. T5 (2019) and BART kept the full encoder-decoder for text-to-text tasks, and Whisper (2022) uses the same design for speech. Decoders later grew into today's chat models, but encoders still do much of the quiet work: embeddings, rerankinglater · 5.38, classification.

In plain words first
Readers, writers and translators

An encoder is a careful reader: it sees the whole text at once, both before and after every word, and builds a rich understanding of each part. A decoder is a writer: it produces text one word at a time and can only look back at what is already written. An encoder-decoder is a translator: one part reads the whole source, the other writes the result while glancing back at the source. Which one you want depends on whether your job is to understand, to write, or to transform.

Step 1 · The three families

Family Trained by Sees Good at Examples
Encoder-only filling in masked words the whole text, both directions classification, tagging, embeddings, reranking BERT, RoBERTa, DeBERTa, most embedding models
Decoder-only predicting the next token only earlier tokens generating text; with enough scale, nearly everything GPT, Llama, Mistral, Claude, Gemini
Encoder-decoder reconstructing or transforming text encoder: all; decoder: earlier output plus the whole input translation, summarisation, speech recognition T5, BART, Whisper

Step 2 · A masked language model

BERT-style encoders are trained to recover words that were hidden with a [MASK] token, using both sides of the context. DistilBERT is a smaller, faster copy of BERT (66 million parameters):

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F
from transformers import pipeline

fill = pipeline("fill-mask", model="distilbert/distilbert-base-uncased")
for r in fill("The parcel was [MASK] because of the heavy rain.", top_k=5):
    print(f"{r['token_str']:<12} {r['score']:.3f}")
What you should see
flooded      0.378
abandoned    0.060
evacuated    0.056
inaccessible 0.055
closed       0.051

The model uses "because of the heavy rain", which comes after the gap, to choose its answer. A decoder could not do that, because it only sees the past. This two-way reading is why encoders are strong at understanding tasks; it is also why they cannot generate text naturally, one word after another.

pipeline is Hugging Face's high-level helper: it downloads the model (about 270 MB the first time), tokenises the text, runs the model and decodes the result. Lesson 7.23 opens up those steps.

Step 3 · Contextual embeddings, measured

In Lesson 5.33, GPT-2's embedding table gave "bank" one vector, dominated by money. Inside an encoder, each layer mixes in the surrounding words, so the same word gets different vectors in different sentences. Take the vector for "bank" from DistilBERT's last layer in three sentences:

from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModel

tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("distilbert/distilbert-base-uncased")
encoder = AutoModel.from_pretrained("distilbert/distilbert-base-uncased")
encoder.eval()

def word_vector(sentence, word="bank"):
    enc = tok(sentence, return_tensors="pt")
    with torch.no_grad():
        hidden = encoder(**enc).last_hidden_state[0]       # (tokens, 768): one contextual vector per token
    position = enc["input_ids"][0].tolist().index(tok.convert_tokens_to_ids(word))
    return hidden[position]

river = word_vector("we sat on the grassy bank of the river")
money = word_vector("i paid the cheque into my bank account")
loan = word_vector("the bank approved my loan this morning")
cos = lambda a, b: F.cosine_similarity(a, b, dim=0).item()
print(f"bank (account) vs bank (loan):  {cos(money, loan):.2f}")
print(f"bank (account) vs bank (river): {cos(money, river):.2f}")
print(f"bank (loan)    vs bank (river): {cos(loan, river):.2f}")
What you should see
bank (account) vs bank (loan):  0.83
bank (account) vs bank (river): 0.60
bank (loan)    vs bank (river): 0.58

The two financial uses are more similar to each other than either is to the river bank. Same token, same starting embedding, different final vectors: that is a contextual embedding, built by attention (Lesson 7.17).

Step 4 · Sentence embeddings with mean pooling

To compare whole sentences, you need one vector per sentence. The standard recipe: run an encoder, then average its token vectors (mean pooling), ignoring padding. Models such as all-MiniLM-L6-v2 (22 million parameters, 384 numbers per sentence) were fine-tuned on hundreds of millions of sentence pairs so that this average captures meaning:

emb_tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
emb_model = AutoModel.from_pretrained("sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2")
emb_model.eval()

def embed(texts):
    enc = emb_tok(texts, padding=True, truncation=True, return_tensors="pt")
    with torch.no_grad():
        hidden = emb_model(**enc).last_hidden_state               # (sentences, tokens, 384)
    mask = enc["attention_mask"].unsqueeze(-1).float()            # 1 for real tokens, 0 for padding
    vectors = (hidden * mask).sum(dim=1) / mask.sum(dim=1)        # average over real tokens only
    return F.normalize(vectors, dim=-1)                           # length 1, so dot product = cosine

v = embed(["my parcel never arrived", "the package did not get delivered", "how do I reset my password"])
print("shape:", tuple(v.shape))
print(f"parcel never arrived ~ package not delivered: {v[0] @ v[1]:.2f}")
print(f"parcel never arrived ~ reset my password:     {v[0] @ v[2]:.2f}")
What you should see
shape: (3, 384)
parcel never arrived ~ package not delivered: 0.68
parcel never arrived ~ reset my password:     0.15
Reading this output
Reading this code
  • padding=True makes all sentences in a batch the same length by adding padding tokens; attention_mask marks which tokens are real. Averaging without the mask would mix padding into the result, a common bug.
  • "my parcel never arrived" and "the package did not get delivered" share almost no words, yet their vectors are close, because the model learned that they mean the same thing. That is exactly what TF-IDF could not do in Lesson 5.32.
  • sentence-transformers, a library that wraps this recipe in one call (model.encode(texts)), is what most code uses; here you see what it does inside.

Step 5 · Fixing the search engine from Lesson 5.32

The same six FAQs and queries as Lesson 5.32, now searched by meaning:

faqs = ["How do I track my order?", "How long does a refund take to reach my account?",
        "What should I do if my item arrived damaged?", "How do I change my delivery address?",
        "How do I reset my password?", "Can I cancel an order after it has shipped?"]
faq_vectors = embed(faqs)

for query in ["my order has not arrived, where can i track it", "forgot my password",
              "the parcel came broken", "i moved house last week"]:
    scores = faq_vectors @ embed([query])[0]
    best = int(scores.argmax())
    print(f"{query!r:<50} -> {faqs[best]!r}  score {scores[best]:.2f}")
What you should see
'my order has not arrived, where can i track it'   -> 'How do I track my order?'  score 0.86
'forgot my password'                               -> 'How do I reset my password?'  score 0.81
'the parcel came broken'                           -> 'What should I do if my item arrived damaged?'  score 0.61
'i moved house last week'                          -> 'How do I change my delivery address?'  score 0.33

"The parcel came broken", which scored 0 against every FAQ with TF-IDF, now finds the damaged-item FAQ. "I moved house last week" shares no word at all with "How do I change my delivery address?" and still finds it. This is semantic search, the retrieval half of RAGlater · 10.45 (Lesson 14.71). Keyword search still wins on exact codes and rare names, which is why production systems often combine both (hybrid searchlater · 5.38, Lesson 5.38).

Step 6 · Embeddings as features: the ticket classifier again

In Lesson 5.32, a bag-of-words classifier scored 82% on ticket phrasings it had never seen. Replace the word counts with sentence embeddings and keep everything else the same (the same data, the same held-out templates, the same logistic regression):

import random
import numpy as np
from sklearn.linear_model import LogisticRegression

TEMPLATES = {
 "where": ["where is my order {id}", "my order {id} has not arrived yet", "order {id} still not delivered", "when will my {item} arrive",
           "it has been {n} days and no delivery", "tracking for {id} has not updated since {day}", "still waiting for my {item}",
           "the delivery was due on {day} but nothing came", "can you check the status of order {id}", "my parcel is late, it was promised for {day}",
           "is my {item} lost? no update for {n} days", "courier says delivered but i never got order {id}"],
 "refund": ["i want a refund for order {id}", "please refund my money for the {item}", "refund not received after {n} days",
            "how long does a refund take", "i was charged twice for order {id}, please refund", "cancel order {id} and give my money back",
            "money was deducted but order {id} failed", "where is my refund for the returned {item}", "i returned the {item} {n} days ago and got no refund",
            "can i get my money back for order {id}", "the refund amount for {id} is wrong", "please reverse the extra charge on my card"],
 "damaged": ["my {item} arrived broken", "the {item} was damaged in transit", "received a cracked {item} in order {id}",
             "the box was crushed and the {item} is ruined", "item in order {id} is defective", "the {item} stopped working after one day",
             "glass {item} came shattered", "package was torn open and the {item} scratched", "wrong colour and a dent on the {item}",
             "the {item} is leaking", "screen of the {item} has a crack", "parts missing from my {item}"],
 "account": ["how do i change my delivery address", "update the phone number on my account", "i cannot log in to my account",
             "reset my password please", "change the address for order {id}", "how do i delete my account", "my email address is wrong on the account",
             "add a second address to my profile", "otp not received while signing in", "how to change the name on my account",
             "my account got locked after three tries", "set a default delivery address"],
}
ITEMS = ["phone", "lamp", "mixer", "kettle", "shoes", "jacket", "headphones", "mug", "vase", "watch"]
DAYS = ["monday", "tuesday", "wednesday", "thursday", "friday", "saturday", "sunday"]
rng = random.Random(0)
rows = []
for label, templates in TEMPLATES.items():
    for t_id, template in enumerate(templates):
        for _ in range(8):
            rows.append((template.format(id=f"sk-{rng.randint(10000, 99999)}", item=rng.choice(ITEMS),
                                         day=rng.choice(DAYS), n=rng.randint(2, 15)), label, t_id))

train = [(t, l) for t, l, tid in rows if tid not in {2, 6, 10}]
test = [(t, l) for t, l, tid in rows if tid in {2, 6, 10}]

X_train = embed([t for t, _ in train]).numpy()
X_test = embed([t for t, _ in test]).numpy()
clf = LogisticRegression(max_iter=2000, C=10).fit(X_train, [l for _, l in train])
print("accuracy on unseen phrasings:", round(clf.score(X_test, [l for _, l in test]), 3))

for t in ["the box came smashed and the glass is in pieces",
          "i do not want a refund, i just want my order to arrive",
          "the courier left it at the wrong address"]:
    print(f"{clf.predict(embed([t]).numpy())[0]:<8} {t}")
What you should see
accuracy on unseen phrasings: 0.969
damaged  the box came smashed and the glass is in pieces
refund   i do not want a refund, i just want my order to arrive
account  the courier left it at the wrong address

Accuracy on unseen phrasings rises from 82% to about 97%. The embeddings already "know" that "shattered" is like "broken" and "locked" is about accounts, so the classifier needs no examples of those words. Negation and fine distinctions are still hard: "I do not want a refund" is still labelled refund, and "left it at the wrong address" lands in account because it is about an address. Embeddings capture topic better than logic; a language model reading the whole ticket (the Talking to models: APIs, messages, prompts and context section) handles such cases better, at a much higher cost per ticket. This pattern (a frozen pretrained encoder plus a tiny classifier trained on its embeddings) is one of the cheapest, most effective tools in applied NLP. Fine-tuning the whole encoder on your labels usually does better still (Lesson 3.22 fine-tunes embedding models).

Step 7 · Which family for which job

Job Usual choice today
Search, RAG retrieval, deduplication, clustering encoder embedding model (Lesson 14.71)
Reranking search results encoder cross-encoder: reads query and document together and outputs a score (Lesson 5.38)
High-volume classification with labelled data small encoder, fine-tuned, or embeddings plus a classifier (this lesson)
Chat, writing, reasoning, agents, few-shot tasks decoder-only LLM (the Talking to models: APIs, messages, prompts and context and Agents from first principles sections)
Speech to text encoder-decoder (Whisper, the Multimodal, voice and generative media section)
Translation, summarisation usually a decoder LLM now; encoder-decoders where cost and latency matter
The same idea as a formula
  • masked language modelling: maximise log p(x_i | x_{\i}) for randomly hidden positions i, using tokens on both sides
  • mean pooling: s = Σᵢ mᵢ hᵢ / Σᵢ mᵢ, with mᵢ = 1 for real tokens and 0 for padding
  • semantic similarity: cos(s₁, s₂) = s₁ · s₂ after normalising to length 1
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "BERT is an older, worse GPT."
    ✓ It is a different tool. Encoders read in both directions and are often better and far cheaper for classification, embeddings and reranking.
  • ✗ "Any language model's average hidden state is a good sentence embedding."
    ✓ Averages from models not trained for similarity are much weaker. Embedding models are fine-tuned specifically so that cosine similarity reflects meaning.
  • ✗ "Semantic search makes keyword search obsolete."
    ✓ Embeddings can miss exact identifiers, codes and rare names. Hybrid search uses both.

Exercises

Predict before you run

What will fill("The courier [MASK] the parcel at the door.", top_k=3) return as its top guesses? (Solution P1.)

Make it your own

In Step 5, add three queries of your own, including one in another language you know (for example "mera parcel toota hua aaya", Hindi for "my parcel came broken"). Which FAQs do they match? (Solution M1.)

Debugging exercise

A colleague's semantic search ranks short FAQs oddly. Their embed function averages last_hidden_state with .mean(dim=1) and no mask, and they embed all FAQs in one padded batch. What is wrong? (Solution D1.)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Fill in the verb

Words like "left", "delivered" and "dropped", with "left" usually on top: verbs that fit both "the courier " and " the parcel at the door". Run it to see the exact scores. The model needed the words after the gap to choose a verb that takes "the parcel" as its object.

SolutionM1 · Your own queries

English paraphrases usually match well. all-MiniLM-L6-v2 was trained mostly on English, so the Hindi-in-Latin-letters query may still match the damaged-item FAQ through shared or similar tokens, but less reliably. For multilingual users, choose a multilingual embedding model (for example paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2 or a larger multilingual model) and test it on real queries in each language.

SolutionD1 · Averaging the padding

In a padded batch, short sentences are filled with padding tokens up to the length of the longest one. .mean(dim=1) averages those padding vectors in too, so short FAQs are pulled towards a common "padding" direction, and their similarity scores depend on the other FAQs in the batch. Use the attention mask to average only real tokens, as embed does in Step 4.

Recap
  • Encoders see the whole text in both directions and suit understanding: classification, tagging, embeddings, reranking.
  • Decoders see only earlier tokens and generate text; scaled up, they became today's chat models.
  • Encoder-decoders read an input and write a new output: translation, summarisation, speech recognition.
  • Inside an encoder, the same word gets different vectors in different contexts; mean pooling with the attention mask turns token vectors into a sentence vector.
  • Sentence embeddings power semantic search and make strong features for classifiers; on unseen phrasings they beat bag of words.
