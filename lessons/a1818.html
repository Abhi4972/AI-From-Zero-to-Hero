In plain words first
The idea in one line

Three major-lab frameworks define the 2026 industry governance of frontier capability. Anthropic Responsible Scaling Policy v3.0 (February 2026) introduces tiered AI Safety Levels (ASL-1 through ASL-5+), modeled on biosafety levels, with ASL-3 activated May 2025 for CBRN-relevant models. OpenAI Preparedness Framework v2 (April 2025) defines five criteria for tracked capabilities and separates Capabilities Reports from Safeguards Reports. DeepMind Frontier Safety Framework v3.0 (September 2025) introduces Critical Capability Levels including a new Harmful Manipulation CCL. All three now include competitor-adjustment clauses allowing deferral if peer labs ship without comparable safeguards. Cross-lab alignment remains structural, not terminological: "Capability Thresholds," "High Capability thresholds," and "Critical Capability Levels" denote analogous constructs.

The problem

In plain words first

A frontier safety framework is a lab's own written rulebook for when a model has become dangerous enough to need stronger safeguards. Each one defines capability thresholds (for example, "could meaningfully help someone make a biological weapon"), the evaluations run to check whether a model has crossed one, and the safeguards required once it has, from tighter security around the model's weights to classifiers that block certain requests. Anthropic calls its version the Responsible Scaling Policy (with AI Safety Levels, ASLs), OpenAI the Preparedness Framework, and Google DeepMind the Frontier Safety Framework (with Critical Capability Levels, CCLs). They are voluntary and keep changing; this lesson compares their structure and the hardest question they share: what a lab should do if competitors move ahead without the same safeguards.

Lessons 7-17 establish that deception is possible, dual-use capability exists, and evaluation has limits. A lab with a frontier-capable model needs an internal governance structure that: - Defines thresholds for when new safeguards are required. - Defines required evaluations before scaling. - Describes what a safety case looks like. - Handles the race-dynamic problem (if competitors ship without safeguards, what do you do?).

The three 2025-2026 frameworks are the state of the art — imperfect, evolving, and aligned enough across labs that the governance question is now whether the frameworks are adequate, not whether they exist.

Idea 1 · Anthropic Responsible Scaling Policy v3.0 (February 2026)

ASL structure: - ASL-1: systems with no meaningful catastrophic risk. - ASL-2: the baseline standard for frontier models before any capability threshold is crossed. - ASL-3: required once a model could meaningfully help with chemical or biological weapons; first activated in May 2025 with Claude Opus 4. - Higher levels: tied to capabilities such as automating AI research.

What changed in v3.0 (24 February 2026), as Anthropic and outside analysts describe it: - Two sets of mitigations. Measures Anthropic will take regardless of what other developers do, and a broader capabilities-to-mitigations map that it argues would manage the risks only if adopted across the industry. The older policy's higher safeguard levels, which would be very hard to meet alone, are no longer unilateral commitments. - Frontier Safety Roadmap. Public, non-binding goals across security, alignment, safeguards and policy, with progress graded openly. - Risk Reports every 3 to 6 months, partly redacted, assessing overall risk from deployed models, with third-party review required in some circumstances. - AI R&D. The earlier AI R&D-4 and AI R&D-5 thresholds are replaced by a single threshold for models that could roughly double the pace of AI progress. The earlier commitment to an "affirmative case" at the AI R&D-4 threshold was dropped in favour of an industry-wide recommendation. - Pause language removed. Critics noted that the older pledge not to train or deploy models without adequate safeguards no longer appears in the same form.

Idea 2 · OpenAI Preparedness Framework v2 (April 15, 2025)

Five criteria for tracked capabilities: - Plausible. Reasonable threat model exists. - Measurable. Empirical evaluation possible. - Severe. Harm is large. - Net-new. Not a pre-existing risk scaled up. - Instantaneous-or-irremediable. Harm occurs fast or cannot be undone.

Capabilities that meet all five are tracked. Others are not.

Other PF v2 structure: - Separate Capabilities Reports (what the model can do) from Safeguards Reports (what controls exist). - Safety Advisory Group reviews. - Leadership approves; Board's Safety & Security Committee oversees. - "Adjustment clause": OpenAI may reduce requirements if another lab ships without comparable safeguards.

Idea 3 · DeepMind Frontier Safety Framework v3.0 (September 2025)

Critical Capability Levels (CCLs) by domain: - Bioweapon Uplift - Cyber Uplift - ML R&D Acceleration - Harmful Manipulation (new in v3.0): models that could substantially change beliefs/behavior in high-stakes contexts.

v2.0 (February 2025) added a Deceptive Alignment section and higher security levels for ML R&D CCLs.

Idea 4 · Cross-lab alignment

  • Anthropic "Capability Thresholds."
  • DeepMind "Critical Capability Levels."
  • OpenAI "High Capability thresholds."

No industry-standard terminology. Structurally aligned: each of the three frameworks defines tiered capability thresholds with published evaluation criteria. All three include competitor-adjustment clauses (2025 onward).

Idea 5 · Safety cases

A safety case is a structured written argument, backed by evidence, that a specific deployment is acceptably safe. Clymer et al. (2024) group the arguments it can rest on into kinds, in rough order of how much they depend on trusting the model:

  • Inability. The model cannot do the harmful thing at all, shown by capability evaluations (and, for CBRN, possibly unlearning).
  • Control. The model might try, but the deployment would catch or block it (Lesson 10): monitoring, restricted permissions, audits.
  • Trustworthiness. The model can and is not stopped, but would not try, shown by alignment evidence such as interpretability. This is the hardest to establish today (Lessons 7–9).

Real safety cases combine them. A CBRN case for today's models leans on inability plus deployment safeguards; a case about deceptive alignment cannot rest on behaviour tests alone and usually falls back on control.

Idea 6 · The race-dynamic problem

Competitor-adjustment clauses are controversial. Critics argue they create a race to the bottom: if all three labs will reduce requirements when a competitor defects, the equilibrium shifts toward defection. Defenders argue the alternative (unilateral safeguards) produces worse outcomes if the defecting lab is less safety-conscious.

UK AISI, US CAISI, and EU AI Office (Lesson 18.24) are the external governance counterparts. The lab frameworks are voluntary; the regulatory frameworks are emerging.

Worked example
Why frameworks evaluate before the threshold

A lab evaluates its models each time effective training compute grows 4×. Capability on a dangerous-skill evaluation rises by about 8 points per 4× step.

  1. The threshold of concern is 70 points. The last evaluated model scored 64.
  2. The next model, one 4× step later, will score about 72: past the threshold before anyone tested it.
  3. So frameworks set an earlier warning level (a buffer). With a warning level at 60, the 64-point model already triggers preparation of the stronger safeguards before the next step.
  4. The buffer must be at least one step's worth of capability growth (here 8 points), plus measurement error. Too small a buffer and thresholds are crossed between evaluations.

Predict before you run

Before running the code below: capability grows by 8 points per evaluation step, with measurement noise of about 3 points. If the warning level sits 4 points below the threshold, how often does a model cross the threshold without any warning first?

See it drawn
How big a buffer is enough
import random
random.seed(2)
def missed(buffer, step=8.0, noise=3.0, threshold=70.0, trials=20000):
    misses = 0
    for _ in range(trials):
        cap = random.uniform(threshold - 3 * step, threshold - step)
        while True:
            measured = cap + random.gauss(0, noise)
            if measured >= threshold - buffer:          # warning fired at this evaluation
                break
            cap += step
            if cap >= threshold:                        # the next model is past the threshold, untested
                misses += 1; break
    return misses / trials

for buffer in (0, 4, 8, 12):
    print(f"warning level {buffer:2d} points below threshold: crossed without warning {missed(buffer):6.1%}")
What you should see
warning level  0 points below threshold: crossed without warning  85.2%
warning level  4 points below threshold: crossed without warning  49.2%
warning level  8 points below threshold: crossed without warning  14.5%
warning level 12 points below threshold: crossed without warning   1.3%
Reading this output

With no buffer, most models (85%) cross the threshold between evaluations. A 4-point buffer still lets about half through, because it is smaller than one step of growth. At 8 points, one full step, misses fall to about one in seven, and a 12-point buffer that also covers measurement noise makes them rare (about 1%). The principle carries over to the real frameworks: warning levels and evaluation frequency have to be set together with the pace of capability growth.

Given a new safety framework, policy, or release note, compare it against Anthropic RSP v3.0, OpenAI PF v2, DeepMind FSF v3.0 along the five structural axes.

Produce:

1. Tier structure. Does the framework define discrete capability thresholds? Are they per-domain (FSF-style) or global (RSP-style)?
2. CBRN threshold. What CBRN evaluation is required? Does it reference WMDP (Lesson 17) or an equivalent? Does it include an elicitation study?
3. AI R&D threshold. Is there a model-autonomous-research threshold? Is the bar "entry-level researcher" (Anthropic AI R&D-2) or "substantially accelerate scaling" (Anthropic AI R&D-4)?
4. Competitor-adjustment. Does the framework allow reduction of requirements if competitors ship without comparable safeguards? Frame as race-dynamic or as incentive-compatibility, as appropriate.
5. Safety-case structure. Is a written safety case required? Does it target monitoring, illegibility, or incapability? What is the evidence bar?

Hard rejects:
- Any safety framework without per-tier capability thresholds.
- Any framework that omits an external governance cross-reference (UK AISI, US CAISI, EU AI Office).
- Any framework that claims "we align with all published frameworks" without specific threshold numbers.

Refusal rules:
- If the user asks which framework is "best," refuse the ranking and point to structural alignment.
- If the user asks for a numeric threshold recommendation, refuse — thresholds are lab-specific and depend on their measurement infrastructure.

Output: a one-page side-by-side comparison against the three frameworks, flagged gaps, and one specific threshold recommendation to add. Cite RSP v3.0, PF v2, FSF v3.0 once each.

Solutions

SolutionP2 · The competitor clause, both ways

For: a lab that holds back while a less careful lab ships gains nothing for safety and loses influence over norms; the clause keeps careful labs at the frontier, where their safety research and policy voice matter. It assumes the careful lab's presence improves outcomes. Against: if every lab lowers its bar when another does, the weakest lab sets everyone's standard, a race to the bottom. It assumes the clause will be invoked often and that commitments without it would hold.

SolutionB1 · Check the current version

Read each framework's current text, not a summary. Anthropic's RSP v3.0 (February 2026) separates measures it will take regardless of others from an industry-wide map, and outside analysts noted that the earlier "do not train or deploy without adequate safeguards" language no longer appears in the same form. A blanket "all three pause" claim needs a quote and a version date for each lab.
