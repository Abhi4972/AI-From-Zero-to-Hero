In plain words first
The idea in one line

Voice cloning reads your text in someone else's voice. Voice conversion rewrites your voice into someone else's while preserving what you said. Both hang on the same decomposition: separate speaker identity from content.

The problem

In plain words first

A recording mixes three things: what is said (content), who says it (speaker identity) and how (prosody: timing, pitch, emotion). Voice cloning takes content from text and identity from a short sample of someone's voice. Voice conversion takes content from one recording and identity from another person, so a sentence spoken by A comes out sounding like B. Both work by pulling those factors apart and recombining them. The same technology powers dubbing and voices for people who have lost theirs, and also phone scams, so the engineering includes consent checks, inaudible watermarks and detectors, and since August 2026 the EU requires generated audio to be marked. This lesson runs a real open voice-conversion model on a LibriSpeech recording and measures what it keeps and what it changes, then builds a working audio watermark from scratch and attacks it. Nothing is trained.

In 2026, a 5-second audio clip is enough to produce a high-quality clone of anyone's voice with a consumer GPU. ElevenLabs, F5-TTS, OpenVoice v2 and XTTS v2 all offer zero-shot or few-shot cloning (Meta described Voicebox in 2023 but did not release it, citing misuse risk). The technology is a blessing (accessibility TTS, dubbing, assistive voices) and a weapon (scam calls, political deepfakes, IP theft).

Two closely-related tasks:

  • Voice cloning (TTS-side): text + 5-second reference voice → audio in that voice.
  • Voice conversion (speech-side): source audio (person A saying X) + reference voice of person B → audio of B saying X.

Both factor a waveform into (content, speaker, prosody) and recombine content from one source with speaker from another.

Key constraints: since 2 August 2026, the EU AI Act (Article 50) requires providers of systems that generate synthetic audio to mark the output in a machine-readable, detectable way (systems already on the market before that date have until 2 December 2026), and deployers must disclose deepfakes of real people. California's AB 2905 (signed 2024) requires calls that use AI-generated voices to say so. Laws such as Tennessee's ELVIS Act (2024) protect a person's voice as part of their likeness, and platforms require consent to clone a real voice. Build marking and consent checks into the pipeline, not around it.

The idea

Zero-shot cloning. Pass a 5-second clip to a model that has been trained on thousands of speakers. The speaker encoder maps the clip to a speaker embedding; the TTS decoder conditions on that embedding plus text.

Used by: F5-TTS (2024), YourTTS (2022), XTTS v2 (2023), OpenVoice v2 (2024).

Few-shot fine-tuning. Record 5-30 minutes of the target voice. LoRA-fine-tune a base model for an hour. Quality leaps from "okay" to "indistinguishable". ElevenLabs offers this as Professional Voice Cloning; open communities do it with F5-TTS and XTTS v2.

Voice conversion (VC). Two families:

  • Recognition-synthesis. Run ASR-like model to extract content representation (e.g., soft phoneme posteriors, PPGs), then resynthesize with target speaker embedding. Robust to language and accent. Used by PPG-based VC systems and Diff-HierVC (2023); kNN-VC (2023) does the same with self-supervised WavLM features in place of phoneme posteriors.
  • Disentanglement. Train an autoencoder that separates content, speaker, and prosody in latent space at the bottleneck. Swap speaker embedding at inference. Lower quality but faster. Used by AutoVC (2019), VITS-VC variants.

Neural codec-based cloning (2024+). VALL-E, VALL-E 2, NaturalSpeech 3, VoiceBox — treat audio as discrete tokens from SoundStream / EnCodec, train a large autoregressive or flow-matching model over codec tokens. These were research systems from Microsoft and Meta, mostly not released because of misuse concerns; the token-based idea lives on in open models and in speech LLMs (Lessons 6.10 and 6.13).

The same idea as a formula

Factorisation. Write an utterance's features as a function of three latent factors, X=g(c,s,p): content c, speaker s, prosody p. Cloning computes X^=g(ctext​,sref​,p^​); conversion computes X^=g(csource​,starget​,psource​). Everything hinges on encoders that recover c without s and s without c.

Speaker-conditioned decoding. In SpeechT5 and similar models, a fixed-length speaker embedding s (an x-vector) is projected and added to (or concatenated with) every decoder step: ht′​=ht​+Ws, so the same content produces different voices.

kNN-VC. With per-frame self-supervised features qt​ of the source and the set M of all frames of the target speaker,

y^​t​=k1​m∈kNNk​(qt​,M)∑​m

then a vocoder trained on such features produces audio. Because every output frame is made of real target frames, the voice is the target's; content survives because WavLM features of the same phone are close across speakers.

Evaluation. Speaker similarity SECS=cos(e(x^),e(xtarget​)) with a speaker-verification model e; content as WER of an ASR transcript; naturalness by listeners.

Spread-spectrum watermark. For bit bj​∈{−1,+1}, add a secret noise pattern wj​ (from a keyed generator, filtered to a band) at low amplitude: y=x+α∑j​bj​wj​. Detection correlates: b^j​=sign(⟨y,wj​⟩). The watermark part contributes α∥wj​∥2, growing with segment length N; the host audio contributes noise of size about σ∥wj​∥, growing only with N​. Longer segments or a quieter host band make bits reliable at lower loudness.

Worked example
A watermark budget by hand

Bits per second. 32 bits in 19 seconds of speech is about 1.7 bits per second, 9,500 samples per bit at 16 kHz.

Correlation gain. If the watermark sits 30 dB below the speech, its amplitude ratio is 10−30/20=0.032. Over N=9,500 samples, the watermark term grows like N and the speech interference like N​, so the detection signal-to-noise ratio is about 0.032×9500​=3.1 standard deviations: most bits right, a few at risk. Filtering both to a band where speech is quiet raises the ratio further, which is why the run below gets every bit.

Chance agreement. With no watermark present, each detected bit matches the payload with probability ½. For 32 bits, the expected match is 16 with standard deviation 32×0.25​=2.8; a "detection" of 22/32 is only about 2 standard deviations, so real systems test the correlation strength against a threshold before reading bits.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Voice conversion changes the voice and keeps everything else."
    ✓ It changes the speaker factor; prosody and some content often change too. Measure WER after conversion.
  • ✗ "A watermark proves audio is fake."
    ✓ It proves the audio came from a system that marked it. Unmarked synthetic audio exists, so detection and provenance (signed metadata) are separate layers.
  • ✗ "A cloning model only needs the reference audio."
    ✓ Many (F5-TTS among them) also need the reference transcript, exactly matching, to align the prompt.

Idea 1 · The ethics bit, not a bolt-on

Watermarking. Meta's AudioSeal (2024, a 16-bit message), Sony's SilentCipher (2024) and Resemble AI's PerTh (open-sourced as resemble-perth) embed a short ID imperceptibly in the audio and are designed to survive common re-encoding and edits, to varying degrees. The run under Step 4 builds a simple one from scratch.

Consent gates. Must pair every cloned output with a verifiable consent record. "I, Rohit, on 2026-04-22, authorize this voice for X purpose." Store in a tamper-evident log.

Detection. AASIST, RawNet2 and wav2vec 2.0-based classifiers are the standard open detectors, benchmarked in the ASVspoof challenges (ASVspoof 5 ran in 2024). They do well on generators like those they were trained on and worse on new ones (Lesson 6.16), so detection complements watermarking rather than replacing it.

Idea 2 · Numbers (2026)

Published SECS and WER figures for cloning models come from different test sets, prompts and speaker-verification models, so they cannot be compared across papers, and SECS values depend strongly on which embedding model computes them. Compare candidate models on your own reference voices with one SV model and one ASR model, as the runs below do, and confirm with listening tests.

def clone_pipeline(ref_audio, text, target_embedder, tts_model):
    speaker_emb = target_embedder.encode(ref_audio)
    mel = tts_model(text, speaker=speaker_emb)
    return vocoder(mel)

import torch
# kNN-VC (Baas, van Niekerk and Kamper, 2023), loaded from its GitHub repository via torch.hub
knn_vc = torch.hub.load("bshall/knn-vc", "knn_vc", prematched=True, trust_repo=True, pretrained=True)

KNN-VC runs WavLM to extract per-frame embeddings for source and target pool, then replaces each source frame with its nearest neighbor in the pool. Non-parametric, works with a minute of target speech.

Not run here: kNN-VC downloads its code and weights from GitHub at load time. The run below uses SpeechT5's voice-conversion model through transformers, which converts with an x-vector instead of a pool of target frames.

Converting a real recording to two other voices

Source: a LibriSpeech sentence by one male speaker. Targets: x-vectors of two CMU ARCTIC speakers, slt (female) and bdl (male). For each conversion, Whisper's transcript and WER, and speaker similarity (WavLM base+ SV) to the source speaker and to a TTS sample in each target voice.

import io, time
import numpy as np, torch, soundfile as sf, librosa
from datasets import load_dataset, Audio
from transformers import (SpeechT5Processor, SpeechT5ForSpeechToSpeech, SpeechT5ForTextToSpeech, SpeechT5HifiGan,
                          WhisperProcessor, WhisperForConditionalGeneration, AutoFeatureExtractor, WavLMForXVector)

libri = load_dataset("hf-internal-testing/librispeech_asr_dummy", "clean", split="validation").cast_column("audio", Audio(decode=False))
def clip(i):
    return sf.read(io.BytesIO(libri[i]["audio"]["bytes"]), dtype="float32")[0]
xv = load_dataset("Matthijs/cmu-arctic-xvectors", revision="refs/convert/parquet", split="validation")
names = [f.split("_arctic")[0].replace("cmu_us_", "") for f in xv["filename"]]
voice = {s: torch.tensor(xv[names.index(s)]["xvector"]).unsqueeze(0) for s in ["slt", "bdl"]}

vc_proc = SpeechT5Processor.from_pretrained("microsoft/speecht5_vc")
vc = SpeechT5ForSpeechToSpeech.from_pretrained("microsoft/speecht5_vc").eval()           # speech in -> mel out
vocoder = SpeechT5HifiGan.from_pretrained("microsoft/speecht5_hifigan").eval()
tts_proc = SpeechT5Processor.from_pretrained("microsoft/speecht5_tts")
tts = SpeechT5ForTextToSpeech.from_pretrained("microsoft/speecht5_tts").eval()
wproc = WhisperProcessor.from_pretrained("openai/whisper-base.en")
asr = WhisperForConditionalGeneration.from_pretrained("openai/whisper-base.en").eval()
sfe = AutoFeatureExtractor.from_pretrained("microsoft/wavlm-base-plus-sv")
sv = WavLMForXVector.from_pretrained("microsoft/wavlm-base-plus-sv").eval()

def transcribe(w):
    with torch.no_grad():
        return wproc.batch_decode(asr.generate(wproc(w, sampling_rate=16000, return_tensors="pt").input_features),
                                  skip_special_tokens=True)[0].strip()
def wer(ref, hyp):
    r, h = wproc.tokenizer.normalize(ref).split(), wproc.tokenizer.normalize(hyp).split(); d = list(range(len(h) + 1))
    for i, a in enumerate(r, 1):
        prev, d[0] = d[0], i
        for j, b in enumerate(h, 1):
            prev, d[j] = d[j], min(d[j] + 1, d[j - 1] + 1, prev + (a != b))
    return d[-1] / len(r)
def spk(w):
    with torch.no_grad():
        return torch.nn.functional.normalize(sv(**sfe(w, sampling_rate=16000, return_tensors="pt")).embeddings[0], dim=-1)
def convert(w, target):
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    with torch.no_grad():
        return vc.generate_speech(vc_proc(audio=w, sampling_rate=16000, return_tensors="pt")["input_values"],
                                  voice[target], vocoder=vocoder).numpy()
def say(text, target):
    torch.manual_seed(0)
    with torch.no_grad():
        return tts.generate_speech(tts_proc(text=text, return_tensors="pt")["input_ids"], voice[target], vocoder=vocoder).numpy()

t0 = time.time()
src = clip(1); text = libri[1]["text"]
refs = {s: say("Author of the danger trail, Philip Steels, etcetera.", s) for s in voice}   # a sample of each target voice
e_src = spk(src)
print(f"source: LibriSpeech speaker 1272, {len(src) / 16000:.1f} s: {transcribe(src)!r}")
for target in voice:
    out = convert(src, target)
    print(f"\nconverted to {target}: {len(out) / 16000:.1f} s, Whisper hears {transcribe(out)!r}, WER {wer(text, transcribe(out)):.0%}")
    print(f"  speaker similarity to the source speaker {float(spk(out) @ e_src):.2f}, "
          f"to a TTS sample of {target} {float(spk(out) @ spk(refs[target])):.2f}, "
          f"to a TTS sample of the other voice {float(spk(out) @ spk(refs[[s for s in voice if s != target][0]])):.2f}")
print(f"\n({time.time() - t0:.0f} s)")

A watermark from scratch, under attack

32 secret bits hidden in 19 seconds of LibriSpeech speech, 30 dB below the speech, either in the 4–7.5 kHz band or in 1–3.5 kHz. Detection uses only the key. Each version is attacked with noise, a volume change, resampling through 8 kHz, and 8-bit quantisation.

from scipy.signal import butter, sosfiltfilt

# A blind spread-spectrum watermark from scratch: each bit is a secret pseudo-random noise pattern,
# added with sign +1 or -1, quietly, in one frequency band. Detection needs only the key, not the original.
def band(x, lo, hi, sr=16000):
    return sosfiltfilt(butter(6, [lo, hi], btype="band", fs=sr, output="sos"), x)

def embed(x, bits, key=1234, lo=4000, hi=7500, snr_db=30):
    seg = len(x) // len(bits); rng = np.random.default_rng(key)
    chips = band(rng.standard_normal(seg * len(bits)), lo, hi).reshape(len(bits), seg)
    wm = np.concatenate([(1 if b else -1) * c for b, c in zip(bits, chips)])
    wm *= np.sqrt(np.mean(x ** 2) / np.mean(wm ** 2) / 10 ** (snr_db / 10))    # set the watermark's loudness
    y = x.copy(); y[: len(wm)] += wm
    return y

def detect(y, n_bits, key=1234, lo=4000, hi=7500):
    seg = len(y) // n_bits; rng = np.random.default_rng(key)
    chips = band(rng.standard_normal(seg * n_bits), lo, hi).reshape(n_bits, seg)
    yb = band(y, lo, hi)[: seg * n_bits].reshape(n_bits, seg)                    # look only where the mark lives
    return [int((r * c).sum() > 0) for r, c in zip(yb, chips)]

host = np.concatenate([clip(i) for i in range(3)])                                 # about 19 s of real speech
bits = list(np.random.default_rng(7).integers(0, 2, 32))
attacks = {"none": lambda y: y,
           "noise at 20 dB SNR": lambda y: y + np.random.default_rng(1).standard_normal(len(y)) * np.sqrt(np.mean(y ** 2) / 100),
           "volume x0.3": lambda y: 0.3 * y,
           "resample 16k -> 8k -> 16k": lambda y: librosa.resample(librosa.resample(y, orig_sr=16000, target_sr=8000), orig_sr=8000, target_sr=16000)[: len(y)],
           "16-bit then 8-bit quantisation": lambda y: np.round(y * 127) / 127}
for lo, hi in [(4000, 7500), (1000, 3500)]:
    y = embed(host, bits, lo=lo, hi=hi)
    print(f"watermark in {lo}-{hi} Hz, 30 dB below the speech (SNR {10 * np.log10(np.mean(host ** 2) / np.mean((y - host) ** 2)):.1f} dB):")
    for name, attack in attacks.items():
        got = detect(attack(y), 32, lo=lo, hi=hi)
        print(f"   {name:32s} bits correct {sum(g == b for g, b in zip(got, bits))}/32")
    print(f"   {'no watermark (false alarm check)':32s} bits matching by chance {sum(g == b for g, b in zip(detect(host, 32, lo=lo, hi=hi), bits))}/32")

Reading this output

Both versions survive added noise at 20 dB, a volume change and 8-bit quantisation with all 32 bits. Resampling through 8 kHz removes everything above 4 kHz, and the high-band watermark loses 2 bits (it survives at all only because the band filter leaks a little energy below 4 kHz); the 1–3.5 kHz version keeps all 32, at the cost of sitting where speech is loudest and the mark is easier to hear. The last lines are the false-alarm check: reading "bits" from unmarked speech gives 22/32 and 16/32 by chance, so a real detector first tests whether the correlation is strong enough to say "watermarked" at all, and production systems add synchronisation (to survive cropping and time shifts) and error-correcting codes.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Cloning in production. ElevenLabs (instant and professional cloning), F5-TTS, OpenVoice v2 and XTTS v2 are the common choices; most commercial services require the speaker's recorded consent statement before cloning a voice.
  • Marking and provenance. Open watermarkers such as AudioSeal, SilentCipher and PerTh are used alongside C2PA content credentials (signed metadata stating which tool produced a file). Since 2 August 2026, the EU AI Act requires generated audio to be machine-detectably marked.

Sources: Commission guidance coverage of Article 50, AudioSeal, SpeechT5 voice conversion, kNN-VC, OpenVoice.

Pitfalls

  • Misaligned reference transcript. F5-TTS and similar require the reference text to match the reference audio exactly, punctuation included.
  • Reverberant reference. Echo kills the clone. Record dry, close-mic.
  • Emotional mismatch. Training reference "cheerful" produces cheerful clones of everything. Match reference emotion to target use.
  • Language leakage. Cloning an English speaker then asking the model to speak French often carries the accent anyway; use cross-lingual models such as XTTS v2.
  • No watermark. Since August 2026, generated audio offered in the EU must be marked in a machine-detectable way (AI Act Article 50).
Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Cloning and conversion both separate content, speaker and prosody, then recombine.
  • Measure identity (SECS with a speaker-verification model) and content (ASR WER) together; conversion trades one against the other.
  • A watermark is a keyed, quiet signal detected by correlation; robustness depends on band, loudness, segment length and synchronisation.
  • Detection of unmarked fakes is a separate, weaker layer.
  • Consent records and EU marking requirements belong inside the pipeline.

Given the task (language, reference length available, adaptation budget, license constraints, consent status, deployment scale), output:

1. Approach. Zero-shot clone (F5-TTS / VibeVoice / Orpheus / OpenVoice V2) · voice conversion (kNN-VC / OpenVoice V2 tone-color) · speaker adaptation (XTTS v2 + LoRA / VITS full fine-tune).
2. Reference prep. Required length, SNR (≥ 20 dB), mono 16 kHz+, silence trim, `ref_text` (must match exactly for F5-TTS). Reject music-bed references.
3. Consent artifact. Explicit recorded consent from voice owner. Template: name + date + purpose + scope + revocation procedure. Store 7+ years.
4. Watermark. AudioSeal-embedded 16-bit payload on every output. Configure detector in CI to verify presence before publishing audio.
5. Safety filters. Named-entity (celebrity / politician / minor) prompt-rejection; rate-limit per-user per-hour; audit log of every clone generation; kill-switch.

Refuse to ship cloning without a watermarking strategy. Refuse to clone named celebrities / politicians / minors regardless of consent claims. Refuse references under 3 s or SNR &lt; 20 dB. Refuse F5-TTS for commercial deployments (CC-BY-NC). Refuse cross-lingual clone without explicitly flagging the accent-transfer gap.

Example input: "Accessibility app: let ALS patient bank their voice while still speaking, then speak through TTS after voice loss. English, US."

Example output:
- Approach: OpenVoice V2 (MIT, zero-shot, 6 s reference). Accessibility use case with inherent consent; patient is voice owner.
- Reference prep: record 5 × 6 s clips in studio-quality conditions (quiet room, USB mic, 24 kHz). Store raw + transcripts. Build centroid reference for stability.
- Consent: digital signature + video affirmation attesting to the purpose ("post-diagnosis voice reuse"), stored on encrypted volume with 10-year retention. Revocation hotline.
- Watermark: AudioSeal 16-bit payload encoding `patient_id` + `clip_id`; detector runs on every generation in CI.
- Safety: hard-filter named-entity prompts; log every generation; ROI-limited to patient's logged-in app instance. No API exposure.

Exercises

  1. Easy. Run code/main.py. Demonstrates the speaker-embedding swap by computing the cosine between two "speakers" pre and post swap.
  2. Medium. Use OpenVoice v2 to clone your own voice. Measure SECS between reference and clone. Measure CER via Whisper.
  3. Hard. Apply SilentCipher watermark to 20 clones, run them through 128 kbps MP3 encode+decode, detect the payload. Report bit-accuracy.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Yes to the voice, no to every word

Yes: the converted audio scores 0.91–0.92 against its target voice and much lower against the source speaker (0.42 and 0.67). No: Whisper's WER is 45% and 27%, with words such as "Quilter's" and "matter" damaged. Changing identity without touching content is the hard part of voice conversion.

SolutionB1 · Wrong key, wrong alignment: random bits

With the wrong key, the detector correlates against unrelated noise patterns and gets 18 of 32 bits right, chance level, the same as unmarked audio: without the key, the mark is invisible. Cutting 0.5 seconds shifts every segment boundary, so each bit's pattern no longer lines up, and only 13 of 32 bits come out right, again chance level. That is why production watermarks embed synchronisation patterns, or mark every short frame independently as AudioSeal does, so that cropping, insertion and time shifts do not break detection.
