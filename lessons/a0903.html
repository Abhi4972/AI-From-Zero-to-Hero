In plain words first
The idea in one line

Dynamic programming needs a model. Monte Carlo needs nothing but episodes. Run the policy, watch the returns, average them. The simplest idea in RL — and the one that unlocks everything downstream.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
Monte Carlo "Random sampling" Estimate expectations by averaging over iid samples from the distribution.
Return $G_t$ "Future reward" Sum of discounted rewards from step $t$ to episode end: $\sum_{k\ge 0} \gamma^k r_{t+k+1}$.
First-visit MC "Count each state once" Only the first visit in an episode contributes to the value estimate.
Every-visit MC "Use all visits" Every visit contributes; slightly biased for finite samples, consistent in the limit, and more sample-efficient.
ε-greedy "Exploration noise" Pick greedy action with prob $1-\epsilon$; random action with prob $\epsilon$.
Importance sampling "Correcting for sampling from the wrong distribution" Reweight returns by $\pi(a \mid s)/\mu(a \mid s)$ products to estimate $V^\pi$ from $\mu$ data.
On-policy "Learn from my own data" Target policy = behavior policy. Vanilla MC, PPO, SARSA.
Off-policy "Learn from someone else's data" Target policy ≠ behavior policy. Importance-sampled MC, Q-learning, DQN.

The problem

In plain words first

Dynamic programming needs the rule book of the world. Usually you do not have it: you can only try things and see what happens. Monte Carlo methods learn from complete experiences. Play an episode to the end, add up the rewards that followed each state, and average those totals over many episodes; the average converges to the state's true value. To improve behaviour, do the same for each action and lean toward the actions whose average is best, while still trying others now and then, because averages are only known for what you have tried. This lesson runs the lesson's Monte Carlo evaluation and control on the grid world (tabular averages that take a second), shows how badly variance behaves when you learn about one policy from another's experience, and evaluates two blackjack strategies in Gymnasium's blackjack environment by simply playing 100,000 hands each. No neural network is trained.

Dynamic programming is elegant, but it assumes you can query P(s′∣s,a) for every state and action. Almost nothing in the real world works that way. A robot cannot analytically compute the distribution over camera pixels after a joint torque. A pricing algorithm cannot integrate over every possible customer reaction. An LLM cannot enumerate all possible continuations after a token.

You need a method that only needs the ability to sample from the environment. Run the policy. Get a trajectory s_0, a_0, r_1, s_1, a_1, r_2, …, s_T. Use it to estimate values. That is Monte Carlo.

The shift from DP to MC is philosophically important: we move from known model + exact backup to sampled rollouts + averaged return. The variance jumps, but the applicability explodes. Every RL algorithm after this lesson — TD, Q-learning, REINFORCE, PPO, GRPO — is a Monte Carlo estimator at heart, sometimes with bootstrapping layered on top.

The idea

The core idea, in one line: Vπ(s)=Eπ​[Gt​∣st​=s]≈(1/N)∑i​G(i)(s) where G(i)(s) are observed returns following visits to s under policy π.

First-visit vs every-visit MC. Given an episode that visits state s multiple times, first-visit MC only counts the return from the first visit; every-visit MC counts all visits. First-visit MC is unbiased. Every-visit MC can be slightly biased after a finite number of episodes, because it averages a random number of returns per episode and that number can correlate with the returns, but both converge to Vπ(s) as the visit count goes to infinity. First-visit is simpler to analyze (iid samples). Every-visit uses more data per episode and typically converges faster in practice.

Incremental mean. Instead of storing all returns, update the running average:

Vn​(s)=Vn−1​(s)+(1/n)[Gn​−Vn−1​(s)]

Reorganize: Vnew​=Vold​+α⋅(target−Vold​) with α=1/n. Swap 1/n for a constant step-size α∈(0,1) and you get a non-stationary MC estimator that tracks changes in π. That move is the entire jump from MC to TD to every modern RL algorithm.

Exploration is now a problem. DP touched every state by enumeration. MC only sees states the policy visits. If π is deterministic, whole regions of the state space never get sampled, and their value estimates stay at zero forever. Three fixes, in historical order:

  1. Exploring starts. Start each episode from a random (s, a) pair. Guarantees coverage; unrealistic in practice (you cannot "reset" a robot into an arbitrary state).
  2. ε-greedy. Act greedy w.r.t. current Q, but with probability ϵ pick a random action. All state-action pairs get sampled asymptotically.
  3. Off-policy MC. Collect data under a behavior policy μ, learn about target policy π via importance sampling. High variance, but it's the bridge to replay-buffer methods like DQN.

Monte Carlo Control. Evaluate → improve → evaluate, just like policy iteration, but evaluation is sampling-based:

  1. Run π, get an episode.
  2. Update Q(s,a) from observed returns.
  3. Make π ε-greedy w.r.t. Q.
  4. Repeat.

Converges to Q∗ and π∗ with probability 1 under mild conditions (every pair visited infinitely often, α satisfies Robbins-Monro).

The same idea as a formula

Monte Carlo estimate. From N observed returns after visiting s: V^(s)=N1​∑i=1N​G(i)(s). It is unbiased (first-visit) and its standard error is σG​/N​, so halving the error costs four times the episodes.

Incremental form. Vn​=Vn−1​+n1​(Gn​−Vn−1​); with a constant step α it becomes an exponential moving average that tracks a changing policy.

Returns, backwards. Gt​=rt+1​+γGt+1​, GT​=0: one pass from the end of the episode.

Off-policy evaluation. To evaluate target π from episodes of behaviour μ, weight each return by the probability ratio of the whole trajectory:

ρ=t=0∏T−1​μ(at​∣st​)π(at​∣st​)​,V^OIS​=N1​i∑​ρi​Gi​,V^WIS​=∑i​ρi​∑i​ρi​Gi​​

Ordinary importance sampling is unbiased but its variance grows exponentially with the episode length; weighted importance sampling is biased for small N but far more stable.

MC control replaces the sweep of policy iteration with sampling: estimate Q(s,a) from returns, act ε-greedily with respect to it, repeat. With ε>0 every action keeps a probability of at least ε/∣A∣.

Worked example
Monte Carlo by hand

Returns from one episode. Rewards −1,−1,−1 then the episode ends; γ=0.9:

  • G2​=−1, G1​=−1+0.9×(−1)=−1.9, G0​=−1+0.9×(−1.9)=−2.71.

Averaging. Three episodes give returns from the start of −2.71, −4.10 and −3.44: V^(start)=−3.42. A fourth return of −2.0 updates it to −3.42+41​(−2.0+3.42)=−3.06.

An importance weight. Target policy picks "down" with 0.45, behaviour with 0.25: one step contributes 0.45/0.25=1.8; a step the target rarely takes (0.05/0.25=0.2). Over 60 steps the product ranges from about 10−42 to 1015: a few episodes carry almost all the weight.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "Monte Carlo needs a model of the environment."
    ✓ It needs only the ability to play episodes to the end; nothing about transition probabilities.
  • ✗ "More episodes always fix the estimate quickly."
    ✓ The error falls only as 1/N​, and long, noisy episodes make each return very variable.
  • ✗ "You can evaluate any policy from any other policy's data."
    ✓ Only where the behaviour policy takes the target's actions, and the importance weights can make the estimate useless in practice (the run after Step 5).

Step 1 · rollout → list of (s, a, r)

def rollout(env, policy, max_steps=200):
    trajectory = []
    s = env.reset()
    for _ in range(max_steps):
        a = policy(s)
        s_next, r, done = env.step(s, a)
        trajectory.append((s, a, r))
        s = s_next
        if done:
            break
    return trajectory

No model, only env.reset() and env.step(s, a). Same interface as a gym environment but stripped down.

Step 2 · compute returns (reverse sweep)

def returns_from(trajectory, gamma):
    returns = []
    G = 0.0
    for _, _, r in reversed(trajectory):
        G = r + gamma * G
        returns.append(G)
    return list(reversed(returns))

One pass, O(T). The backward recurrence Gt​=rt+1​+γGt+1​ avoids re-summing.

Step 3 · first-visit MC evaluation

def mc_policy_evaluation(env, policy, episodes, gamma=0.99):
    V = defaultdict(float)
    counts = defaultdict(int)
    for _ in range(episodes):
        trajectory = rollout(env, policy)
        returns = returns_from(trajectory, gamma)
        seen = set()
        for t, ((s, _, _), G) in enumerate(zip(trajectory, returns)):
            if s in seen:
                continue
            seen.add(s)
            counts[s] += 1
            V[s] += (G - V[s]) / counts[s]
    return V

Three lines do the work: mark state as seen on first visit, increment count, update running mean.

Step 4 · ε-greedy MC control (on-policy)

def mc_control(env, episodes, gamma=0.99, epsilon=0.1):
    Q = defaultdict(lambda: {a: 0.0 for a in ACTIONS})
    counts = defaultdict(lambda: {a: 0 for a in ACTIONS})

    def policy(s):
        if random() < epsilon:
            return choice(ACTIONS)
        return max(Q[s], key=Q[s].get)

    for _ in range(episodes):
        trajectory = rollout(env, policy)
        returns = returns_from(trajectory, gamma)
        seen = set()
        for (s, a, _), G in zip(trajectory, returns):
            if (s, a) in seen:
                continue
            seen.add((s, a))
            counts[s][a] += 1
            Q[s][a] += (G - Q[s][a]) / counts[s][a]
    return Q, policy

Learning about one policy from another

The mostly down-and-right target policy is evaluated three ways, 100 times each: on-policy Monte Carlo, and from uniform-random episodes with ordinary and with weighted importance sampling, against the exact value from a linear solve.

import random, runpy, numpy as np
m = runpy.run_path("main.py")                      # the lesson's grid world, rollout and returns (no learning here)
A = m["ACTIONS"]
pi_probs = {"up": 0.05, "down": 0.45, "left": 0.05, "right": 0.45}      # target policy: mostly down/right
target = lambda s, rng: rng.choices(A, weights=[pi_probs[a] for a in A])[0]
behaviour = m["uniform_policy"]                                           # mu: uniform random, prob 0.25 each

# Exact V^pi(start) with gamma = 1, from the linear system (Lesson 9.1).
S = [s for s in m["states"]() if s != m["TERMINAL"]]; ix = {s: i for i, s in enumerate(S)}
P = np.zeros((len(S), len(S))); r = np.zeros(len(S))
for s in S:
    for a in A:
        s2, rew, _ = m["step"](s, a); r[ix[s]] += pi_probs[a] * rew
        if s2 != m["TERMINAL"]: P[ix[s], ix[s2]] += pi_probs[a]
exact = np.linalg.solve(np.eye(len(S)) - P, r)[0]
print(f"exact V^pi(start) = {exact:.3f}")

def estimates(n, rng):
    on = np.mean([sum(x[2] for x in m["rollout"](target, rng)) for _ in range(n)])       # on-policy Monte Carlo
    G, rho = [], []
    for _ in range(n):                                                                    # off-policy: play mu, reweight
        tr = m["rollout"](behaviour, rng)
        G.append(sum(x[2] for x in tr)); rho.append(np.prod([pi_probs[a] / 0.25 for _, a, _ in tr]))
    G, rho = np.array(G), np.array(rho)
    return on, (rho * G).mean(), (rho * G).sum() / rho.sum(), rho
rng = random.Random(0)
print("episodes | on-policy MC        | ordinary importance sampling | weighted importance sampling   (mean +/- spread over 100 repeats)")
for n in (10, 100, 1000):
    runs = [estimates(n, rng) for _ in range(100)]
    on, ois, wis = (np.array([x[k] for x in runs]) for k in range(3))
    print(f"{n:8d} | {on.mean():7.2f} +/- {on.std():5.2f} | {ois.mean():12.2f} +/- {ois.std():9.2f} | {wis.mean():12.2f} +/- {wis.std():6.2f}")
rho = np.concatenate([x[3] for x in runs])
print(f"importance weights of uniform-policy episodes: median {np.median(rho):.2e}, largest {rho.max():.2e}, "
      f"largest 1% of episodes carry {np.sort(rho)[-len(rho) // 100:].sum() / rho.sum():.0%} of the total weight")

Reading this output

On-policy Monte Carlo behaves as the formula box says: unbiased, with a spread that falls from 1.1 at 10 episodes to 0.12 at 1,000, a factor of about 100​. Ordinary importance sampling is unbiased on average but useless in practice: at 1,000 episodes its spread is still ±3.7, thirty times on-policy's, and at 10 episodes ±12. Weighted importance sampling is much steadier (±0.86 at 1,000) but biased when data are scarce (−15.5 on average at 10 episodes). The last line shows why: uniform-policy episodes are long and rarely look like the target policy, so the median importance weight is 7×10−10 while the largest is over 1,000, and the top 1% of episodes carry 63% of the total weight. Off-policy estimates are only as good as the overlap between the two policies.

Pitfalls

  • Infinite episodes. MC requires episodes to terminate. If your policy can loop forever, cap max_steps and treat the cap as implicit failure. GridWorld with a random policy routinely times out — that is normal, just make sure you count it correctly.
  • Variance. MC uses full returns. On long episodes, variance is huge — one unlucky reward at the end shifts V(s0​) by the same amount. TD methods (Lesson 9.4) cut this by bootstrapping.
  • State coverage. Greedy MC on a fresh Q with ties will only ever try one action. You must explore (ε-greedy, exploring starts, UCB).
  • Non-stationary policies. If π changes (as in MC control), old returns are from a different policy. Constant-α MC handles this; sample-average MC does not.
  • Off-policy importance sampling. The weights π(a∣s)/μ(a∣s) multiply across a trajectory. Variance explodes with horizon. Cap with per-decision weighted IS or switch to TD.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

The 2026 role of Monte Carlo methods:

Use case Why MC
Short-horizon games (blackjack, poker) Episodes terminate naturally; returns are clean.
Offline evaluation of a logged policy Average discounted returns over stored trajectories.
Monte Carlo Tree Search Classic MCTS averages rollout returns at the leaves; AlphaZero replaces the rollouts with a value network.
LLM RL evaluation Compute average reward over sampled completions for a given policy.
Baseline estimation in PPO The advantage target At​=Gt​−V(st​) uses an MC Gt​.
Teaching RL Simplest algorithm that actually works — strip bootstrapping to see the core.

Modern deep-RL algorithms (PPO, SAC) interpolate between pure MC (full returns) and pure TD (one-step bootstrap) via n-step returns or GAE. Both endpoints are instances of the same estimator.

Blackjack in Gymnasium

Blackjack-v1 with Sutton and Barto's rules: two fixed strategies, "stick at 17 or more" and "stick at 20 or more", each played for 100,000 hands, and first-visit Monte Carlo values of the second.

import numpy as np, gymnasium as gym
from collections import defaultdict

# Blackjack-v1 (Sutton & Barto example 5.1): state = (player sum, dealer's showing card, usable ace); reward +1/0/-1.
env = gym.make("Blackjack-v1", sab=True)
def evaluate(stick_at, episodes=100_000, seed=0):
    """First-visit Monte Carlo evaluation of 'stick at stick_at or more, otherwise hit' (no learning)."""
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed); V = defaultdict(float); n = defaultdict(int); total = 0.0
    for ep in range(episodes):
        s, _ = env.reset(seed=int(rng.integers(2**31))); seen = []; done = False
        while not done:
            seen.append(s)
            s, r, term, trunc, _ = env.step(0 if s[0] >= stick_at else 1); done = term or trunc
        total += r
        for st in set(seen):                         # gamma = 1 and reward only at the end: every state's return is r
            n[st] += 1; V[st] += (r - V[st]) / n[st]
    return total / episodes, V, n
for k in (17, 20):
    mean, V, n = evaluate(k)
    print(f"stick at {k}+: average result per hand {mean:+.4f} over 100,000 hands")
mean, V, n = evaluate(20)
print("V(player sum, dealer card, no usable ace) under 'stick at 20+', dealer showing 2 / 6 / 10 / ace:")
for p in (13, 16, 19, 20, 21):
    print(f"  sum {p}: " + "  ".join(f"{V[(p, d, False)]:+.3f} (n={n[(p, d, False)]:>5})" for d in (2, 6, 10, 1)))

Break it on purpose

In mc_control, set epsilon = 0 so the policy is always greedy, with Q initialised to 0 for every action and ties broken by the first action in ACTIONS ("up"). What does the agent do in the first episode, and why can it never discover that "down" and "right" are better? (Solution B1.)

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Off-policy evaluation in industry. Recommendation and ads teams estimate a new policy's value from logged data with importance sampling, weighted and doubly robust estimators before an A/B test; libraries include Open Bandit Pipeline and SCOPE-RL.
  • Monte Carlo returns in LLM RL. GRPO and RLOO estimate the value of a prompt by sampling several completions and averaging their rewards, a Monte Carlo baseline instead of a learned critic (TRL, verl, OpenRLHF).
  • Search. MCTS with learned values and policies (AlphaZero, MuZero, DeepMind's mctx) remains the backbone of game-playing systems.

Sources: Open Bandit Pipeline, SCOPE-RL, TRL GRPO, mctx, Sutton & Barto ch. 5.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Monte Carlo estimates values by averaging complete returns; no model needed.
  • Error falls as 1/N​; long episodes mean noisy returns.
  • Exploration is required: ε-greedy, exploring starts or off-policy data.
  • Off-policy evaluation reweights by ∏π/μ; ordinary IS is unbiased but wild, weighted IS biased but stable.
  • Constant-step updates turn Monte Carlo into the template for TD and everything after it.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Much worse

Much worse: about ±3.7 across repeats, thirty times the on-policy error, and ±10 with 100 episodes. The uniform policy's episodes are long (about 59 steps) and full of "up" and "left" moves that the target policy rarely makes, so most importance weights are vanishingly small and a handful are enormous; the estimate is decided by whichever rare episodes happen to resemble the target policy. Weighted importance sampling tames the variance at the price of bias. The practical lesson: off-policy evaluation needs behaviour and target policies that overlap well, or shorter horizons, or per-decision and doubly robust estimators.

SolutionB1 · Stuck on "up"

All actions start at Q=0, so the greedy choice is the first, "up". From the top-left corner, "up" bumps into the wall, the agent stays put, and the episode runs to the 200-step cap with return about −87 (with γ=0.99). Only the pair (start, up) is ever updated, so Q(start,up) becomes very negative and the next episode switches to "down" or "left", which get the same treatment; the agent does eventually move, but every state is explored only by exhausting bad actions one at a time, and actions that look poor after one unlucky episode are never retried. Without ε>0 (or optimistic initial values, or exploring starts) Monte Carlo control has no guarantee of finding the optimum.
