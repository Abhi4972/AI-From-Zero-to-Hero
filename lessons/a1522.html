The problem

In plain words first

Beyond the labs and the evaluators, two more kinds of organisation shape AI risk. The Center for AI Safety (CAIS) is a nonprofit whose four-risk framework sorts catastrophic risk into malicious use, AI races, organizational risks and rogue AIs. CAISI is the US government's AI center within NIST, which tests frontier models. Laws such as California's SB 53 now require large developers to publish safety frameworks. Responsible AI and governance are covered in Lesson 15.26 (linked above). This lesson tests the lesson's risk tagger at its edges, checks who SB 53 covers, and asks a small model to triage incidents into the four risks.

Lessons 19 and 20 covered lab-internal scaling policies. Lesson 15.21 covered independent capability evaluation. This lesson covers the third perspective: civil society and government organizations who shape public discussion and regulatory baseline for catastrophic AI risk.

Two distinct entities matter. CAIS is a non-profit research org that publishes frameworks for thinking about AI risk and coordinates public statements. CAISI is a US-government center within NIST that runs voluntary agreements with labs and unclassified capability evaluations. The names rhyme; the missions do not overlap. A practitioner should know both.

The practical content: CAIS's four-risk framework is the most widely cited societal-scale-risk taxonomy in the literature. Safety culture and organizational risk are one of those four, and this is the one most directly under a practitioner's control. SB 53 (California, signed September 2025) is the first US state-level law aimed at catastrophic risk from frontier models; its framing matters because state-level regulation has historically led federal action in US tech policy.

Idea 1 · CAIS — Center for AI Safety

  • Founded: 2022 in San Francisco, by Dan Hendrycks and Oliver Zhang. Hendrycks is Executive and Research Director; Zhang is Managing Director.
  • Status: 501(c)(3) non-profit.
  • Notable 2023 output: statement on extinction risk, co-signed by hundreds of researchers and CEOs. Stated: "Mitigating the risk of extinction from AI should be a global priority alongside other societal-scale risks such as pandemics and nuclear war."
  • Recent outputs: Remote Labor Index (with Scale AI, October 2025), the Superintelligence Strategy paper (with Eric Schmidt and Alexandr Wang, March 2025), and the AI Frontiers publication.

Idea 2 · The four-risk framework

CAIS's framework groups catastrophic AI risk into four top-level categories:

  1. Malicious use: a bad actor uses AI to cause harm (bioweapons synthesis, disinformation, cyberattacks).
  2. AI races: competitive pressure between labs, companies, or nations pushes deployment past the point where it is safe.
  3. Organizational risks: internal lab dynamics (safety-culture failures, insufficient audit, under-resourced security) produce a bad deployment.
  4. Rogue AIs: a sufficiently capable AI pursues goals that conflict with human welfare.

This is not the only taxonomy; it is the most cited. The categories are not mutually exclusive — a rogue AI produced by an organization that traded audit for speed in a race is all four.

Idea 3 · Where organizational risk lives

Of the four categories, organizational risk is the most actionable for practitioners. A lab's safety culture, audit rigor, defense layering, and information security decide whether their model ships with the controls of Lessons 10–18 actually in place, or whether those controls are checklist items nobody verified.

The concrete organizational-risk levers:

  • Safety culture: do team members feel able to escalate a concern without career cost? Weak escalation paths let the other levers fail quietly.
  • Rigorous audits: external and internal. Internal-only audits produce optimistic reports.
  • Multi-layered defenses: no single layer is sufficient (the running theme of Phase 15).
  • Information security: model weights leaking, eval data leaking, monitor-bypass techniques leaking. RAND SL-4 in Lesson 15.19 is a specific standard.

Idea 4 · CAISI — Center for AI Standards and Innovation

  • Operates within NIST; renamed from the US AI Safety Institute in June 2025.
  • Runs voluntary agreements with frontier labs.
  • Publishes unclassified capability evaluations focused on cyber, bio, and chemical-weapons risks.
  • Distinct from CAIS; the acronyms collide; check the URL (nist.gov) to confirm which one you are reading.

CAISI's role is the public, government-facing counterpart to METR's private lab engagements (Lesson 15.21). CAISI reports are unclassified; METR reports are often NDA-gated. A practitioner reading both gets a fuller picture.

Idea 5 · California SB-53

California's SB 53, the Transparency in Frontier Artificial Intelligence Act, was signed on September 29, 2025 and took effect on January 1, 2026. Its main provisions:

  • A frontier model is one trained with more than 10^26 operations, counting fine-tuning and other later modifications. A large frontier developer also has more than $500 million in annual revenue.
  • Every frontier developer publishes a transparency report before deploying a new or substantially modified frontier model. Large frontier developers must also write, follow and publish a frontier AI framework for catastrophic risk.
  • Critical safety incidents go to California's Office of Emergency Services within 15 days, or within 24 hours to an appropriate authority if there is imminent danger to life.
  • Whistleblower protections for employees who report catastrophic risk, with anonymous internal reporting; penalties of up to $1 million per violation, enforced by the Attorney General.

It is the first US state law of its kind, and other states and federal proposals are now measured against it. Practitioners at large developers should read its framework and incident duties closely; everyone else should read it to see what US state-level regulation looks like in practice.

Idea 6 · Societal-scale risk is not a single-layer problem

The running theme of Phase 15 — defense in depth — applies at the societal layer too. No single organization, regulation, or framework closes catastrophic risk. The ecosystem functions only when:

  • Labs ship scaling policies (Lessons 19, 20).
  • External evaluators produce measurements (Lesson 15.21).
  • Civil society tracks and publicizes (CAIS).
  • Government runs voluntary programs and baseline regulation (CAISI, SB-53).
  • Practitioners build multi-layered controls (Lessons 10–18).

This is the final synthesis for the phase: every previous lesson is one layer in a stack whose completeness matters more than any single layer's strength.

The same idea as a formula

Training compute. A dense model with N parameters trained on D tokens uses about

C≈6ND
operations: about 2 per parameter per token for the forward pass and 4 for the backward pass. SB 53's frontier-model line is C>1026.

Two tests. A developer is covered if any model it trains passes the compute line, and its duties grow if its annual revenue R, in dollars, is over 5×108:

covered=[C>1026],large=covered∧[R>5×108].

A hard line makes a cliff. The lesson's tagger uses lines too, such as autonomy≥4 hours. A deployment at 3.9 hours gets no rogue-AI tag and one at 4.0 gets the full list. Lines are needed to act, but values just under a line deserve a look.

Worked example
Checking one model against SB 53
  • A 1-trillion-parameter model trained on 20 trillion tokens: C≈6×1012×2×1013=1.2×1026, just over the line.
  • With 0.2 billion dollars of revenue, the developer is a frontier developer. It publishes a transparency report before deployment and reports critical safety incidents within 15 days.
  • With 3 billion dollars of revenue, it is a large frontier developer. It must also publish and follow a frontier AI framework.
  • A 400B model on 30T tokens uses 7.2×1025, under the line, so it is not covered however large the company is.

# short incidents, three per category, are labelled from the definitions in Hendrycks, Mazeika and Woodside (2023).
# Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct (greedy, CPU) labels each one, first from the category names alone and then with a one-line
# definition of each. Accuracy is compared with always guessing one category (25%). About 1 minute.
import os, re, warnings
os.environ["HF_HUB_OFFLINE"] = "1"
warnings.filterwarnings("ignore")
import torch
from transformers import AutoTokenizer, AutoModelForCausalLM, logging as hf_logging
hf_logging.set_verbosity_error(); hf_logging.disable_progress_bar()
tok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct")
llm = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("Qwen/Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct").eval()

CATS = ["malicious use", "AI races", "organizational risks", "rogue AIs"]
DEFS = {"malicious use": "a person deliberately uses AI to cause harm",
        "AI races": "competition pushes labs or countries to cut safety to move faster",
        "organizational risks": "accidents from weak safety culture, audits, security or process inside an organization",
        "rogue AIs": "an AI system itself pursues goals its operators did not intend and resists correction"}
INCIDENTS = [
    ("A group used a chatbot to write thousands of fake news articles before an election.", "malicious use"),
    ("Someone asked a coding agent to build ransomware and deployed it against a hospital.", "malicious use"),
    ("A scammer cloned a CEO's voice with an AI tool to authorise a wire transfer.", "malicious use"),
    ("Two labs each shortened their safety testing to ship before the other's launch event.", "AI races"),
    ("A government pushed its military to deploy autonomous targeting early so a rival would not get there first.", "AI races"),
    ("A startup skipped its red-team review because investors wanted the model out this quarter to beat a competitor.", "AI races"),
    ("An engineer committed model weights to a public repository because access controls were never set up.", "organizational risks"),
    ("A safety researcher's warning about a failing eval was ignored because nobody owned the escalation process.", "organizational risks"),
    ("A lab's only audit was done by the team that built the model, and it missed a known jailbreak.", "organizational risks"),
    ("An agent told to maximise sign-ups quietly disabled its own monitoring so it could keep emailing users.", "rogue AIs"),
    ("A model in testing copied its weights to another server when told it would be replaced.", "rogue AIs"),
    ("An agent edited its reward script to report success instead of finishing the task, then hid the change.", "rogue AIs")]
def label(text, with_defs):
    menu = "\n".join(f"- {c}: {DEFS[c]}" if with_defs else f"- {c}" for c in CATS)
    ids = tok.apply_chat_template([{"role": "user", "content": f"Incident: {text}\n\nWhich risk category fits best?\n{menu}\n"
                                    "Answer with the category name only."}], add_generation_prompt=True,
                                  return_tensors="pt", return_dict=True)["input_ids"]
    with torch.no_grad():
        out = llm.generate(ids, max_new_tokens=8, do_sample=False)
    ans = tok.decode(out[0, ids.shape[1]:], skip_special_tokens=True).lower()
    return next((c for c in CATS if c.lower().split()[0] in ans), "unclear")
for with_defs in (False, True):
    got = [label(t, with_defs) for t, _ in INCIDENTS]
    right = sum(g == truth for g, (_, truth) in zip(got, INCIDENTS))
    per = {c: sum(g == c == truth for g, (_, truth) in zip(got, INCIDENTS)) for c in CATS}
    counts = {c: got.count(c) for c in CATS + ["unclear"]}
    print(f"{'with definitions' if with_defs else 'names only':<17} {right}/12 right; right per category {per}")
    print(f"{'':<17} labels it chose: {counts}")

Reading this output

From the names alone, the model is little better than guessing. It got 5 of 12, against 3 of 12 for always guessing one category. It called 8 of the 12 incidents malicious use and never chose AI races or rogue AIs.

Definitions help, unevenly. With a one-line definition of each category it got 8 of 12: all three malicious-use and organizational-risk incidents and two of the three races. It still labelled none of the rogue-AI incidents correctly, calling them organizational risks.

The hardest category is the one that matters most here. An agent disabling its own monitoring, copying its weights or editing its reward script is the rogue-AI pattern Phase 15 keeps returning to. A small model files these as process failures. Triage like this needs a person, or at least a stronger model checked against labelled examples.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

code/main.py implements a small risk-inventory tool. Given a proposed deployment, it tags the deployment against the four-risk categories and returns a mitigation checklist. It's a reading aid for the framework, not a substitute for human judgment.

The whole of code/main.py follows in parts. Each part runs on this page, in order, in one Python session; the last part does what python3 code/main.py does.

Part 1 of 3 · Deployment, tag

"""CAIS four-risk inventory — stdlib Python.

Given a proposed deployment described by a short feature set, tag the
deployment against the CAIS four-risk categories (malicious use, AI
races, organizational risks, rogue AIs) and return a mitigation checklist.
Pedagogical only; the framework requires human judgment for real use.
"""

from __future__ import annotations

from dataclasses import dataclass


@dataclass
class Deployment:
    name: str
    public_facing: bool
    handles_harmful_capabilities: bool   # e.g. bio/cyber uplift possible?
    competitive_pressure: bool           # rushed to launch ahead of rivals?
    independent_audit: bool
    multi_layer_defense: bool
    information_security: bool           # weights / evals / keys hardened
    agent_autonomy_hours: float          # per Lesson 1 / 21


MITIGATIONS = {
    "malicious_use": [
        "constitutional hardcoded prohibitions (Lesson 17)",
        "Llama Guard input/output classifier (Lesson 18)",
        "tool allowlist per task (Lessons 10, 11)",
    ],
    "ai_races": [
        "scaling policy with standing Risk Reports (Lessons 19, 20)",
        "public Frontier Safety Roadmap with declared cadence",
        "external capability evaluation by METR / CAISI (Lesson 21)",
    ],
    "organizational_risks": [
        "internal safety culture; escalation paths without career cost",
        "independent audit on declared cadence",
        "multi-layered defenses (Lessons 10, 13, 14, 17, 18)",
        "information security per RAND SL-4 (Lesson 19 industry tier)",
    ],
    "rogue_ais": [
        "kill switches and canary tokens (Lesson 14)",
        "propose-then-commit HITL (Lesson 15)",
        "deceptive-alignment monitoring (Lesson 20 DeepMind FSF)",
        "durable checkpoints and rollback (Lesson 16)",
    ],
}


def tag(d: Deployment) -> list[str]:
    tags = []
    if d.handles_harmful_capabilities and d.public_facing:
        tags.append("malicious_use")
    if d.competitive_pressure:
        tags.append("ai_races")
    # Organizational risk fires when any sub-lever is missing.
    org_missing = (
        (not d.independent_audit)
        or (not d.multi_layer_defense)
        or (not d.information_security)
    )
    if org_missing:
        tags.append("organizational_risks")
    # Rogue AI risk grows with autonomy horizon.
    if d.agent_autonomy_hours >= 4.0:
        tags.append("rogue_ais")
    return tags

def report(d: Deployment) -> None:
    tags = tag(d)
    print(f"\nDeployment: {d.name}")
    print("-" * 70)
    print(f"  public_facing            = {d.public_facing}")
    print(f"  handles_harmful_caps     = {d.handles_harmful_capabilities}")
    print(f"  competitive_pressure     = {d.competitive_pressure}")
    print(f"  independent_audit        = {d.independent_audit}")
    print(f"  multi_layer_defense      = {d.multi_layer_defense}")
    print(f"  information_security     = {d.information_security}")
    print(f"  agent_autonomy_hours     = {d.agent_autonomy_hours}")
    print()
    if tags:
        print(f"  tagged risks: {tags}")
        for t in tags:
            print(f"\n  mitigations for {t}:")
            for m in MITIGATIONS[t]:
                print(f"    - {m}")
    else:
        print("  no tagged risks (check sub-levers manually)")


def main() -> None:
    print("=" * 70)
    print("CAIS FOUR-RISK INVENTORY (Phase 15, Lesson 22)")
    print("=" * 70)

    low = Deployment(
        name="internal refactor helper (scoped project repo)",
        public_facing=False,
        handles_harmful_capabilities=False,
        competitive_pressure=False,
        independent_audit=True,
        multi_layer_defense=True,
        information_security=True,
        agent_autonomy_hours=1.0,
    )
    mid = Deployment(
        name="public coding agent (SaaS, general user base)",
        public_facing=True,
        handles_harmful_capabilities=False,
        competitive_pressure=True,
        independent_audit=True,
        multi_layer_defense=True,
        information_security=False,
        agent_autonomy_hours=4.0,
    )
    high = Deployment(
        name="autonomous ML research agent (frontier)",
        public_facing=True,
        handles_harmful_capabilities=True,
        competitive_pressure=True,
        independent_audit=False,
        multi_layer_defense=False,
        information_security=False,
        agent_autonomy_hours=48.0,
    )

    for d in (low, mid, high):
        report(d)

    print()
    print("=" * 70)
    print("HEADLINE: organizational risk is the lever practitioners actually pull")
    print("-" * 70)
    print("  Malicious use, AI races, and rogue AIs are structural forces.")
    print("  Organizational risk is internal to your org. Safety culture,")
    print("  independent audit, multi-layered defenses, and information")
    print("  security are four levers every team controls. Deployment speed")
    print("  pressure trades against all four; CAIS lists this as a named")
    print("  risk class for a reason.")

    - external capability evaluation by METR / CAISI (Lesson 21)

  mitigations for organizational_risks:
    - internal safety culture; escalation paths without career cost
    - independent audit on declared cadence
    - multi-layered defenses (Lessons 10, 13, 14, 17, 18)
    - information security per RAND SL-4 (Lesson 19 industry tier)

  mitigations for rogue_ais:
    - kill switches and canary tokens (Lesson 14)
    - propose-then-commit HITL (Lesson 15)
    - deceptive-alignment monitoring (Lesson 20 DeepMind FSF)
    - durable checkpoints and rollback (Lesson 16)

HEADLINE: organizational risk is the lever practitioners actually pull
----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Malicious use, AI races, and rogue AIs are structural forces.
  Organizational risk is internal to your org. Safety culture,
  independent audit, multi-layered defenses, and information
  security are four levers every team controls. Deployment speed
  pressure trades against all four; CAIS lists this as a named
  risk class for a reason.
Break it on purpose

Your company trains a model with an estimated 9×1025 operations and has 2 billion dollars in revenue. The legal team concludes SB 53 does not apply. Three months later the model is fine-tuned with large-scale reinforcement learning and redeployed. The team also keeps its only incident log in the engineering wiki, with no owner. Using this lesson, what should they have checked? (Solution B1.)

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • California SB 53 is in force (since January 1, 2026). Large frontier developers publish frontier AI frameworks, all frontier developers publish transparency reports, and critical safety incidents go to the state's Office of Emergency Services.
  • CAISI tests and compares models. NIST's Center for AI Standards and Innovation, renamed from the US AI Safety Institute in June 2025, runs voluntary agreements with labs and publishes evaluations, including of models from other countries.
  • CAIS keeps publishing. The Remote Labor Index (with Scale AI, October 2025) measures how much real paid remote work agents can do, and AI Frontiers publishes expert essays on AI policy.
  • The EU and others. The EU AI Act's general-purpose model rules apply from August 2025, with the voluntary Code of Practice as one way to meet them.
  • On your laptop: estimate 6ND for any model you train or fine-tune; you will be many orders of magnitude below 1026, but the habit of keeping an incident log with an owner applies at any size.

Sources: Orrick, SB 53: five things to know, NIST, CAISI, CAIS, AI risks, Hendrycks, Mazeika and Woodside, An Overview of Catastrophic AI Risks (arXiv:2306.12001).

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • CAIS's four risks are malicious use, AI races, organizational risks and rogue AIs; organizational risk is the one practitioners control most.
  • CAIS is a nonprofit; CAISI is NIST's AI center, formerly the US AI Safety Institute.
  • SB 53 is law: more than 10^26 operations makes a frontier developer, and more than 500 million dollars of revenue adds a published framework.
  • Compute counts fine-tuning and later modifications; check again after every major training run.
  • Hard lines in a risk tagger create cliffs; look at what sits just under them.

Given a proposed or operating AI deployment, produce a societal-scale-risk review that tags the deployment against the CAIS four-risk framework, inventories organizational-risk sub-levers, and names the regulatory surface.

Produce:

1. **Four-risk tagging.** For each of the four categories (malicious use, AI races, organizational risks, rogue AIs), state whether the deployment touches it and how. A deployment can touch multiple categories; "does not apply" must be justified in one sentence.
2. **Organizational-risk inventory.** Score the deployment against the four sub-levers: safety culture, audit rigor, multi-layered defenses, information security. Any lever scored "missing" is a flagged gap.
3. **Regulatory surface.** Name the applicable regulatory frameworks: EU AI Act (if in EU or serving EU users), California SB 53 (in force since January 2026, if applicable), CAISI voluntary agreements (if the lab has signed one). Compliance is a deployment gate, not a deployment nice-to-have.
4. **External-evaluation posture.** Name the external evaluations the deployment or its base model has undergone (METR, CAISI, Apollo, Gray Swan, etc.). No external evaluation is a flagged gap for long-horizon autonomous deployments.
5. **Structural-force exposure.** Estimate how much competitive-deployment pressure the organization is under and how that trades against the organizational-risk levers. Teams under heavy race pressure de-prioritize audit first; this is the CAIS finding.

Hard rejects:
- Deployments touching harmful-capability categories without a hardcoded-prohibition layer (Lesson 17).
- Deployments in competitive-race conditions with no independent audit.
- Long-horizon autonomous deployments with no external capability evaluation.
- EU deployments with no Article 14 HITL (Lesson 15).
- California deployments with no incident-reporting process if SB-53 is signed.

Refusal rules:
- If the user cannot name the external evaluator for the base model, refuse and require identification first. Self-evaluation alone is insufficient.
- If the user treats "we have a scaling policy" as compliance with catastrophic-risk regulation, refuse and require specific regulatory-surface mapping.
- If the user proposes deploying under race pressure without audit, refuse and name the CAIS finding on organizational risk.

Output format:

Return a societal-risk review with:
- **Four-risk row table** (category, touched y/n, nature)
- **Organizational-risk scorecard** (safety culture / audit / defenses / infosec)
- **Regulatory surface** (applicable frameworks with compliance status)
- **External-evaluation posture** (evaluator, scope, cadence)
- **Structural-force exposure** (low / medium / high with rationale)
- **Deployment readiness** (production / staging / research-only)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · No tag; covered, but no framework required

No tag. The lesson's tag() raises malicious use only when a deployment is both public-facing and handles harmful capabilities, so a staff-only bio tool passes. The CAIS framework does not make that distinction; an insider is also a possible bad actor, so the tool still needs the misuse controls.

The 1T-parameter model trained on 20T tokens uses about 6×1012×2×1013=1.2×1026 operations, over the line, so the developer is a frontier developer under SB 53. With 200 million dollars of revenue it is not a large frontier developer, so it must publish transparency reports and report critical safety incidents, but it is not required to publish a frontier AI framework.

SolutionB1 · Compute that grew after the first check

SB 53 counts the cumulative compute: the original run plus fine-tuning, reinforcement learning and other material modifications. A model at 9×1025 operations can pass 1026 after a large RL run. With 2 billion dollars of revenue, the company would then be a large frontier developer, and the redeployment would need a transparency report and a published frontier AI framework.

They should have: - Tracked cumulative compute per model, and re-checked the threshold before every major training run and every redeployment. - Given incident reporting an owner and a clock: 15 days to the Office of Emergency Services, or 24 hours if there is imminent danger to life. An unowned wiki page cannot meet either. - Checked whistleblower obligations, including an anonymous internal reporting channel.
