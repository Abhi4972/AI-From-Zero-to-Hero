The problem

In plain words first

A language model assigns probabilities to what comes next. The oldest working kind, the n-gram model, just counts: how often did "sat" follow "the cat" in a big pile of text? Counting is easy. The hard part is everything the pile never contained. Most long phrases you will ever see are new, and a pure counter gives them probability zero. Smoothing methods move a little probability from seen events to unseen ones. The best classic method, Kneser-Ney, also asks in how many different contexts a word has appeared, not just how often. This lesson measures how often new phrases really occur, derives Kneser-Ney's formula and works an example by hand, scores the models with perplexity (how many choices the model is effectively torn between at each word), generates text from them, and compares them with a small transformer on exactly the same sentences.

Before transformers, before RNNs, before word embeddings, a language model predicted the next word by counting how often it followed the previous n-1 words. Count "the cat" → "sat" 47 times, "the cat" → "jumped" 12 times, "the cat" → "refrigerator" 0 times. Normalize to get a probability distribution.

That is an n-gram language model. It ran every speech recognizer, every spell checker, and every phrase-based machine translation system from 1980 through 2015. It still runs when you need cheap on-device language modeling.

The interesting problem is what to do about unseen n-grams. A raw count-based model assigns zero probability to anything it has not seen, which is catastrophic because sentences are long and almost every long sentence contains at least one unseen sequence. Fifty years of smoothing research fixed that. Kneser-Ney smoothing is the result, and modern deep learning inherited its empirical tradition.

The idea

Idea 1 · The prediction game

Before any of this machinery existed, one experiment defined what a language model is. Cover the next letter of an English sentence. Ask someone to guess it, one guess at a time, until they get it right. Write down the guess count. Repeat for a few hundred letters.

The guess counts are not trivia. They are a lossless re-encoding of the text: hand the count sequence to a second, identical guesser and they can reconstruct every letter, because at each position they know exactly which guesses come first. A message you can re-encode in fewer symbols carries less information per symbol, so the guess-count statistics put a ceiling on the entropy of English.

Shannon ran this in 1951 and got a number that still governs the field. A 27-symbol alphabet (26 letters plus space) could carry log2(27) ≈ 4.75 bits per letter. Human guessers with 100 letters of context landed between 0.6 and 1.3 bits per letter. English is roughly three-quarters forced moves. The structure a model must learn was measured before any model could learn it.

Every language model since is a mechanical player of this game, and every evaluation number in this lesson is the game scored:

  • Cross-entropy loss is the average number of bits the model needs per symbol. Training an LM is literally minimizing its score at the guessing game.
  • Perplexity is 2^bits (or e^nats): the branching factor still facing the model after its guesswork. Uniform guessing over 27 symbols is perplexity 27; a 1-bit-per-letter player has perplexity 2.
  • Context length is the player's memory. A trigram model plays with two tokens of memory. A transformer plays the same game with 100K tokens. The rules never changed; the player got better.

One unit switch to track: the game scores per letter in bits (log2), while the n-gram formulas below score per word token in nats (natural log) — and since perplexity e^H in nats equals 2^H in bits, the two views are the same measurement in different units.

N-gram probability: P(w_i | w_{i-n+1}, ..., w_{i-1}). Fix n (typically 3 for trigrams, 4 for 4-grams). Compute from counts:

P(w∣context)=count(context)count(context,w)​

The zero-count problem. Any n-gram not seen in training gets probability zero. How common are unseen n-grams? Measured under "Use it" on the Brown corpus with 2,000 sentences held out: a small share of held-out words never appeared in training, but most held-out trigrams and the large majority of held-out 4-grams were never seen. You cannot evaluate on any real text without smoothing.

Smoothing approaches, in order of sophistication:

  1. Laplace (add-one). Add 1 to every count. Simple, terrible on rare events.
  2. Good-Turing. Reallocate probability mass from higher-frequency events to unseen ones based on frequency-of-frequencies.
  3. Interpolation. Combine n-gram, (n-1)-gram, etc., estimates with tunable weights.
  4. Backoff. If n-gram has count zero, fall back to (n-1)-gram. Katz backoff normalizes this.
  5. Absolute discounting. Subtract a fixed discount D from all counts, redistribute to unseen.
  6. Kneser-Ney. Absolute discounting plus a clever choice for the lower-order model: use continuation probability (how many contexts a word appears in) instead of raw frequency.

The Kneser-Ney insight is deep. "San Francisco" is a common bigram. Unigram "Francisco" appears mostly after "San." Naive absolute discounting gives "Francisco" high unigram probability (because the count is high). Kneser-Ney notices that "Francisco" appears in only one context and lowers its continuation probability accordingly. Result: a novel bigram ending in "Francisco" gets the appropriate low probability.

Evaluation: perplexity. The exponent of the average negative log-likelihood per word on a held-out test set. Lower is better. A perplexity of 100 means the model is as confused as it would be choosing uniformly among 100 words.

perplexity=exp(−N1​i∑​logP(wi​∣contexti​))

Step 1 · trigram counts

from collections import Counter, defaultdict


def train_ngram(corpus_tokens, n=3):
    ngrams = Counter()
    contexts = Counter()
    for sentence in corpus_tokens:
        padded = ["<s>"] * (n - 1) + sentence + ["</s>"]
        for i in range(len(padded) - n + 1):
            ctx = tuple(padded[i:i + n - 1])
            word = padded[i + n - 1]
            ngrams[ctx + (word,)] += 1
            contexts[ctx] += 1
    return ngrams, contexts


def raw_probability(ngrams, contexts, context, word):
    ctx = tuple(context)
    if contexts.get(ctx, 0) == 0:
        return 0.0
    return ngrams.get(ctx + (word,), 0) / contexts[ctx]

Input is a list of tokenized sentences. Output is n-gram counts and context counts. <s> and </s> are sentence boundaries.

Step 2 · Laplace smoothing

def laplace_probability(ngrams, contexts, vocab_size, context, word):
    ctx = tuple(context)
    numerator = ngrams.get(ctx + (word,), 0) + 1
    denominator = contexts.get(ctx, 0) + vocab_size
    return numerator / denominator

Add 1 to every count. Smooths but over-allocates mass to unseen events, hurting rare-known events too.

Step 3 · Kneser-Ney (bigram, interpolated)

def kneser_ney_bigram_model(corpus_tokens, discount=0.75):
    unigrams = Counter()
    bigrams = Counter()
    unigram_contexts = defaultdict(set)

    for sentence in corpus_tokens:
        padded = ["<s>"] + sentence + ["</s>"]
        for i, w in enumerate(padded):
            unigrams[w] += 1
            if i > 0:
                prev = padded[i - 1]
                bigrams[(prev, w)] += 1
                unigram_contexts[w].add(prev)

    total_unique_bigrams = sum(len(ctx_set) for ctx_set in unigram_contexts.values())
    continuation_prob = {
        w: len(ctx_set) / total_unique_bigrams for w, ctx_set in unigram_contexts.items()
    }

    context_totals = Counter()
    for (prev, w), count in bigrams.items():
        context_totals[prev] += count

    unique_follow = defaultdict(set)
    for (prev, w) in bigrams:
        unique_follow[prev].add(w)

    def prob(prev, w):
        count = bigrams.get((prev, w), 0)
        denom = context_totals.get(prev, 0)
        if denom == 0:
            return continuation_prob.get(w, 1e-9)
        first_term = max(count - discount, 0) / denom
        lambda_prev = discount * len(unique_follow[prev]) / denom
        return first_term + lambda_prev * continuation_prob.get(w, 1e-9)

    return prob

Three moving parts. continuation_prob captures "how many different contexts does this word appear in?" (the Kneser-Ney innovation). lambda_prev is the mass freed by the discount, used to weight the backoff. The final probability is the discounted main term plus the weighted continuation term.

The same idea as a formula

Interpolated Kneser-Ney, bigram case. With a discount D (usually about 0.75):

PKN​(w∣v)=c(v)max(c(v,w)−D, 0)​+λ(v)Pcont​(w)

λ(v)=c(v)D⋅​{w′:c(v,w′)>0}​​,Pcont​(w)=​{(v′,w′):c(v′,w′)>0}​​{v′:c(v′,w)>0}​​

Read it in three parts. The first term is the bigram estimate with D taken off every seen count. λ(v) is exactly the mass removed: D times the number of distinct words seen after v, so the probabilities still sum to 1. That mass is shared out by Pcont​(w), the fraction of all distinct bigram types that end in w: how many different words w follows, not how often it occurs.

Why a constant discount works. Church and Gale (1991) compared bigram counts in two halves of a large corpus: bigrams seen c times in the first half occurred on average about c−0.75 times in the second, for all but the smallest counts. Subtracting a constant is what the data says to do.

Perplexity and information. For a test text of N tokens, cross-entropy H=−N1​∑i​log2​P(wi​∣contexti​) bits per token and perplexity =2H. A model with perplexity 100 is, on average, as uncertain as a uniform choice among 100 words. Perplexities are only comparable for the same text cut into the same units: a per-token perplexity for a subword model is not a per-word perplexity.

Worked example
Kneser-Ney by hand: why "Francisco" is rare

A toy corpus has 1,000 distinct bigram types. "Francisco" occurs 50 times, always after "San". "glasses" occurs 30 times, after 25 different words ("reading glasses", "my glasses", ...). Now take a context never followed by either word, "red", with c(red)=40, 20 distinct followers, and D=0.75.

  • λ(red)=0.75×20/40=0.375: 37.5% of the probability after "red" goes to the backoff distribution.
  • Pcont​(Francisco)=1/1000=0.001; Pcont​(glasses)=25/1000=0.025.
  • PKN​(Francisco∣red)=0+0.375×0.001=0.000375; PKN​(glasses∣red)=0.375×0.025=0.0094.

Plain frequency would have ranked "Francisco" (50 occurrences) above "glasses" (30). Kneser-Ney ranks "glasses" 25 times higher after a new context, because it has already shown it can follow many different words, while "Francisco" has only ever followed one.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "With enough text, unseen n-grams stop being a problem."
    ✓ The number of possible n-grams grows as ∣V∣n; with more text, new n-grams keep appearing (Zipf's law). Smoothing is always needed.
  • ✗ "Laplace smoothing is a gentle fix."
    ✓ With a vocabulary of tens of thousands, adding 1 to every count gives most of each context's probability to words never seen there. Under "Use it" it is far worse than Kneser-Ney.
  • ✗ "Lower perplexity always means a better model."
    ✓ Only on the same text with the same tokenization and vocabulary. Mapping rare words to <unk> makes perplexity look better without making the model better.

Step 4 · generating text with sampling

import random


def generate(prob_fn, vocab, prefix, max_len=30, seed=0):
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    tokens = list(prefix)
    for _ in range(max_len):
        candidates = [(w, prob_fn(tokens[-1], w)) for w in vocab]
        total = sum(p for _, p in candidates)
        r = rng.random() * total
        acc = 0.0
        for w, p in candidates:
            acc += p
            if r <= acc:
                tokens.append(w)
                break
        if tokens[-1] == "</s>":
            break
    return tokens

Sampling proportional to probability. Always gives different output per seed. For beam-search-like output, pick the argmax at each step (greedy) and add a small randomness knob (temperature).

Step 5 · perplexity

import math


def perplexity(prob_fn, sentences):
    total_log_prob = 0.0
    total_tokens = 0
    for sentence in sentences:
        padded = ["<s>"] + sentence + ["</s>"]
        for i in range(1, len(padded)):
            p = prob_fn(padded[i - 1], padded[i])
            total_log_prob += math.log(max(p, 1e-12))
            total_tokens += 1
    return math.exp(-total_log_prob / total_tokens)

Lower is better. Under "Use it", the lesson's Kneser-Ney bigram is compared with Laplace and with GPT-2 (small, 124M parameters) on the same held-out Brown sentences, per word. The gap between counting and a neural model is large, and it is why the field moved on; exact numbers depend on vocabulary handling, so compare models only on identical test text and identical token units.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

  • Classical NLP teaching. The clearest exposure to smoothing, MLE, and perplexity you can get.
  • KenLM. Production n-gram library. Used as a rescorer in speech and MT systems where low latency matters.
  • On-device autocomplete. Trigram models in keyboards. Still.
  • Baselines. Always compute an n-gram LM perplexity before declaring your neural LM good. If your transformer does not beat KN by a wide margin, something is wrong.

Reading this output

The bigram samples are locally fluent (each pair of words is plausible) and globally meaningless: the model has a memory of one word, so a sentence wanders from topic to topic and rarely ends sensibly. The GPT-2 numbers are the lesson in evaluation. Fed the n-gram's own format, lowercased with spaces around every punctuation mark, GPT-2 does worse than the bigram (about 283 against 228): that format almost never occurs on the web, so the model is surprised by the text's form, not its content. Given the same sentences as ordinary cased prose, its perplexity drops to about 129, well below the bigram, even though it must predict every rare word in full while the n-gram had them collapsed into <unk>. Same sentences, same model, a factor of two from formatting alone: perplexity compares models only on identical text and units. With that care, a 124M-parameter transformer with 1,024 tokens of context clearly beats a counting model with one word of memory (and Brown-like text may be in its training data, so treat the exact figure as approximate).

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • n-gram models still ship in speech recognition. KenLM language models are fused into CTC beam search (NVIDIA NeMo, pyctcdecode for wav2vec 2.0) to fix spelling and domain words cheaply, and keyboards use small n-gram models on device.
  • Counting as a data tool. Deduplication (MinHash over n-gram shingles in Hugging Face datatrove and NVIDIA NeMo Curator), benchmark-contamination checks and training-data attribution in LLM pipelines rest on n-gram matching over huge corpora.
  • The training objective is the same game. Every LLM is still trained to minimise next-token cross-entropy, Shannon's prediction game, and perplexity remains the first number reported during pretraining.

Sources: KenLM, pyctcdecode, datatrove, NeMo Curator.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • An n-gram model estimates P(w∣previous n−1 words) from counts.
  • Most held-out n-grams are new; unsmoothed models give real text probability zero.
  • Kneser-Ney: subtract a constant discount, give the freed mass to a continuation distribution based on how many contexts a word follows.

  • Transformers beat n-grams by a wide margin, but counting remains a core data tool.

medium40 mintrain

A bigram language model predicts the next character from the current character only. It is the smallest model that uses context, and its loss is the same next-token cross-entropy that trains a GPT. In this task you train a table of next-character logits with gradient descent. The checks then measure it on a held-out text that your code never sees.

Task

Write two functions.

  1. train(ids, vocab_size, rng) trains the model on one long sequence of character ids and returns the model.
  2. next_log_probs(model, prev_ids) returns logP(next∣prev) for each context in prev_ids.

The model is a matrix W of shape (V,V). Row a holds the logits of the character that follows character a, so P(⋅∣a)=softmax(Wa​). The training pairs are (xt​,xt+1​) for t=0,…,T−2. Train W with gradient descent on the mean negative log-likelihood

L=−T−11​t=0∑T−2​logP(xt+1​∣xt​).

The training text is TEXT in support.py. It holds the "What it actually means" lines of the first 26 entries of the course glossary, glossary/terms.md, from "Activation Checkpointing" to "Benchmark Contamination". The text is lower case. Every character outside VOCAB is a space. VOCAB has 29 characters: the space, the letters a to z, the full stop and the comma. encode(text) gives the ids and decode(ids) gives the text back.

During training, call report(step, loss) from practice_check once for each update step. The first step is 0. The page draws these values as a live loss curve, and the checks count them.

Input and output

Name Type Shape Meaning
ids int array (T,) the character ids of the training text, from 0 to vocab_size - 1
vocab_size int the number of characters, 29
rng np.random.Generator the only source of random numbers
model any the value that train returns
prev_ids int array (M,) the context characters
  • train returns the model. The model can be any Python value, for example the matrix W.
  • next_log_probs returns a float array of shape (M, 29). Row i holds the log probabilities of the 29 characters after prev_ids[i].

Constraints

  • Budget: at most 300 update steps, so at most 300 calls to report.
  • Target: a mean negative log-likelihood of at most 2.55 nats per character on a held-out text from other glossary entries. This page does not show that text. A uniform model gets log29=3.367. A unigram model that ignores the context gets about 2.9.
  • Train with gradient descent. Pairs that never occur in the training text must keep a finite log probability.
  • Take every random number from rng. The same seed must give the same model.
  • Do not change ids.
  • Use numpy only. One full submit has a limit of 15 seconds.

Examples

ids = encode(TEXT)
model = train(ids, len(VOCAB), np.random.default_rng(1))
np.exp(next_log_probs(model, encode("q"))[:, encode("u")])

[[0.9663]]

The output is rounded to 4 decimals. After q, the model gives the letter u a probability of 0.9663. The reference starts with logits near 0, so its first reported loss is 3.3666, close to log29. After 300 steps its loss on the training text is 2.3148. A unigram model gets 2.9110 on the same text.

Your code
Common mistakes the checks catch
  • Counts each next character as its own context, so the model never learns which character follows which.
  • Swaps context and target, so the model predicts the previous character from the next one.
  • Returns the raw logits from next_log_probs and forgets the log softmax.
  • Adds the gradient to W instead of subtracting it, so the loss goes up.
  • Draws the first logits from np.random and not from the rng argument, so two runs with one seed differ.
  • Trains for 400 steps, more than the budget of 300.
Hint 1

The contexts are ids[:-1] and the targets are ids[1:]. Each pair adds one term to the loss. Start W with small random values from rng, for example with the scale 0.01.

Hint 2

For one pair with context a and target b, the gradient of −logP(b∣a) with respect to row Wa​ is softmax(Wa​)−onehot(b). The other rows get no gradient from that pair. Divide by the number of pairs for the mean.

Hint 3

You can add the gradients of all pairs at once. A one-hot matrix of the contexts, shape (T - 1, V), times W gives the logits of every pair, and its transpose times the per-pair gradient gives the gradient of W. A faster way counts each pair once: with a count matrix C, the gradient is (C.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True) * softmax(W) - C) / (T - 1).

Hint 4

A row of a rare character gets a small gradient, because few pairs start with it. Use a large learning rate, for example 50, so that these rows also learn inside 300 steps. In next_log_probs, apply a stable log softmax to the rows of W.

Reference solution and notes
import numpy as np

from practice_check import report
from support import TEXT, VOCAB, decode, encode

STEPS = 300
LEARNING_RATE = 50.0


def log_softmax(logits):
    shifted = logits - np.max(logits, axis=1, keepdims=True)
    return shifted - np.log(np.sum(np.exp(shifted), axis=1, keepdims=True))


def bigram_counts(ids, vocab_size):
    counts = np.zeros((vocab_size, vocab_size))
    np.add.at(counts, (ids[:-1], ids[1:]), 1.0)
    return counts


def train(ids, vocab_size, rng):
    W = rng.normal(scale=0.01, size=(vocab_size, vocab_size))
    counts = bigram_counts(ids, vocab_size)
    row_totals = counts.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True)
    n = counts.sum()
    for step in range(STEPS):
        log_probs = log_softmax(W)
        loss = -np.sum(counts * log_probs) / n
        report(step, float(loss))
        grad_W = (row_totals * np.exp(log_probs) - counts) / n
        W -= LEARNING_RATE * grad_W
    return W


def next_log_probs(model, prev_ids):
    return log_softmax(model[np.asarray(prev_ids, dtype=int)])

What gradient descent finds

The loss for row a is a cross-entropy between the observed next characters after a and softmax(Wa​). Its minimum is at the observed frequencies, P(b∣a)=Cab​/∑c​Cac​. These are the counts of the lesson on text generation before transformers. Gradient descent moves toward them but stops after 300 steps. A pair that never occurs keeps a small, finite probability. Early stopping acts like the smoothing that the lesson adds to the counts.

Why held-out text

The training loss rewards a model that copies the training text. The held-out text has the same kind of English but other words. The check measures how well the model predicts characters it did not see. A model with too much freedom for rare pairs gets a low training loss and a high held-out loss.

Why the counts trick is exact

All pairs with the same context and target have the same gradient. Summing them is the same as multiplying one gradient by the count. The count form is fast because its cost depends on V2, not on the length of the text.

Where this goes next

The GPT pre-training lesson uses the same loss. It replaces the table W with an embedding, attention blocks and an output layer, so the context can be many tokens long. The first loss of that model is also close to logV.

Exercises

  1. Easy. Train a trigram LM on a 1,000-sentence Shakespeare corpus. Generate 20 sentences. They will be locally plausible but globally incoherent. This is the canonical demo.
  2. Medium. Implement perplexity for your KN model on a held-out Shakespeare split. Compare against Laplace. You should see KN lower perplexity by 30-50%.
  3. Hard. Build a trigram spell corrector: given a misspelled word and its context, generate corrections and rank by context probability under the LM. Evaluate on the Birkbeck spelling corpus (public).

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Closer to 90%

83% in the run: 1.8% of held-out words, 26% of bigrams, 63% of trigrams and 83% of 4-grams were never seen in over a million words of training text. Language is productive: most specific four-word sequences are new even when every word in them is familiar. This is the sparsity that smoothing, and later neural models' shared representations, exist to handle.

SolutionB1 · Words tied to one context are over-predicted

Replacing continuation probability with plain frequency makes the backoff distribution favour words that are frequent only because of one fixed phrase ("Francisco", "Kong", "Angeles" in a news corpus). After any context the model has not seen with them, those words get far more probability than they deserve, and that mass is taken from words that really can follow many contexts. Held-out sentences in which an unseen bigram ends in a widely usable word lose probability, so perplexity rises. This variant is plain interpolated absolute discounting; the continuation trick is the whole of Kneser-Ney's advantage over it.
