In plain words first
The idea in one line

The learning rate is the single most important hyperparameter. Not the architecture. Not the dataset size. Not the activation function. The learning rate. If you tune nothing else, tune this.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
Learning rate "How fast the model learns" The scalar that multiplies the gradient to determine the parameter update size
Schedule "Change the LR over time" A function that maps training step to learning rate, designed to optimize convergence
Warmup "Start with a small LR" Linearly ramping the LR from near-zero to the target value over the first $N$ steps to stabilize optimizer statistics
Cosine annealing "Smooth LR decay" Decreasing the LR following a cosine curve from $\text{lr} {\max}$ to $\text{lr} $ over training
Step decay "Drop LR at milestones" Multiplying the LR by a factor (usually 0.1) at fixed epoch intervals
1cycle policy "Up then down" Leslie Smith's method of ramping LR up then down in a single cycle for faster convergence
LR range test "Find the best learning rate" Training briefly while increasing LR to find the value where loss starts diverging
Cosine with warm restarts "Reset and repeat" Periodically resetting the LR to $\text{lr} _{\max}$ and decaying again (SGDR)
Eta min "The floor for the LR" The minimum learning rate that the schedule decays to
Peak learning rate "The maximum LR" The highest LR reached during training, typically after warmup

The problem

In plain words first

A learning-rate schedule changes the step size during training. Early on, warmup starts small and ramps up, so the first noisy updates cannot throw the model somewhere bad. Later, decay (cosine, step or 1cycle) shrinks the steps, so the model can settle into a minimum instead of bouncing around it. The peak value still matters most; the schedule decides how you get there and how you finish.

Set the learning rate to 0.1. Training diverges -- loss jumps to infinity in 3 steps. Set it to 0.0001. Training crawls -- after 100 epochs, the model has barely moved from random. Set it to 0.01. Training works for 50 epochs, then the loss oscillates around a minimum it can never reach because the steps are too large.

The optimal learning rate is not a constant. It changes during training. Early on, you want large steps to cover ground quickly. Late in training, you want tiny steps to settle into a sharp minimum. The difference between a 90% accurate model and a 95% accurate model is often just the schedule.

Every major model published in the last three years uses a learning rate schedule. Llama 2 (7B and 13B) used a peak of 3e-4 with 2,000 warmup steps and cosine decay to 10 percent of the peak; Llama 3 405B used a peak of 8e-5 with 8,000 warmup steps, decaying to 8e-7 over 1.2 million steps. GPT-3 175B used a peak of 6e-5 with warmup over 375 million tokens. These are not arbitrary choices. They are the result of extensive hyperparameter sweeps that cost millions of dollars.

You need to understand schedules because the defaults will not work for your problem. When you fine-tune a pretrained model, the right schedule is different than training from scratch. When you increase batch size, the warmup period needs to change. When training breaks at step 10,000, you need to know whether it's a schedule problem or something else.

Idea 1 · Constant Learning Rate

The simplest approach. Pick a number, use it for every step.

lr(t)=lr0​

Rarely optimal. It's either too high for the end of training (oscillation around the minimum) or too low for the beginning (wasted compute on tiny steps). Works fine for small models and debugging. A terrible choice for anything that trains for more than an hour.

Idea 2 · Step Decay

The old-school approach from the ResNet era. Cut the learning rate by a factor (usually 10x) at fixed epochs.

lr(t)=lr0​⋅γ⌊epoch/step_size⌋

Where γ=0.1 and step_size = 30 means: lr drops by 10x every 30 epochs. ResNet-50 used this -- lr=0.1, drop by 10x at epochs 30, 60, and 90.

The problem: the optimal decay points depend on the dataset and architecture. Move to a different problem and you need to re-tune when to drop. The transitions are abrupt -- loss can spike when the rate suddenly changes.

Idea 3 · Cosine Annealing

Smooth decay from the maximum learning rate to a minimum, following a cosine curve:

lr(t)=lrmin​+0.5⋅(lrmax​−lrmin​)⋅(1+cos(Tπt​))

Where t is the current step and T is the total number of steps.

At t=0, the cosine term is 1, so lr=lrmax​. At t=T, the cosine term is -1, so lr=lrmin​. The decay is gentle at first, accelerates in the middle, and becomes gentle again near the end.

This is the default for most modern training runs. No hyperparameters to tune beyond lrmax​ and lrmin​. The cosine shape matches the empirical observation that most learning happens in the middle of training -- you want reasonable step sizes during that critical period.

Idea 4 · Warmup: Why You Start Small

Adam and other adaptive optimizers maintain running estimates of gradient mean and variance. At step 0, these estimates are initialized to zero. The first few gradient updates are based on garbage statistics. If your learning rate is large during this period, the model takes huge, poorly-directed steps.

Warmup fixes this. Start with a tiny learning rate (often lr_max / warmup_steps or even zero) and linearly ramp up to lrmax​ over the first N steps. By the time you reach the full learning rate, Adam's statistics have stabilized.

lr(t)=lrmax​⋅warmup_stepst​for t<warmup_steps

Typical warmup: 1-5% of total training steps. Llama 2 trained on about 2 trillion tokens and warmed up for 2,000 steps. GPT-3 warmed up over 375 million tokens.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "A decaying schedule always beats a constant learning rate."
    ✓ When gradients are nearly exact, as on Step 4's small, easy dataset, a constant rate can win. Decay pays off when gradient noise sets a floor on the loss, as in the picture under Step 4.
  • ✗ "Warmup only exists because Adam's statistics start at zero."
    ✓ That is one reason. Warmup also helps plain SGD at large batch sizes (Goyal et al., 2017) and protects against the large, badly aimed gradients of a freshly initialised network.
  • ✗ "Copy the published learning rate."
    ✓ Peaks shrink as models grow: 3e-4 for Llama 2 7B, 8e-5 for Llama 3 405B, 6e-5 for GPT-3 175B. Batch size, model size and optimiser all move the right value; run a range test.

Idea 5 · Linear Warmup + Cosine Decay

The modern default. Ramp up linearly, then decay with cosine:

if t < warmup_steps:
    lr(t) = lr_max * (t / warmup_steps)
else:
    progress = (t - warmup_steps) / (total_steps - warmup_steps)
    lr(t) = lr_min + 0.5 * (lr_max - lr_min) * (1 + cos(pi * progress))

This is what Llama, GPT, PaLM, and most modern transformers use. The warmup prevents early instability. The cosine decay settles the model into a good minimum.

Idea 6 · 1cycle Policy

Leslie Smith's discovery (2018): ramp the learning rate up from a low value to a high value in the first half of training, then ramp it back down in the second half. Counterintuitive -- why would you increase the learning rate midway through?

The theory: a high learning rate acts as regularization by adding noise to the optimization trajectory. The model explores more of the loss landscape during the ramp-up phase, finding better basins. The ramp-down phase then refines within the best basin found.

Phase 1 (0 to T/2):    lr ramps from lr_max/25 to lr_max
Phase 2 (T/2 to T):    lr ramps from lr_max to lr_max/10000

1cycle often trains faster than cosine annealing for a fixed compute budget. The tradeoff: you must know the total number of steps in advance.

Idea 9 · Real Numbers from Published Models

The same idea as a formula

For step t out of T, peak rate ηmax​, final rate ηmin​:

  • Step decay: ηt​=η0​⋅γ⌊t/s⌋ (multiply by γ, e.g. 0.1, every s steps).
  • Linear warm-up over Tw​ steps: ηt​=ηmax​t/Tw​.
  • Cosine decay after warm-up: ηt​=ηmin​+21​(ηmax​−ηmin​)(1+cos(πT−Tw​t−Tw​​)).
  • Warm-up, stable, decay (WSD): warm up, hold ηmax​ for most of training, then decay quickly over the last 10–20%. A checkpoint from the stable phase can be decayed at any time, so training can be extended without restarting.
  • 1cycle: rise to ηmax​ over about 30% of training, then fall far below the start.

Why warm up. At the start the weights are random and Adam's second-moment estimate v^ rests on a handful of gradients, so its per-weight steps are unreliable; a large rate then can push the model into a region it never recovers from. A short ramp (often 0.1–2% of the steps) avoids it.

Why decay. Near the end, the noise in mini-batch gradients keeps the weights bouncing around a minimum at a distance that grows with η; lowering η lets them settle, which is why the loss often drops sharply during the final decay.

Step 1 · Schedule Functions

Each function takes the current step and returns the learning rate at that step.

import math


def constant_schedule(step, lr=0.01, **kwargs):
    return lr


def step_decay_schedule(step, lr=0.1, step_size=100, gamma=0.1, **kwargs):
    return lr * (gamma ** (step // step_size))


def cosine_schedule(step, lr=0.01, total_steps=1000, lr_min=1e-5, **kwargs):
    if step >= total_steps:
        return lr_min
    return lr_min + 0.5 * (lr - lr_min) * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * step / total_steps))


def warmup_cosine_schedule(step, lr=0.01, total_steps=1000, warmup_steps=100, lr_min=1e-5, **kwargs):
    if total_steps <= warmup_steps:
        return lr * (step / max(warmup_steps, 1))
    if step < warmup_steps:
        return lr * step / warmup_steps
    progress = (step - warmup_steps) / (total_steps - warmup_steps)
    return lr_min + 0.5 * (lr - lr_min) * (1 + math.cos(math.pi * progress))


def one_cycle_schedule(step, lr=0.01, total_steps=1000, **kwargs):
    mid = max(total_steps // 2, 1)
    if step < mid:
        return (lr / 25) + (lr - lr / 25) * step / mid
    else:
        progress = (step - mid) / max(total_steps - mid, 1)
        return lr * (1 - progress) + (lr / 10000) * progress

Step 3 · Training Network

A simple two-layer network on the circle dataset, same as previous lessons, but now we vary the schedule.

import random


def sigmoid(x):
    x = max(-500, min(500, x))
    return 1.0 / (1.0 + math.exp(-x))


def relu(x):
    return max(0.0, x)


def relu_deriv(x):
    return 1.0 if x > 0 else 0.0


def make_circle_data(n=200, seed=42):
    random.seed(seed)
    data = []
    for _ in range(n):
        x = random.uniform(-2, 2)
        y = random.uniform(-2, 2)
        label = 1.0 if x * x + y * y < 1.5 else 0.0
        data.append(([x, y], label))
    return data


def train_with_schedule(schedule_fn, schedule_name, data, epochs=300, base_lr=0.05, **kwargs):
    random.seed(0)
    hidden_size = 8
    total_steps = epochs * len(data)

    std = math.sqrt(2.0 / 2)
    w1 = [[random.gauss(0, std) for _ in range(2)] for _ in range(hidden_size)]
    b1 = [0.0] * hidden_size
    w2 = [random.gauss(0, std) for _ in range(hidden_size)]
    b2 = 0.0

    step = 0
    epoch_losses = []

    for epoch in range(epochs):
        total_loss = 0
        correct = 0

        for x, target in data:
            lr = schedule_fn(step, lr=base_lr, total_steps=total_steps, **kwargs)

            z1 = []
            h = []
            for i in range(hidden_size):
                z = w1[i][0] * x[0] + w1[i][1] * x[1] + b1[i]
                z1.append(z)
                h.append(relu(z))

            z2 = sum(w2[i] * h[i] for i in range(hidden_size)) + b2
            out = sigmoid(z2)

            error = out - target
            d_out = error * out * (1 - out)

            for i in range(hidden_size):
                d_h = d_out * w2[i] * relu_deriv(z1[i])
                w2[i] -= lr * d_out * h[i]
                for j in range(2):
                    w1[i][j] -= lr * d_h * x[j]
                b1[i] -= lr * d_h
            b2 -= lr * d_out

            total_loss += (out - target) ** 2
            if (out >= 0.5) == (target >= 0.5):
                correct += 1
            step += 1

        avg_loss = total_loss / len(data)
        accuracy = correct / len(data) * 100
        epoch_losses.append(avg_loss)

    return epoch_losses

import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def noisy_bowl(schedule_fn, steps=3000, peak=0.15, **kw):
    rng = random.Random(0)
    w, curvature, losses = [3.0, -2.0], [1.0, 10.0], []
    for t in range(steps):
        lr = schedule_fn(t, lr=peak, total_steps=steps, **kw)
        grads = [c * wi + rng.gauss(0, 1) for c, wi in zip(curvature, w)]
        w = [wi - lr * g for wi, g in zip(w, grads)]
        losses.append(0.5 * sum(c * wi * wi for c, wi in zip(curvature, w)))
    return [sum(losses[i:i + 50]) / 50 for i in range(0, steps, 50)]

Step 5 · LR Too High vs Too Low

Demonstrate the three failure modes: too high (divergence), too low (crawling), and just right.

def lr_sensitivity(data):
    learning_rates = [1.0, 0.1, 0.01, 0.001, 0.0001]

    print("\nLR Sensitivity (constant schedule, 100 epochs):")
    print(f"  {'LR':>10} {'Start Loss':>12} {'End Loss':>12} {'Status':>15}")
    print("  " + "-" * 52)

    for lr in learning_rates:
        losses = train_with_schedule(constant_schedule, f"lr={lr}", data, epochs=100, base_lr=lr)
        start = losses[0]
        end = losses[-1]

        if end > start or math.isnan(end) or end > 1.0:
            status = "DIVERGED"
        elif end > start * 0.9:
            status = "BARELY MOVED"
        elif end < 0.15:
            status = "CONVERGED"
        else:
            status = "LEARNING"

        end_str = f"{end:.6f}" if not math.isnan(end) else "NaN"
        print(f"  {lr:>10.4f} {start:>12.6f} {end_str:>12} {status:>15}")

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • The peak learning rate matters most; the schedule shapes the start and the finish.
  • Warmup ramps up from near zero so early noisy updates cannot do damage.
  • Cosine decay is the common default; step decay and 1cycle are alternatives.
  • Decay matters when gradient noise sets a loss floor; on easy, nearly noiseless problems a constant rate can do fine.
  • Schedules need the total number of steps; make sure the code handles running past it.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · The constant rate

Constant ends lowest (0.0054), ahead of 1cycle (0.0082), warmup + cosine (0.0084), cosine (0.0084) and step decay (0.0145). On an easy problem with one example per step and accurate gradients, keeping the step size up simply means more progress. Step decay does worst because its first drop, at epoch 75, cuts the rate to 0.005 while the network still has ground to cover.

SolutionB1 · Schedules past their end

one_cycle_schedule returns about −0.01: a negative learning rate, so every step climbs the loss instead of descending it. warmup_cosine_schedule returns 0.0059 and rising, because the cosine is periodic and starts back up; by step 2,000 it is back to 0.0097, almost the peak. Clamp the progress at 1, as cosine_schedule does with its if step >= total_steps line. PyTorch's OneCycleLR refuses to step past its total and raises an error instead.

Use it: the same idea with real libraries

PyTorch provides schedulers in torch.optim.lr_scheduler:

import torch
import torch.nn as nn
import torch.optim as optim
from torch.optim.lr_scheduler import CosineAnnealingLR, OneCycleLR, StepLR

torch.manual_seed(0)
model = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(10, 64), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(64, 1))
optimizer = optim.Adam(model.parameters(), lr=3e-4)
X, y = torch.randn(256, 10), torch.randn(256, 1)

def train_step(model, optimizer):
    optimizer.zero_grad()
    loss = nn.functional.mse_loss(model(X), y)
    loss.backward()
    optimizer.step()
    return loss.item()

scheduler = CosineAnnealingLR(optimizer, T_max=1000, eta_min=1e-5)

for step in range(1000):
    loss = train_step(model, optimizer)
    scheduler.step()
    if step in (0, 499, 999):
        print(f"step {step:4d}: lr {scheduler.get_last_lr()[0]:.6f}, loss {loss:.4f}")

easy20 minlab

The learning rate decides if gradient descent converges, crawls or blows up. A rate sweep is the first experiment on any new model: train with each rate, keep the final loss, and pick the rate. In this lab you run the sweep on a small linear regression and compare the result with the bound that theory gives.

support.py gives the data, the grid of rates and the trainer. make_regression(seed) returns X of shape (64, 4) and y of shape (64,). train(X, y, lr, steps) runs full batch gradient descent on the mean squared error from w=0. It returns the loss before each step and after the last step, an array of steps + 1 values. When the loss passes 1e10, the trainer stops and fills the rest of the curve with inf.

Task

Write five helper functions and answers().

  1. run_sweep(X, y, lrs, steps=STEPS) returns a float array of shape (len(lrs), steps + 1). Row i is train(X, y, lrs[i], steps).
  2. is_diverged(losses) returns True when the final loss is not finite or is larger than the first loss. Otherwise it returns False.
  3. best_lr(lrs, curves) returns the rate with the lowest final loss among the runs that did not diverge. If two runs have the same final loss, return the smaller rate. If every run diverged, return None.
  4. smallest_diverging_lr(lrs, curves) returns the smallest rate, by value, whose run diverged. If no run diverged, return None.
  5. stability_bound(X) returns 2/λmax​, where λmax​ is the largest eigenvalue of the Hessian H=n2​XTX of the mean squared error.

Then do the experiment in answers().

  1. Make the data with make_regression(seed=0).
  2. Change the learning rate over LEARNING_RATES. Train each rate for STEPS steps.
  3. Measure the final loss of each run. Report the best rate as best_lr and its final loss as best_final_loss.
  4. Report the smallest rate that diverged as smallest_diverging_lr.
  5. Compute the theory bound on the same X. Report it as stability_bound.

Your code
Common mistakes the checks catch
  • Marks a run as diverged only when its final loss is not finite, so a finite loss that grows counts as stable.
  • Returns the first diverged rate in list order, not the smallest diverged rate.
  • Uses X^T X / n as the Hessian and forgets the factor 2, so the bound is twice too large.
  • Calls np.argmin on the raw final losses, so a NaN run becomes the best rate.
  • Picks the larger rate when two runs have the same final loss.
Hint 1

Read the final loss with losses[-1] and the first loss with losses[0]. np.isfinite is False for both inf and nan.

Hint 2

Do not call np.argmin on the final losses directly. A nan in the array makes np.argmin return the index of the nan. Skip the diverged runs first.

Hint 3

The mean squared error is n1​∥Xw−y∥2. Its Hessian is n2​XTX. np.linalg.eigvalsh returns the eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix.

Hint 4

For a quadratic loss, one step multiplies the error along each eigenvector by 1−ηλ. The run is stable only when ∣1−ηλmax​∣<1, so η<2/λmax​.

Reference solution and notes
import numpy as np

from support import LEARNING_RATES, STEPS, make_regression, train


def run_sweep(X, y, lrs, steps=STEPS):
    return np.array([train(X, y, lr, steps) for lr in lrs])


def is_diverged(losses):
    final = losses[-1]
    return bool(not np.isfinite(final) or final > losses[0])


def best_lr(lrs, curves):
    best = None
    for lr, losses in zip(lrs, curves):
        if is_diverged(losses):
            continue
        final = losses[-1]
        if best is None or final < best[1] or (final == best[1] and lr < best[0]):
            best = (lr, final)
    return None if best is None else best[0]


def smallest_diverging_lr(lrs, curves):
    diverged = [lr for lr, losses in zip(lrs, curves) if is_diverged(losses)]
    return min(diverged) if diverged else None


def stability_bound(X):
    n = X.shape[0]
    hessian = (2.0 / n) * (X.T @ X)
    return float(2.0 / np.max(np.linalg.eigvalsh(hessian)))


def answers():
    X, y = make_regression(seed=0)
    curves = run_sweep(X, y, LEARNING_RATES)
    best = best_lr(LEARNING_RATES, curves)
    return {
        "best_lr": float(best),
        "best_final_loss": float(curves[LEARNING_RATES.index(best)][-1]),
        "smallest_diverging_lr": float(smallest_diverging_lr(LEARNING_RATES, curves)),
        "stability_bound": stability_bound(X),
    }

What the sweep shows

The final loss falls as the rate grows, from 1.29 at 0.001 to 0.0104 at 0.2. At 0.3 every run blows up. The theory bound is 0.2277, so the best rate on the grid is the largest rate below the bound. This is a common result: the best constant rate is close to the edge of stability.

Why the bound is exact here

The loss is a quadratic in w. Gradient descent moves the error along each eigenvector of the Hessian by a factor of 1−ηλi​ per step. When η>2/λmax​, that factor has a magnitude above 1 for the largest eigenvalue, and the error grows each step. The feature with scale 2 gives the largest eigenvalue, so it sets the bound for all the weights.

The trap

A sweep that stores nan for a diverged run and then calls np.argmin reports the diverged rate as the best one. Mark the diverged runs first. A finite loss that ends above its start is also a divergence.

Where this goes next

A neural network has no single fixed Hessian, so the bound moves during training. That is one reason for warmup and decay in the learning rate schedule lesson, and the reason that the LR range test sweeps rates during one run.

Take-homeTake-home. Implement a learning rate schedule as one function of the step: a linear ramp from zero to a peak, a cosine decay from the peak to a floor, and a fixed floor after the last step. Deliver tests that pin the value at every boundary, prove the curve has no jumps, and show that a resumed run lands on the right value. Describe your design and your tests.

Level 4 of 4 · about 25 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Write one function with three branches: up to the warmup step, the peak times the step over the warmup length; between the warmup step and the total, the floor plus half the gap times one plus the cosine of pi times the progress since warmup ended; past the total, the floor. Test the fixed points: zero at step zero, the peak at the end of warmup from both branches, the floor at the last step and the floor one step later. Add a continuity test that bounds each one step change, and a resume test that computes the schedule from a saved global step.

The function.

if warmup_steps > 0 and step <= warmup_steps:
    lr = lr_max * step / warmup_steps
elif step <= total_steps:
    progress = (step - warmup_steps) / (total_steps - warmup_steps)
    lr = lr_min + 0.5 * (lr_max - lr_min) * (1 + cos(pi * progress))
else:
    lr = lr_min

The cosine evaluates to 1 at the end of warmup, which gives the peak and matches the ramp's endpoint, and to -1 at the total, which gives the floor. The capstone lesson makes the point that this continuity is the reason to write one function over the step rather than three pieces glued together: a glued schedule loses a boundary the first time the peak or a step count changes. Two guards: the ramp branch runs only when the warmup is above zero, so a warmup of zero steps starts at the peak and decays at once, with no division by zero. The constructor rejects a warmup that is not less than the total, as the lesson's code does with an error, because the rate would hold the peak at the last step and drop to the floor one step later.

Why the floor is pinned, not an error.

A trainer that overshoots the configured total keeps a usable rate and logs a warning. Extending a run means changing the total in the config, not editing the loop. The floor is non-zero on purpose: the lesson keeps it near 10 percent of the peak so the optimizer keeps learning in the long tail, and warns that a floor of zero looks good on a plot and leaves the model under-trained.

The tests.

  1. Fixed points. With a non-zero warmup, lr(0) is 0, lr(warmup) equals the peak from the ramp branch and from the cosine branch, lr(total) equals the floor, lr(total + 1) equals the floor.
  2. Continuity. For every step from 0 to total, the change between consecutive steps is at most the larger of the warmup slope, peak over warmup steps, and the steepest cosine slope, pi times the gap over twice the decay length. The lesson's exercise notes that a short cosine segment can be steeper than the ramp, so the ramp slope alone is not a safe bound.
  3. Resume. Save a checkpoint with a global step, reload, and assert that the next value equals lr(global step + 1). The schedule reads a monotonic global step from the checkpoint, not an epoch counter or a loop variable, because sharded datasets and restarted loaders break the epoch to step mapping.
  4. Shape. Render the schedule to a text plot and a CSV, with one row per step, and check the plot into the run directory so a reviewer can see the curve without running anything.

Production notes from the lesson.

The schedule's four numbers live in a versioned config, not in code. The warmup is a fraction of the total, around 1 to 3 percent, so the shape survives a longer run. The log row is step, learning rate, gradient L2 norm and loss, in that order, so dashboards can read it.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Set the floor to zero so the schedule finishes cleanly." The lesson keeps a non-zero floor near 10 percent of the peak so the optimizer keeps learning in the tail; a zero floor looks clean and leaves the model under-trained.
  • "Test a few steps in the middle, the boundaries follow from the formula." An off by one at a boundary is the bug the lesson warns about; it shows up hours into a run as a rate that is 1 percent off at the moment it matters.
  • "Compute the rate from the epoch and the batch index." Sharded datasets and restarted loaders break that mapping; the global step is the durable axis.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) Why implement the three regions in one function? Expected: so the ramp endpoint and the cosine start share one formula for the peak, and a change to the peak or the step counts cannot open a gap at a boundary.
  • (level 4) What is the right bound for the continuity test? Expected: the larger of the ramp slope, peak over warmup steps, and the steepest cosine slope, pi times the gap over twice the decay length; a short cosine segment can be steeper than the ramp.
  • (level 4) Why pin at the floor after the last step instead of raising? Expected: a trainer that overshoots keeps a usable rate and logs a warning; extending training means changing the total in the config, not the loop.
  • (level 4) What makes a resumed run land on the right value? Expected: the schedule reads a monotonic global step saved in the checkpoint, not an epoch counter or a local loop variable.

Explain the mechanismA transformer recipe starts the learning rate at zero and ramps it linearly up to the peak during the first steps, then decays it with a cosine. A teammate wants to start at the peak to save those steps. Explain what the ramp protects, how long it should be, and how to write it down so the recipe scales to a longer run.

Level 3 of 4 · about 10 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. The first updates are the fragile ones: the weights sit near their initialization, Adam's running estimates of the gradient mean and variance are built from a handful of samples, and the gradient norm is large and noisy. A full size step on that footing diverges or drops the model into a plateau it never leaves. Warmup keeps the effective step small until the statistics settle, over about 1 to 5 percent of the total steps, and the schedule should express that length as a fraction of the run rather than as a fixed step count.

What is wrong at step zero.

Adam's two moment estimates start at zero. Bias correction fixes their scale, but the first steps still use statistics from one or two gradients, which the schedules lesson calls garbage statistics. At the same time the weights are at their random initialization and the gradient norm is at its largest and noisiest. A peak step on that mix is a large, poorly directed move. The capstone lesson gives the two outcomes: the run diverges outright, or it settles into a loss plateau it never escapes.

What warmup does.

lr(t) = lr_max * t / warmup_steps     for t below warmup_steps

The rate climbs from zero to the peak over the warmup steps. By the time the full rate arrives, the moment estimates have seen hundreds of gradients and the weights have moved off their starting point. The schedules lesson's examples of published runs use a warmup of 2000 steps to a peak of 3e-4 followed by a cosine decay to 3e-5, and a warmup over 375 million tokens to a peak of 6e-4.

How long.

The schedules lesson gives 1 to 5 percent of the total steps and, when in doubt, 3 to 5 percent. The capstone lesson gives 1 to 3 percent and makes the stronger point: write the warmup as a fraction. A run of 200 million steps with 2,000 warmup steps is at the peak almost at once, and a run of 20,000 steps with the same count warms up for 10 percent. The same number describes two different schedules.

How to see that it worked.

Log the gradient norm next to the learning rate at each step. In a well tuned warmup the norm rises roughly in step with the rate and settles after the peak. A norm that stays high after the ramp ends says the peak is too aggressive. A divergent run shows the norm spike before the loss does.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Warmup wastes steps at a low rate." The lessons spend 1 to 5 percent of training on it, and a run that diverges in its first steps wastes all of them.
  • "Bias correction already handles Adam's cold start, so warmup is redundant." The correction rescales the averages; it cannot turn two samples into a good estimate.
  • "Set the warmup to 2000 steps, the big runs use that." That count was a fraction of one specific run; on a short run it is most of the training, and on a longer one it is nothing.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) Why is Adam sensitive at step zero in particular? Expected: its moment estimates start at zero and the first steps use statistics from one or two gradients; bias correction fixes their scale but not their noise.
  • (level 3) What does the gradient norm look like during a healthy warmup? Expected: it rises roughly in step with the learning rate and settles after the peak; a norm that stays high after the ramp says the peak is too aggressive.
  • (level 4) Why does a fixed warmup step count break when the run gets longer? Expected: 2000 steps is 10 percent of a 20,000 step run and a vanishing share of a 200 million step run, so the same count gives two different schedules; a fraction keeps the shape.
  • (level 4) You change the batch size. What do you revisit? Expected: the warmup length and the peak; the schedules lesson lists the warmup period as the setting that must change when the batch size changes.

Check your understanding

Q1Why is a constant learning rate usually suboptimal for training neural networks?

The optimal step size changes during training. Early on, large steps cover ground quickly. Late in training, small steps are needed to settle into a minimum. A constant rate can't serve both needs.

Q2What is the purpose of learning rate warmup?

Adam's moment estimates are initialized to zero. Early gradient updates are based on unreliable statistics. Warmup starts with a tiny LR and ramps up, giving Adam time to accumulate meaningful estimates.

Q3What learning rate schedule do Llama 3, GPT-3, and most modern LLMs use?

Linear warmup + cosine decay is the standard for transformer training. Llama 3 used 2000 warmup steps with cosine decay from 3e-4 to 3e-5. This schedule requires no milestone tuning.

Q4What makes the 1cycle policy different from other schedules?

Leslie Smith's 1cycle ramps LR from low to high (first half) then high to very low (second half). The high-LR phase helps the model explore more of the loss landscape before settling into the best basin.

Q5If training loss suddenly spikes and diverges, what is the most likely learning rate issue?

A learning rate that's too high causes the optimizer to take steps larger than the loss basin, overshooting the minimum and causing the loss to increase. This manifests as sudden divergence.
