The idea in one line

Probability is the language AI uses to express uncertainty.

Terms you'll hear
Term What people say What it actually means
Sample space "All the possibilities" The set $S$ of every possible outcome of an experiment
PMF "The probability function" A function that gives the exact probability of each discrete outcome, summing to 1
PDF "The probability curve" A density function for continuous variables. Integrate it over an interval to get probability
Conditional probability "Probability given something" $P(A \mid B) = P(A \text{ and } B) / P(B)$. The foundation of Bayesian thinking and Bayes' theorem
Independence "They don't affect each other" $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \cdot P(B)$. Knowing one event tells you nothing about the other
Expected value "The average" The probability-weighted sum of all outcomes. The loss function is an expected value
Variance "How spread out" The expected squared deviation from the mean. High variance = noisy, unstable estimates
Normal distribution "The bell curve" $f(x) = (1/\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}) \cdot \exp(-(x-\mu)^2/(2\sigma^2))$. Appears everywhere due to the CLT
Central Limit Theorem "Averages become normal" The centered, scaled mean of many independent samples converges to a normal distribution for any source with finite variance
Joint distribution "Two variables together" $P(X, Y)$ describes the probability of every combination of $X$ and $Y$ outcomes
Marginal distribution "Sum out the other variable" $P(X) = \sum_y P(X, Y)$. Recovers one variable's distribution from the joint
Log probability "Log of the probability" $\log P(x)$. Turns products into sums, preventing numerical underflow in long sequences
Softmax "Turn scores into probabilities" $\text{softmax}(z_i) = \exp(z_i) / \sum_j \exp(z_j)$. Maps real-valued logits to a valid probability distribution
Cross-entropy "The loss function" $-\sum p_{\text{true}} \cdot \log(p_{\text{predicted}})$. Measures how different two distributions are. Lower is better
Logits "Raw model outputs" Unnormalized scores before softmax. Named after the logistic function
Sampling "Drawing random values" Generating values according to a probability distribution. How models generate output

The problem

In plain words first

A probability is a number from 0 to 1 saying how likely something is, and a distribution lists the probability of every possible outcome. A classifier's output is a distribution over classes; a language model's output is a distribution over the next word. Two summaries describe a distribution: its expected value (the average outcome) and its variance (how spread out the outcomes are). Computers work with the logarithms of probabilities, because multiplying many small numbers underflows to zero.

A classifier outputs [0.03, 0.91, 0.06]. A language model picks the next word from 50,000 candidates. A diffusion model generates images by sampling from learned distributions. All of these are probability in action.

Every prediction a model makes is a probability distribution. Every loss function measures how far the predicted distribution is from the true one. Every training step adjusts parameters to make one distribution look more like another. Without probability, you cannot read a single ML paper, debug a single model, or understand why your training loss is NaN.

Idea 1 · Events, Sample Spaces, and Probability

The sample space S is the set of all possible outcomes. An event is a subset of the sample space. Probability maps events to numbers between 0 and 1.

Coin flip:
  S = {H, T}
  P(H) = 0.5,  P(T) = 0.5

Single die roll:
  S = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}
  P(even) = P({2, 4, 6}) = 3/6 = 0.5

Three axioms define all of probability: 1. P(A)≥0 for any event A 2. P(S)=1 (something always happens) 3. P(A or B)=P(A)+P(B) when A and B cannot both occur

Everything else (Bayes' theorem, expectations, distributions) follows from these three rules.

Idea 2 · Conditional Probability and Independence

P(A∣B) is the probability of A given that B happened.

P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B)

Example: deck of cards
  P(King | Face card) = P(King and Face card) / P(Face card)
                      = (4/52) / (12/52)
                      = 4/12 = 1/3

Two events are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other:

Independent:Equivalent to:​P(A∣B)=P(A)P(A and B)=P(A)⋅P(B)​

Coin flips are independent. Drawing cards without replacement is not.

Idea 3 · Probability Mass Functions vs Probability Density Functions

Discrete random variables have a probability mass function (PMF). Each outcome has a specific probability that you can read off directly.

PMF: P(X = k)

Fair die:
  P(X = 1) = 1/6
  P(X = 2) = 1/6
  ...
  P(X = 6) = 1/6

  Sum of all probabilities = 1

Continuous random variables have a probability density function (PDF). The density at a single point is not a probability. Probability comes from integrating the density over an interval.

PDF: f(x)

P(a <= X <= b) = integral of f(x) from a to b

f(x) can be greater than 1 (density, not probability)
integral from -inf to +inf of f(x) dx = 1

This distinction matters in ML. Classification outputs are PMFs (discrete choices). VAE latent spaces use PDFs (continuous).

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "A probability density can never be above 1."
    ✓ Densities can be any size; only their area must be 1. A normal distribution with σ = 0.1 has density 3.99 at its peak.
  • ✗ "Independent events can't happen together."
    ✓ That describes mutually exclusive events. Independent events can happen together; one just tells you nothing about the other. Two coin flips are independent and can both be heads.
  • ✗ "A softmax output of 0.91 means the model is right 91 percent of the time."
    ✓ Softmax always produces numbers that sum to 1. Whether 0.91 matches real-world frequency is a separate property, calibration, that many models lack.

Idea 4 · Common Distributions

Bernoulli: one trial, two outcomes. Models binary classification.

P(X=1)P(X=0)Mean=p,​=p=1−pVariance=p(1−p)​

Categorical: one trial, k outcomes. Models multi-class classification (softmax output).

​P(X=i)=pi​,where ∑i​pi​=1Example: P(cat)=0.7,P(dog)=0.2,P(bird)=0.1​

Uniform: all outcomes equally likely. Used for random initialization.

Discrete:Continuous:​P(X=k)=1/n for k∈{1,…,n}f(x)=1/(b−a) for x∈[a,b]​

Normal (Gaussian): the bell curve. Parameterized by mean (μ) and variance (σ2).

f(x) = (1 / sqrt(2*pi*sigma^2)) * exp(-(x - mu)^2 / (2*sigma^2))

Standard normal: mu = 0, sigma = 1
  68% of data within 1 sigma
  95% within 2 sigma
  99.7% within 3 sigma

Poisson: counts of rare events in a fixed interval. Models event rates.

P(X=k)Mean=λ,​=k!λke−λ​Variance=λ​

Idea 5 · Expected Value and Variance

Expected value is the weighted average outcome.

Discrete:Continuous:​E[X]=i∑​xi​P(X=xi​)E[X]=∫xf(x)dx​

Variance measures spread around the mean.

Var(X)Standard deviation​=E[(X−E[X])2]=E[X2]−(E[X])2=Var(X)​​

In ML, expected value appears as the loss function (average loss over the data distribution). Variance tells you about model stability. High variance in gradients means noisy training.

Worked example
Worked by hand

A fair die: each face 1 to 6 has probability 1/6. Expected value: (1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6) / 6 = 21 / 6 = 3.5, a value the die can never show. Variance: the average of the squared distances from 3.5, (6.25 + 2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 2.25 + 6.25) / 6 = 17.5 / 6 = 2.9167, so the standard deviation is about 1.71. The lesson's expected_value and variance functions return exactly these numbers for range(1, 7) with probabilities [1/6] * 6.

Idea 6 · Joint and Marginal Distributions

A joint distribution P(X,Y) describes two random variables together.

Joint PMF example (X = weather, Y = umbrella):

Y=0 (no umbrella) Y=1 (umbrella) Marginal P(X)
X=0 (sun) 0.40 0.10 P(X=0)=0.50
X=1 (rain) 0.05 0.45 P(X=1)=0.50
Marginal P(Y) P(Y=0)=0.45 P(Y=1)=0.55 1.00

The marginal distribution sums out the other variable:

P(X=x)=y∑​P(X=x,Y=y)

The row and column totals in the table above are the marginals.

Idea 7 · Why the Normal Distribution Shows Up Everywhere

The Central Limit Theorem: the average of many independent random variables from the same distribution, once centered and scaled by n​, approaches a normal distribution, whatever the shape of the original distribution, as long as its variance is finite. The same holds for the centered and scaled sum.

Roll 1 die:  uniform distribution (flat)
Average of 2 dice:  triangular (peaked)
Average of 30 dice: nearly perfect bell curve

This works for ANY starting distribution with finite variance.

This is why: - Measurement errors are approximately normal (many small independent sources) - Weight initializations in neural networks use normal distributions - Gradient noise in SGD is approximately normal (sum of many sample gradients) - The normal distribution is the maximum entropy distribution for a given mean and variance

Idea 8 · Log Probabilities

Raw probabilities cause numerical problems. Multiplying many small probabilities together quickly underflows to zero.

P(sentence)​=P(word1​)⋅P(word2​)⋅…⋅P(wordn​)=0.01⋅0.003⋅0.02⋅…→0.0(underflow after ∼30 terms)​

Log probabilities fix this. Multiplications become additions.

logP(sentence)​=logP(word1​)+logP(word2​)+…+logP(wordn​)=−4.6+−5.8+−3.9+…→finite number (no underflow)​

Rules: - log(a⋅b)=log(a)+log(b) - log probabilities are always ≤0 (since 0<P≤1) - More negative = less likely - Cross-entropy loss is the negative log probability of the correct class

See it drawn
Underflow, measured

Multiply 1,000 probabilities of 0.01, as a language model scoring a 1,000-token text might, and compare with adding their logarithms:

import math

p = 1.0
for _ in range(1000):
    p *= 0.01
print("product of 1000 probabilities:", p)
print("sum of their logs:            ", 1000 * math.log(0.01))
What you should see
product of 1000 probabilities: 0.0
sum of their logs:             -4605.170185988091

The true product is 10⁻²⁰⁰⁰, far below the smallest positive float (about 10⁻³⁰⁸), so the product is stored as exactly 0 and every comparison between texts is lost. The sum of logs, −4605.17, keeps all the information.

Idea 9 · Softmax as a Probability Distribution

Neural networks output raw scores (logits). Softmax converts them into a valid probability distribution.

softmax(z_i) = exp(z_i) / sum(exp(z_j) for all j)

Properties:
  - All outputs are in (0, 1)
  - All outputs sum to 1
  - Preserves relative ordering of inputs
  - exp() amplifies differences between logits

The softmax trick: subtract the max logit before exponentiating to prevent overflow.

z = [100, 101, 102]
exp(102) = overflow

z_shifted = z - max(z) = [-2, -1, 0]
exp(0) = 1  (safe)

Same result, no overflow.

Log-softmax combines softmax and log for numerical stability. PyTorch uses this internally for cross-entropy loss.

Idea 10 · Sampling

Sampling means drawing random values from a distribution. In ML: - Dropout randomly samples which neurons to zero out - Data augmentation samples random transformations - Language models sample the next token from the predicted distribution - Diffusion models sample noise and progressively denoise

Sampling from arbitrary distributions requires techniques like inverse transform sampling, rejection sampling, or the reparameterization trick (used in VAEs).

The same idea as a formula

Rules. 0≤P(A)≤1, P(Ω)=1, P(A∪B)=P(A)+P(B)−P(A∩B). Conditional probability P(A∣B)=P(A∩B)/P(B); A and B are independent when P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B).

Mass and density. A discrete variable has P(X=x)=p(x) with ∑x​p(x)=1. A continuous one has a density with ∫p(x)dx=1 and P(a≤X≤b)=∫ab​p(x)dx; a density can exceed 1, a probability cannot.

Expectation and variance. E[X]=∑x​xp(x) (or ∫xp(x)dx), and

Var(X)=E[(X−μ)2]=E[X2]−μ2,E[aX+b]=aE[X]+b,Var(aX+b)=a2Var(X)

Expectations always add; variances add when the variables are independent.

Averages concentrate. The mean Xˉn​ of n independent draws with variance σ2 has variance σ2/n, so its spread shrinks like σ/n​, and (central limit theorem) its distribution approaches a normal one, whatever the shape of the original.

Softmax and log-probabilities. softmax(z)i​=ezi​/∑j​ezj​ turns any scores into probabilities. Products of many probabilities underflow, so models add logs instead: log∏i​pi​=∑i​logpi​.

Step 1 · Probability basics

import math
import random

def factorial(n):
    result = 1
    for i in range(2, n + 1):
        result *= i
    return result

def combinations(n, k):
    return factorial(n) // (factorial(k) * factorial(n - k))

def conditional_probability(p_a_and_b, p_b):
    return p_a_and_b / p_b

p_king_given_face = conditional_probability(4/52, 12/52)

Step 2 · PMF and PDF from scratch

def bernoulli_pmf(k, p):
    return p if k == 1 else (1 - p)

def categorical_pmf(k, probs):
    return probs[k]

def poisson_pmf(k, lam):
    return (lam ** k) * math.exp(-lam) / factorial(k)

def uniform_pdf(x, a, b):
    if a <= x <= b:
        return 1.0 / (b - a)
    return 0.0

def normal_pdf(x, mu, sigma):
    coeff = 1.0 / (sigma * math.sqrt(2 * math.pi))
    exponent = -0.5 * ((x - mu) / sigma) ** 2
    return coeff * math.exp(exponent)

Step 3 · Expected value and variance

def expected_value(values, probabilities):
    return sum(v * p for v, p in zip(values, probabilities))

def variance(values, probabilities):
    mu = expected_value(values, probabilities)
    return sum(p * (v - mu) ** 2 for v, p in zip(values, probabilities))

die_values = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]
die_probs = [1/6] * 6
mu = expected_value(die_values, die_probs)
var = variance(die_values, die_probs)
print(f"Die: E[X] = {mu:.4f}, Var(X) = {var:.4f}, SD = {var**0.5:.4f}")
What you should see
Die: E[X] = 3.5000, Var(X) = 2.9167, SD = 1.7078

Step 4 · Sampling from distributions

def sample_bernoulli(p, n=1):
    return [1 if random.random() < p else 0 for _ in range(n)]

def sample_categorical(probs, n=1):
    cumulative = []
    total = 0
    for p in probs:
        total += p
        cumulative.append(total)
    samples = []
    for _ in range(n):
        r = random.random()
        for i, c in enumerate(cumulative):
            if r <= c:
                samples.append(i)
                break
    return samples

def sample_normal_box_muller(mu, sigma, n=1):
    samples = []
    for _ in range(n):
        u1 = random.random()
        u2 = random.random()
        z = math.sqrt(-2 * math.log(u1)) * math.cos(2 * math.pi * u2)
        samples.append(mu + sigma * z)
    return samples

Step 5 · Softmax and log probabilities

def softmax(logits):
    max_logit = max(logits)
    shifted = [z - max_logit for z in logits]
    exps = [math.exp(z) for z in shifted]
    total = sum(exps)
    return [e / total for e in exps]

def log_softmax(logits):
    max_logit = max(logits)
    shifted = [z - max_logit for z in logits]
    log_sum_exp = max_logit + math.log(sum(math.exp(z) for z in shifted))
    return [z - log_sum_exp for z in logits]

def cross_entropy_loss(logits, target_index):
    log_probs = log_softmax(logits)
    return -log_probs[target_index]

Step 6 · Central Limit Theorem demonstration

def demonstrate_clt(dist_fn, n_samples, n_averages):
    averages = []
    for _ in range(n_averages):
        samples = [dist_fn() for _ in range(n_samples)]
        averages.append(sum(samples) / len(samples))
    return averages

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Discrete outcomes have probabilities (a PMF); continuous ones have densities (a PDF) whose areas are probabilities.
  • Conditional probability is P(A and B) / P(B); independence means P(A | B) = P(A).
  • Expected value is the probability-weighted average; variance is the expected squared distance from it.
  • Averages of many independent samples become normal, and their spread shrinks like 1/√n.
  • Work in log-probabilities, and compute softmax and log-softmax with the max subtracted.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Reading a joint table

P(rain | umbrella) = P(rain and umbrella) / P(umbrella) = 0.45 / 0.55 ≈ 0.818. The other way round, P(umbrella | rain) = 0.45 / 0.50 = 0.9. Conditioning divides by a different marginal, so the two answers differ. Mixing them up is the most common probability mistake, and the next lesson, Bayes' theorem, is the rule for turning one into the other.

SolutionB1 · log of zero

softmax([0, -800]) returns [1.0, 0.0]: e⁻⁸⁰⁰ underflows to exactly 0. Then math.log(0.0) raises ValueError: math domain error (NumPy would warn and return -inf, which turns a loss into inf). log_softmax never forms the tiny probability: it computes z − log(Σ e^z) with the maximum subtracted inside, and returns [0.0, -800.0] exactly. This is why frameworks provide log_softmax and cross-entropy that take logits directly.

Interview questions

Try answering out loud first, then open one answer at a time.

Questions from real AI engineering interviews that this lesson prepares you for. Answer out loud first, then open the worked answer.

Explain the mechanismA language model scores a sentence as the product of the probability of each word. For long sentences the score is exactly zero, and two long sentences can no longer be compared. What went wrong, and what do you compute instead?

Level 1 of 4 · about 5 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. The product of many small probabilities underflows to zero in floating point. Add the log probabilities instead, and the sum stays a finite negative number. A more negative sum means a less likely sentence, and the order of the sentences is unchanged.

Why the product dies.

Each word has a probability below 1, often far below. The lesson shows the product falling to 0.0 after about 30 terms. Once the product is zero, every longer sentence gets the same score, and a comparison between them is meaningless.

What the log does.

The log turns a product into a sum: log(a times b) equals log(a) plus log(b). Each term is at most 0, because each probability is at most 1, so the sum is a negative number that grows in size with the sentence length but never underflows. The lesson gives the extreme case: 50 words with probability 0.01 each have a raw probability of 10 to the minus 100, and a log probability of about minus 230.26.

Where you already use it.

Cross-entropy loss is the negative log probability of the correct class. Training a classifier or a language model already works in log space. Log-softmax computes the log probabilities straight from the logits, so the tiny probabilities are never formed.

Comparing sentences.

A longer sentence has a more negative sum even when every word is likely. To compare a short sentence with a long one, divide by the token count, or report the perplexity, which is the exponential of the mean negative log probability.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Use a wider float type and the product will be fine." A wider format only moves the point of underflow. Fifty words with probability 0.01 give 10 to the minus 100, and a longer sentence pushes it further down.
  • "Multiply the probabilities and take the log at the end." The product is already zero before the log, and the log of zero is minus infinity.
  • "Clip each probability to a minimum value." That changes the score of every rare word and still underflows for a long enough sentence.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 1) Why is every log probability zero or negative? Expected: a probability is between 0 and 1, and the log of such a number is at most 0.
  • (level 2) How do you compare two sentences of different length? Expected: compare the mean log probability per token, or the perplexity, not the raw sum.
  • (level 2) Where does this appear in a loss function? Expected: cross-entropy is the negative log probability of the correct class, so training already runs in log space.
  • (level 3) How do you get log probabilities from logits without forming the probabilities first? Expected: log-softmax, which subtracts the log-sum-exp of the logits from each logit.
