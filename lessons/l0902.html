Why this came about

The first RAG systems indexed text. Real documents are not only text: annual reports carry their numbers in charts, manuals explain with diagrams, slide decks are mostly layout, and scanned forms are pictures of text. Extracting the text from such pages throws away much of the meaning. Two developments addressed this. CLIP (2021) trained an image encoder and a text encoder together, so that a sentence and a matching picture get similar vectors, which made it possible to search images with words. And vision-language models became good enough to read a chart or a table from a picture. In 2024 the ColPali work combined the two ideas into retrieval that works directly on images of pages, with no parsing step at all.

In plain words first
Searching pictures and pages, not only words

When the answer is in a chart, a diagram or a scanned page, a text search has nothing to find. There are two ways round this. Either have a model that can see write down what each picture shows, and search those descriptions as ordinary text. Or use a model that turns pictures and sentences into the same kind of coordinates, so that a question lands near the right picture. In both cases, once the right picture is found, a model that can see looks at it and answers.

Step 1 · Five "pages" that are pictures

Draw five small figures of the kind found in business documents: a bar chart, a line chart, a pie chart, a table and a flow diagram. They are saved as image files, as pages of a PDF would be after rendering.

Step 2 · What text extraction recovers

A text extractor (Lesson 4.29) reads the characters on a page. For a chart, those are the title, the axis labels and the tick marks:

EXTRACTED_TEXT = {   # what a text extractor recovers from each page: titles and labels, but not what the picture shows

Every word of the chart is there, and the information is gone. "July" appears and "300" appears, but nothing says that the July bar is the one that reaches 310. A text-only pipeline would retrieve this page for a question about refunds and then be unable to answer it, or would answer wrongly from the axis numbers.

Step 3 · Route B: one space for images and text

A multimodal embedding model has two encoders, one for images and one for text, trained so that a picture and a sentence describing it get nearby vectors (Lesson 10.40). Embed the five images, then embed questions as text and look for the nearest image:

from PIL import Image
from sentence_transformers import SentenceTransformer
import numpy as np

clip = SentenceTransformer("sentence-transformers/clip-ViT-B-32")       # one model, two encoders: images and text land in the same space
images = [Image.open(f"mm_pages/{p}.png").convert("RGB") for p in PAGES]
image_vecs = clip.encode(images, normalize_embeddings=True)
print("image vectors:", image_vecs.shape)


QUESTIONS = ["which month had the most refund requests", "what share of complaints are about late delivery",
             "what are the steps from order to delivery", "how long does delivery take outside metro cities"]
for q in QUESTIONS:
    row = clip.encode([q], normalize_embeddings=True)[0] @ image_vecs.T
    print(f"{q:<50} -> {PAGES[int(np.argmax(row))]:<16} (score {row.max():.3f}; runner-up {sorted(row)[-2]:.3f})")
What you should see
image vectors: (5, 512)
which month had the most refund requests           -> refunds_bar      (score 0.377; runner-up 0.274)
what share of complaints are about late delivery   -> complaints_pie   (score 0.373; runner-up 0.336)
what are the steps from order to delivery          -> order_flow       (score 0.352; runner-up 0.298)
how long does delivery take outside metro cities   -> delivery_line    (score 0.314; runner-up 0.258)

Each image became 512 numbers. The first three questions find the right page, with a clear margin over the runner-up. No description of any image was ever written: the question's vector simply landed nearest the right picture, helped by the fact that CLIP can partly read the large title text in an image.

The fourth question fails. The answer ("4 to 7 days") is in a cell of the shipping table, but the model matched the question to the line chart titled "Average delivery days". One vector per image captures what the picture is broadly about. It does not capture a number in a table cell.

Reading this output
What the scores tell you

The similarities are all between about 0.25 and 0.40. Image-to-text similarities from CLIP-style models sit in a narrow band and are not comparable with text-to-text similarities, so thresholds must be set separately, and mixing image and text results needs rank fusion (Lesson 5.38). This model is also small and from 2021, and reads 224 × 224 pixels: fine print is invisible to it. Current multimodal embedding models (the SigLIP family, and hosted models from Cohere, Voyage, Google and Amazon) are much stronger and accept larger images.

Step 4 · Route A: describe, then index

The alternative is to turn each picture into text once, at indexing time, by asking a vision-language model to describe it for search, with every number and label it can read. Then index the descriptions with the ordinary text pipeline of the Retrieval (RAG) section:

CAPTIONS = {   # stand-in for a vision model asked: "Describe this page for search. Include every number and label you can read."
    "refunds_bar": "Bar chart titled 'Refund requests per month'. Values: June 120, July 310 (the highest), August 280, September 150, October 130. Refunds peaked in July and August.",
    "delivery_line": "Line chart titled 'Average delivery days'. June 2.9, July 4.8 (the slowest), August 4.5, September 3.2, October 2.8 days.",
    "complaints_pie": "Pie chart titled 'Complaints by type': late delivery 41%, damaged 22%, wrong item 18%, other 19%.",
    "shipping_table": "Table 'Shipping options'. Metro: 2-3 days, free over INR 499. Other pin codes: 4-7 days, free over INR 499. Express: 1 day, INR 99.",
    "order_flow": "Flow diagram 'Order flow': Order, then Packed, then Shipped, then Delivered.",
}
text_model = SentenceTransformer("sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2")       # the text embedding model from Lesson 4.5
caption_vecs = text_model.encode([CAPTIONS[p] for p in PAGES], normalize_embeddings=True)
for q in QUESTIONS:
    row = text_model.encode([q], normalize_embeddings=True)[0] @ caption_vecs.T
    best = PAGES[int(np.argmax(row))]
    print(f"{q}\n   -> {best}: {CAPTIONS[best][:92]}...")

All four questions find the right page, including the table question, because the description contains "Other pin codes: 4-7 days". The first answer is even visible in the retrieved text: "July 310 (the highest)". The detail that the image embedding could not hold is now plain text.

The price of this route is a vision-model call for every image at indexing time, and its quality depends entirely on the descriptions. "A bar chart" is a useless description; the instruction must ask for the title, every label and value, and what the figure shows. A model can also misread a value, and that error is then stored in your index.

Step 5 · Answering: show the model the picture

Retrieval finds the page. To answer, pass the original image (not only its description) to a vision-language model together with the question, exactly as in Lesson 4.30. The model reads the bar heights directly and you can cite the page. This matters most for route B, where there is no text to answer from, and it protects route A against errors in the stored descriptions. Images are expensive context, often a thousand tokens or more each, so retrieve few and send only the best one to three.

Route C: page images with late interaction

Routes A and B have a middle ground that has become the strongest option for visually rich documents. ColPali and its successors (ColQwen and others) use a vision-language model to embed a page image as many vectors, one per patch of the page, and embed the question as one vector per token. A page's score is the sum, over the question's tokens, of each token's best match among the page's patches (late interaction, the idea of ColBERT from Lesson 5.38). A question about "delivery outside metro cities" can then match the exact region of the page where that table row is. There is no OCR, no layout parsing and no captioning. The costs are storage (hundreds to a thousand vectors per page) and a specialised index; common practice is to retrieve candidates with a cheaper method first and use the page-image model to rerank.

Choosing a route

Your documents Start with Why
Mostly text with occasional figures Parse to text (Lesson 4.29); describe figures and tables and index the descriptions Reuses your text pipeline; keeps exact values
Photos, product images, screenshots where the look matters A multimodal embedding model No descriptions needed; matches visual content
Slide decks, reports full of charts, scanned forms, complex layouts Page-image retrieval (ColPali family), usually as a reranker Layout and figures are the content; parsing loses them
Tables with numbers people will ask about Extract tables to Markdown or rows (Lesson 4.29); never rely on an image embedding One vector cannot hold a table's cells
A mixture Index text chunks and image descriptions together; add page images for the visual documents; fuse by rank Each route covers the others' gaps

Practical points that apply to every route:

  • Keep the link to the source. Store the document, page number and image file with every indexed item, so that the original page can be shown to the model and to the user.
  • Evaluate separately. Build test questions whose answers are in figures and tables, and measure retrieval on those apart from text questions (Lesson 14.72). Averages over a mostly-text test set hide multimodal failures.
  • Permissions apply to pictures too. A chart from a restricted report is as sensitive as its text (Lesson 14.73).
  • Video and audio follow the same pattern: transcribe speech, sample and describe frames, and index the text with timestamps.
Check your mental model
  • ✗ "OCR text is enough for every document."
    ✓ Charts and layouts lose their meaning as plain text: the words survive, the relationships do not.
  • ✗ "A multimodal embedding understands everything in an image."
    ✓ It captures the broad content in one vector. Small details such as a table cell need a description, a patch-level model, or the vision model looking at the page.
  • ✗ "Once an image is described, the image is no longer needed."
    ✓ Give the original to the answering model when you can; descriptions contain errors and omissions.
  • ✗ "Images are cheap context."
    ✓ They cost many tokens; retrieve them selectively.

Predict before you run

With the CLIP model from Step 3, which page will the query "INR 99" retrieve, and will it be right? Think about what a 224-pixel view of the table page looks like to the model. (Solution P1.)

Break it on purpose

Debugging exercise

Image captions generated for indexing say "a bar chart" and nothing else, and retrieval of figures is poor. Fix the captioning. (Solution D1.)

Make it your own

Combine the two routes: for each question, get the ranking of pages from CLIP and from the caption search, and fuse them with the rrf function of Lesson 5.38. Does the fused ranking answer all four questions? Which route would you weight more for tables? (Solution M1.)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Fine print

Unreliable. "INR 99" is small text inside a table cell, and after the page is shrunk to 224 × 224 pixels it is a few blurred pixels. The model has nothing to match except the general look of the pages, so the result is close to a guess. The caption route finds it, because "Express: 1 day, INR 99" is in the description; a keyword index over the captions finds it exactly (Lesson 5.37).

SolutionB1 · Two different spaces

Retrieval becomes random. CLIP's image vectors and MiniLM's text vectors come from unrelated models, and cutting one to the other's length does not make their directions mean the same thing (Lesson 14.71). Image and text can be compared only when both encoders were trained together, as CLIP's two halves were.

SolutionD1 · Captions that carry the content

Change the instruction to ask for what a searcher would need: the title, the type of figure, every axis label, legend entry and data value that can be read, the main trend or comparison, and any text in the image. Ask for Markdown tables for tables. Review a sample of captions against the images, and keep the page image available so that the answering model can check.

SolutionM1 · Fusing the routes

Build each ranking with np.argsort(-row), turn it into a list of page names, and call rrf([clip_ranking, caption_ranking]). The three questions both routes get right stay right. The table question depends on how the two lists order the runners-up; with equal weights the caption route's first place and CLIP's second or third usually put the table first or second. For tables and anything numeric, trust the text route more, by giving it a higher weight or by using it alone.

Explain the mechanismWhat are the main ways to do retrieval over documents with images?

Worked answer. Describe each image or page with a vision model and index the text; embed images directly with a multimodal embedding model that shares a space with text; or embed page images as many patch vectors and match with late interaction (ColPali). The first keeps exact detail and reuses the text pipeline, the second is cheap and good for visual similarity, the third is strongest for visually rich pages and heaviest to store. The retrieved image is then given to a vision-language model to answer from.

A plausible wrong answer. "Run OCR and index the text." That loses charts, layout and anything that is not characters.

Follow-ups. How would you evaluate retrieval for questions answered by figures?

Debug a failureMultimodal RAG answers questions about text well but gets chart questions wrong. What do you check?

Worked answer. Whether chart pages are retrieved at all (measure retrieval on chart questions separately); whether the index holds only extracted text for those pages; whether descriptions include the data values; and whether the answering model receives the image or only the description. Fix the first stage that fails.

A plausible wrong answer. "Use a larger language model." If the chart never reaches it, size does not help.

Follow-ups. When would you add page-image retrieval?

Defend a designCaption-and-index or ColPali for a library of 200,000 slide pages?

Worked answer. Captioning costs a vision-model call per page once and then uses a standard text index; it keeps values searchable by keyword and is easy to operate. Page-image retrieval needs no captions and handles layout better, but stores hundreds of vectors per page and needs a late-interaction index. A common design: caption and index everything, and use a page-image model to rerank the top candidates. Decide by measuring both on real questions, and by storage and latency budgets.

A plausible wrong answer. "ColPali, because it is the newest." Operations and cost matter as much as benchmark scores.

Recap
  • Text extraction keeps the words of a chart or diagram and loses what it shows.
  • A multimodal embedding model places images and text in one space, so questions can retrieve pictures with no captions. It matches broad content, not fine detail.
  • Describing each image with a vision model and indexing the text keeps the detail and reuses the text pipeline, at the cost of a model call per image.
  • Page-image retrieval with late interaction (ColPali) matches regions of a page and is strongest for visually rich documents.
  • Whatever finds the page, let a vision-language model read the original image to answer, and evaluate figure and table questions separately.
