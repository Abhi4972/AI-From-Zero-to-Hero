In plain words first
The idea in one line

ASR inverts speech to text; TTS inverts text to speech. The 2026 stack is three parts: text → tokens, tokens → mel, mel → waveform. Each part has a default model that fits in a laptop.

The problem

In plain words first

Text-to-speech runs speech recognition backwards: from a string to a waveform. Classic systems do it in three stages that are still the best way to debug any modern one. A front end cleans the text ("Dr." becomes "Doctor", "6 pm" becomes "six p m") and often converts it to phonemes. An acoustic model predicts a mel spectrogram, the same picture of sound as Lesson 6.2, including how long each sound lasts. A vocoder turns the mel spectrogram back into samples, inventing the phase information the mel throws away. Newer models merge the stages, but the same jobs still have to be done somewhere. This lesson runs two real open models on a CPU: SpeechT5 with a HiFi-GAN vocoder (the three stages made visible) and an end-to-end VITS model. It measures their output the way papers do, by transcribing it with Whisper and comparing speaker embeddings, and shows what happens when the front end is skipped. Nothing is trained.

You have a string: "Please remind me to water the plants at 6 pm." You need a 3-second audio clip that sounds natural, has correct prosody (pauses, stress), pronounces "plants" with the right vowel, and runs in under 300 ms on a CPU for a live voice assistant. You also need to swap voices, handle code-switched input ("remind me at 6 pm, daijoubu?"), and not embarrass yourself on names.

Modern TTS pipelines look like this:

  1. Text frontend. Normalize text (dates, numbers, emails), convert to phonemes or subword tokens, predict prosody features.
  2. Acoustic model. Text → mel spectrogram. Tacotron 2 (2017), FastSpeech 2 (2020), VITS (2021), F5-TTS (2024), Kokoro (2024).
  3. Vocoder. Mel → waveform. WaveNet (2016), WaveRNN, HiFi-GAN (2020), BigVGAN (2022), neural codec vocoders in 2024+.

In 2026 the acoustic + vocoder split blurs with end-to-end diffusion and flow-matching models. But the mental model of three parts still holds for debugging.

The idea

Tacotron 2 (2017). Seq2seq: char-embedding → BiLSTM encoder → location-sensitive attention → autoregressive LSTM decoder emits mel frames. Slow (AR), wobbly on long text. Still cited as a baseline.

FastSpeech 2 (2020). Non-autoregressive. Duration predictor outputs how many mel frames each phoneme gets. 1-pass, 10× faster than Tacotron. Loses some naturalness (monotonic alignment) but ships everywhere.

VITS (2021). Jointly trains encoder + flow-based duration + HiFi-GAN vocoder end-to-end with variational inference. High quality, single model. Dominant open-source TTS 2022–2024. Related open systems: YourTTS (VITS-based, multi-speaker, zero-shot) and Coqui's XTTS v2 (late 2023, a GPT-style model), long a popular open voice-cloning choice.

F5-TTS (2024). Diffusion transformer over flow matching. Natural prosody, zero-shot voice cloning with 5 seconds of reference audio. One of the most widely used open voice-cloning models; about 335M params.

Kokoro (2024). Small (82M), CPU-runnable, best-in-class English TTS for real-time use. Apache-2.0 weights, built on StyleTTS 2 with a fast ISTFT-based decoder; English first, with more languages since its 1.0 release (2025).

Commercial APIs. ElevenLabs (its v3 model adds inline audio tags such as [whispers] and [laughs]), OpenAI's gpt-4o-mini-tts (speaking style set by a text instruction) and Google Cloud's Chirp 3 HD voices are widely used in products and audiobook production.

The same idea as a formula

Autoregressive acoustic model (Tacotron 2): P(Y∣x)=∏t​P(yt​∣y<t​,x) over mel frames yt​∈R80, with attention deciding which input symbols each frame is about, and a stop token deciding when to end.

Non-autoregressive with durations (FastSpeech 2): predict an integer duration di​ for every phoneme i, repeat its hidden vector di​ times (the length regulator), and predict all T=∑i​di​ frames at once. Durations, pitch and energy are separate predicted targets, so they can be edited.

VITS trains end to end as a variational autoencoder: a posterior encoder maps real audio to a latent z, a normalising flow maps z to a prior conditioned on the text, monotonic alignment search finds the best text-to-frame alignment during training, and a HiFi-GAN-style decoder turns z into a waveform, trained with reconstruction, KL and adversarial losses.

Flow matching (F5-TTS): draw noise x0​ and data x1​, set xt​=(1−t)x0​+tx1​, and train a network to predict the velocity:

L=Et,x0​,x1​​​vθ​(xt​,t,text,reference)−(x1​−x0​)​2

At inference, integrate dx/dt=vθ​ from noise to a mel spectrogram in a few dozen steps.

Vocoder. A mel frame summarises one hop of H samples, so a neural vocoder upsamples by H: HiFi-GAN's 8×8×2×2=256. Without a neural vocoder, Griffin-Lim estimates the missing phase by alternating X^←STFT(ISTFT(∣X∣ei∠X^)).

Evaluation. Mean opinion score from listeners (1–5); intelligibility as character error rate of an ASR transcript, CER=(S+D+I)/Nchars​; speaker similarity (SECS) as the cosine between speaker embeddings of the reference and the output.

Worked example
Frames, samples and speed by hand

Frames. SpeechT5 works at 16 kHz with a hop of 256 samples, 16 ms per mel frame. Its 170 frames for the sentence under Step 5 are 170×16=2,720 ms of audio.

Samples. The vocoder multiplies by the hop: 170×256=43,520 samples, exactly the length the run reports. If the vocoder had been trained with a different hop or sample rate, it would play back too fast or too slow.

Durations. A FastSpeech-style model predicts durations of, say, 5, 7, 4 and 9 frames for four phonemes: 5+7+4+9=25 frames, 400 ms at a 16 ms hop. Multiply every duration by 1.2 and the same words take 480 ms, slower speech with unchanged pitch.

Real-time factor. The run takes 2.97 s (acoustic model) + 0.47 s (vocoder) for 2.72 s of audio: RTF=3.44/2.72≈1.3, slower than real time on this CPU. Streaming systems need RTF well below 1, which is why small models such as Kokoro matter.

Check your mental model
  • ✗ "The mel spectrogram contains everything needed to play the sound."
    ✓ It drops phase and fine frequency detail; the vocoder has to invent them, which is the hard part of making audio sound natural.
  • ✗ "A TTS model reads numbers and abbreviations correctly."
    ✓ Many models see only letters; text normalisation must come first.
  • ✗ "If Whisper understands it, it sounds good."
    ✓ ASR intelligibility and naturalness are different; robotic audio can have a low CER.

Idea 1 · Vocoder evolution

Year Vocoder How it works Speed
2016 WaveNet Autoregressive: one sample at a time, conditioned on all previous ones Far slower than real time at first
2018 WaveRNN A single small RNN, still sample by sample Real time on CPUs with sparsity tricks
2020 HiFi-GAN Convolutional upsampler trained with discriminators 167.9× real time on one V100 GPU (paper)
2022 BigVGAN HiFi-GAN-style, trained to generalise across speakers, languages and recording conditions Similar to HiFi-GAN
2022–2024 Codec decoders (EnCodec, DAC, SNAC) Decode discrete audio tokens produced by a language model Part of token-based TTS and speech LLMs

By 2026 most "TTS" models are end-to-end from text to waveform; the mel spectrogram is an internal representation.

Idea 2 · Evaluation

  • MOS (Mean Opinion Score). 1–5 scale, crowd-sourced. Still the gold standard; painfully slow.
  • CMOS (Comparative MOS). A-vs-B preference. Tighter confidence intervals per annotation.
  • UTMOS, DNSMOS. Reference-free neural MOS predictors. Used for leaderboards.
  • CER (Character Error Rate) via ASR. Run TTS output through Whisper, compute CER against the input text. Proxy for intelligibility.
  • SECS (Speaker Embedding Cosine Similarity). Voice-cloning quality.

Published numbers depend on the test set, the ASR model and the normaliser, so compare systems only within one paper or one leaderboard (for example TTS Arena, which ranks models by blind listener votes). The runs under Step 5 measure CER and SECS directly.

    def __init__(self, mel_channels=80, upsample_rates=[8, 8, 2, 2]):

        # 4 upsample blocks, total 256x to go from mel-rate to audio-rate
        ...
    def forward(self, mel):
        return self.blocks(mel)  # -> waveform

Training: adversarial (discriminator on short windows) + mel-spectrogram reconstruction loss + feature-matching loss. Commoditized — use pretrained checkpoints from hifi-gan repo or nvidia-NeMo.

The three stages, made visible

SpeechT5 (Microsoft's acoustic model, conditioned on a speaker x-vector) predicts a log-mel spectrogram; a HiFi-GAN vocoder turns it into a 16 kHz waveform. The same mel is also inverted with Griffin-Lim. The voice is a CMU ARCTIC speaker's x-vector, the embeddings from Lesson 6.6.

import time
import numpy as np, torch, librosa
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from datasets import load_dataset
from transformers import SpeechT5Processor, SpeechT5ForTextToSpeech, SpeechT5HifiGan

# A classic two-stage system: SpeechT5 (text -> 80-bin log-mel) + a HiFi-GAN vocoder (mel -> 16 kHz waveform).
tproc = SpeechT5Processor.from_pretrained("microsoft/speecht5_tts")
acoustic = SpeechT5ForTextToSpeech.from_pretrained("microsoft/speecht5_tts").eval()
vocoder = SpeechT5HifiGan.from_pretrained("microsoft/speecht5_hifigan").eval()
xv = load_dataset("Matthijs/cmu-arctic-xvectors", revision="refs/convert/parquet", split="validation")
names = [f.split("_arctic")[0].replace("cmu_us_", "") for f in xv["filename"]]
voice = {s: torch.tensor(xv[names.index(s)]["xvector"]).unsqueeze(0) for s in ["slt", "bdl"]}   # a female and a male speaker

text = "Please remind me to water the plants at six pm."
ids = tproc(text=text, return_tensors="pt")["input_ids"]
torch.manual_seed(0)
t0 = time.time()
with torch.no_grad():
    mel = acoustic.generate_speech(ids, voice["slt"])                  # (frames, 80): the acoustic model's output
    t1 = time.time()
    wav = vocoder(mel).numpy()                                          # the vocoder's output
t2 = time.time()
print(f"{len(ids[0])} input tokens -> mel {tuple(mel.shape)} ({mel.shape[0] * 256 / 16000:.2f} s at 256-sample hops) "
      f"-> waveform {wav.shape[0]} samples at 16 kHz")
print(f"acoustic model {t1 - t0:.2f} s, vocoder {t2 - t1:.2f} s on this CPU for {len(wav) / 16000:.2f} s of audio")

# The same mel turned into audio without a neural vocoder: Griffin-Lim phase reconstruction.
mel_amp = 10 ** mel.numpy().T                                          # SpeechT5's mels are log10 amplitudes
gl = librosa.feature.inverse.mel_to_audio(mel_amp, sr=16000, n_fft=1024, hop_length=256, win_length=1024,
                                          fmin=80, fmax=7600, power=1.0, n_iter=32)
fig, ax = plt.subplots(3, 1, figsize=(9, 6.5))
ax[0].imshow(mel.numpy().T, origin="lower", aspect="auto", cmap="magma"); ax[0].set_title("acoustic model output: log-mel, 80 bins"); ax[0].set_ylabel("mel bin")
for a, w, title in [(ax[1], wav, "HiFi-GAN vocoder"), (ax[2], gl, "Griffin-Lim (no neural vocoder, 32 iterations)")]:
    S = librosa.amplitude_to_db(np.abs(librosa.stft(w, n_fft=512, hop_length=128)), ref=np.max)
    a.imshow(S, origin="lower", aspect="auto", cmap="magma", vmin=-80); a.set_title(f"spectrogram of the {title} waveform"); a.set_ylabel("bin")
ax[2].set_xlabel("frame"); plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

Measuring TTS the way papers do

Four sentences through SpeechT5: intelligibility as CER of Whisper's transcript, HiFi-GAN against Griffin-Lim; then speaker similarity (SECS) with a separate speaker-verification model, WavLM base+ SV, for two voices.

from transformers import WhisperProcessor, WhisperForConditionalGeneration, AutoFeatureExtractor, WavLMForXVector

wproc = WhisperProcessor.from_pretrained("openai/whisper-base.en")
asr = WhisperForConditionalGeneration.from_pretrained("openai/whisper-base.en").eval()
def transcribe(w, sr=16000):
    if sr != 16000:
        w = librosa.resample(w, orig_sr=sr, target_sr=16000)
    with torch.no_grad():
        out = asr.generate(wproc(w, sampling_rate=16000, return_tensors="pt").input_features)
    return wproc.batch_decode(out, skip_special_tokens=True)[0].strip()
def cer(ref, hyp):
    r, h = wproc.tokenizer.normalize(ref), wproc.tokenizer.normalize(hyp)
    d = list(range(len(h) + 1))
    for i, a in enumerate(r, 1):
        prev, d[0] = d[0], i
        for j, b in enumerate(h, 1):
            prev, d[j] = d[j], min(d[j] + 1, d[j - 1] + 1, prev + (a != b))
    return d[-1] / max(1, len(r))

sents = ["Please remind me to water the plants at six pm.",
         "The quick brown fox jumps over the lazy dog.",
         "She sells sea shells by the sea shore every summer.",
         "Our meeting moved to Thursday afternoon at the north office."]
def speak(s, spk="slt"):
    with torch.no_grad():
        return vocoder(acoustic.generate_speech(tproc(text=s, return_tensors="pt")["input_ids"], voice[spk])).numpy()
outs = {s: speak(s) for s in sents}
c_hifi = np.mean([cer(s, transcribe(w)) for s, w in outs.items()])
c_gl = []
for s, w in outs.items():
    with torch.no_grad():
        m = acoustic.generate_speech(tproc(text=s, return_tensors="pt")["input_ids"], voice["slt"])
    g = librosa.feature.inverse.mel_to_audio(10 ** m.numpy().T, sr=16000, n_fft=1024, hop_length=256, fmin=80, fmax=7600, power=1.0, n_iter=32)
    c_gl.append(cer(s, transcribe(g)))
print(f"intelligibility (CER of Whisper's transcript against the input text): HiFi-GAN {c_hifi:.1%}, Griffin-Lim {np.mean(c_gl):.1%}")
print("example:", repr(sents[0]), "->", repr(transcribe(outs[sents[0]])))

# Speaker similarity (SECS) with a separate speaker-verification model, WavLM base+ SV.
sfe = AutoFeatureExtractor.from_pretrained("microsoft/wavlm-base-plus-sv")
sv = WavLMForXVector.from_pretrained("microsoft/wavlm-base-plus-sv").eval()
def emb(w):
    with torch.no_grad():
        e = sv(**sfe(w, sampling_rate=16000, return_tensors="pt")).embeddings[0]
    return torch.nn.functional.normalize(e, dim=-1)
e_slt = [emb(outs[s]) for s in sents[:2]]; e_bdl = [emb(speak(s, "bdl")) for s in sents[:2]]
print(f"SECS, same voice (slt), different sentences: {float(e_slt[0] @ e_slt[1]):.2f}")
print(f"SECS, same sentence, slt vs bdl voice:       {float(e_slt[0] @ e_bdl[0]):.2f}")

example: 'Please remind me to water the plants at six pm.' -> 'Please remind me to water the plants at 6pm.'
SECS, same voice (slt), different sentences: 0.95

Whisper transcribes the HiFi-GAN audio with 1.0% CER and the Griffin-Lim audio with 2.7%: Griffin-Lim sounds worse but stays intelligible, which is why CER alone is not a quality score. The example shows the comparison working through normalisation: "six pm" is written back as "6pm", which Whisper's normaliser maps to the same thing. In voice-cloning papers, SECS between the reference and the clone is the main similarity number.

An end-to-end model without a text front end

Meta's MMS-TTS for English, a 36M-parameter VITS model that reads characters. Three inputs full of abbreviations and digits, each also written out by hand; Whisper reports what it hears.

from transformers import VitsModel, AutoTokenizer

# An end-to-end model: Meta's MMS-TTS English, a VITS model (text -> waveform in one network, 16 kHz).
vtok = AutoTokenizer.from_pretrained("facebook/mms-tts-eng")
vits = VitsModel.from_pretrained("facebook/mms-tts-eng").eval()
chars = sorted(k for k in vtok.get_vocab() if len(k) == 1)
print(f"MMS-TTS: {sum(p.numel() for p in vits.parameters()) / 1e6:.0f}M parameters; it reads characters, not phonemes: {''.join(chars)!r}")
print("what the model is actually given for 'Dr. Smith lives at 221B Baker St.':",
      repr(vtok.decode(vtok("Dr. Smith lives at 221B Baker St.")["input_ids"])))
def vits_say(s, seed=0):
    torch.manual_seed(seed)                                   # VITS samples durations and noise: fix the seed
    with torch.no_grad():
        return vits(**vtok(s, return_tensors="pt")).waveform[0].numpy()

for raw, normalised in [("Dr. Smith lives at 221B Baker St.", "Doctor Smith lives at two twenty one B Baker Street."),

    print(f"\ninput:      {raw!r}\n  heard as: {transcribe(vits_say(raw))!r}")
    print(f"normalised: {normalised!r}\n  heard as: {transcribe(vits_say(normalised))!r}")

Reading this output

The tokenizer lower-cases the text and drops punctuation, so the model is given "dr smith lives at 221b baker st" and has never learned to read "dr", "st" or "221b": Whisper hears "Do Smith limbs it with the baker's st?". "3/4 at 10:30" becomes "for regard", and the phone number becomes "The Devil by Hennes". Written out ("Doctor", "two twenty one B", "March fourth at ten thirty"), the same model is understood, and Whisper even writes the numbers back as digits. The vocabulary explains part of it: it contains the digits 0 to 6 but not 7, 8 or 9. Text normalisation is not optional.

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Open models in production use. Kokoro-82M for fast English on CPUs, F5-TTS and its successors for zero-shot cloning, Coqui XTTS v2 (still widely deployed, though Coqui closed in early 2024), and Meta's MMS-TTS for over a thousand languages through transformers.
  • Human preference leaderboards. TTS Arena on Hugging Face ranks open and commercial models by blind pairwise listener votes, the CMOS idea at scale.
  • Speech-native LLM voices. Real-time voice assistants increasingly generate speech directly from an audio-capable LLM (Lessons 6.10 and 6.15) instead of a separate TTS step.

Sources: Kokoro-82M, F5-TTS, MMS-TTS English, SpeechT5 TTS, HiFi-GAN paper.

Pitfalls

  • No text normalizer. "Dr. Smith" reads as "Doctor" or "Drive"? "2026" as "twenty twenty six" or "two zero two six"? Normalize BEFORE phonemizer.
  • OOV proper nouns. "Ghumare" → "ghyu-mair"? Ship a fallback grapheme-to-phoneme model for unknown tokens.
  • Clipping. Vocoder output rarely clips, but mel scaling mismatch at inference can overshoot ±1.0. Always np.clip(wav, -1, 1).
  • Sample-rate mismatch. Kokoro outputs 24 kHz; your downstream pipeline expects 16 kHz → resample or get aliasing.
Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Three jobs: text front end (normalise, phonemise), acoustic model (text → mel with durations), vocoder (mel → waveform). End-to-end models still do all three.
  • Frame count × hop size = sample count; the vocoder must match the acoustic model's hop and sample rate.
  • Neural vocoders (HiFi-GAN) beat Griffin-Lim on naturalness far more than on intelligibility.
  • Measure with ASR CER (intelligibility), SECS (speaker similarity) and human preference (naturalness).
  • Normalise numbers, abbreviations and symbols before synthesis.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Only a little

No: Griffin-Lim's CER is 2.7% against 1.0% for HiFi-GAN. The mel spectrogram carries the words; what Griffin-Lim gets wrong is the phase, which makes the voice sound rough rather than unclear. Naturalness needs listeners or a MOS predictor, not ASR.

SolutionB1 · Fast and high-pitched, and still partly readable

Declaring 16 kHz audio as 24 kHz plays it 1.5 times too fast with every frequency raised by 1.5 (a fifth higher), and the clip is a third shorter. After reading it back at 24 kHz and resampling, the speech is a chipmunk version of itself; Whisper may still recognise much of it, because it is robust, but the voice and timing are wrong for any listener. Always carry the sample rate with the audio.
