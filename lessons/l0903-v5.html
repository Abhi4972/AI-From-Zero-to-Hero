Most of this course is about text agents, because most enterprise agents are text agents. Two other kinds of generative AI show up in real projects often enough to deserve their own module: voice agents, which put an agent on a phone line or in an app's microphone, and generative media, which creates images, audio and video. Both reuse everything else in the course (tools, retrieval, guardrailslater · 15.23, evals), and both add constraints of their own.

Why this came about

Voice assistants existed for years but were rigid. In 2024 realtime speech models made natural, low-latency spoken conversations with language models possible, creating voice agents with their own latency and interruption challenges.

In plain words first
A conversation where a second of silence is too long

Voice agents must answer within about a second, so latency drives the design. The pipeline design chains speech-to-text, a normal text agent and text-to-speech, streaming at every stage; it gives control and any model. Speech-to-speech models hear and speak directly, with fewer parts and lower latency, but fewer choices. Both must handle interruptions, when the user starts talking over the agent.

Voice agents: two architectures

Architecture How it works Strengths Trade-offs
Cascaded pipeline Speech-to-text (STT), then a text LLM with tools, then text-to-speech (TTS), all streaming Use any LLM and your existing text agent; transcripts for logging and evals; swap each part independently More hops, so more latency; tone of voice is lost in the transcript
Speech-to-speech model (realtime) One model hears audio and speaks audio, with tool calling Lowest latency; hears tone, hesitation and interruptions; more natural prosody Fewer model choices; harder to inspect and evaluate; audio tokens cost more

Realtime speech models and APIs include OpenAI's Realtime API, Gemini Live, Amazon Nova Sonic on Bedrock and Azure's Voice Live. Frameworks that handle the media plumbing (streaming audio, voice activity detection, interruptions, telephony) include Pipecat, LiveKit Agents and Vapi-style managed platforms; telephony arrives through providers such as Twilio, Exotel or Amazon Connect. Transport matters: WebRTC handles jitter and packet loss for browsers and mobile apps; WebSockets are simpler for server-to-server audio.

In production
Design a voice agent right from day one
  1. A latency budget per stage, measured on every turn in production, with a target near 800 ms to first audio.
  2. Streaming end to end, barge-in and turn detection before any feature work.
  3. Read-back confirmation before every action, and identity verification before account data.
  4. A human handoff path that is always one sentence away.
  5. Recording and AI disclosure at the start, and PII redaction in stored transcripts.
  6. An eval set of real call audio in your callers' languages, accents and noise levels.

Step 1 · The latency budget

People expect a reply within roughly a second of finishing a sentence; much more and they start talking again or assume the line dropped. Add up a turn stage by stage:

STAGES_MS = {   # illustrative per-stage latencies for one turn, measured on your stack
    "endpointing (detect the caller stopped)": 300,
    "speech-to-text final transcript": 150,
    "LLM time to first token": 350,
    "LLM full answer (60 tokens at 15 ms)": 900,
    "text-to-speech first audio chunk": 120,
    "text-to-speech full answer": 700,
    "network and telephony": 80,
}
s = STAGES_MS
sequential = sum(s.values())                                            # wait for each stage to finish completely
streamed = (s["endpointing (detect the caller stopped)"] + s["speech-to-text final transcript"]
            + s["LLM time to first token"] + 8 * 15                     # first speakable phrase is ~8 tokens
            + s["text-to-speech first audio chunk"] + s["network and telephony"])
print(f"fully sequential pipeline: {sequential} ms until the caller hears anything")
print(f"streamed pipeline:         {streamed} ms (target: under ~800 ms)")
tuned = 150 + s["speech-to-text final transcript"] + 200 + 8 * 15 + s["text-to-speech first audio chunk"] + s["network and telephony"]
print(f"plus a turn-detection model (150 ms) and a faster model (200 ms to first token): {tuned} ms")
speech_to_speech = 150 + 300 + s["network and telephony"]          # one model hears audio and speaks audio
print(f"speech-to-speech model (no separate STT and TTS): about {speech_to_speech} ms")
What you should see
fully sequential pipeline: 2600 ms until the caller hears anything
streamed pipeline:         1120 ms (target: under ~800 ms)
plus a turn-detection model (150 ms) and a faster model (200 ms to first token): 820 ms
speech-to-speech model (no separate STT and TTS): about 530 ms

Three lessons. Streaming everything is not optional: waiting for each stage to finish more than doubles the delay. Endpointing (deciding that the caller has finished) is often the biggest single cost, which is why voice stacks add a small turn-detection model that listens for the end of a thought, not just silence. And the LLM's time to first token matters far more than its total generation time, which favors fast models, short system prompts with prompt caching, and tools that answer in milliseconds. Speech-to-speech models win on raw latency; cascades win on control.

Step 2 · Speak while the model is still thinking

TTS should start on the first natural phrase, not the full answer. A small chunker sits between the LLM stream and the TTS engine:

import re

def speakable_chunks(token_stream):
    """Turn an LLM token stream into phrases a TTS engine can start speaking immediately."""
    buf = ""
    for tok in token_stream:
        buf += tok
        if re.search(r"[.!?,;:]\s*$", buf) and len(buf.split()) >= 3:   # a natural pause, not a single word
            yield buf.strip()
            buf = ""
    if buf.strip():
        yield buf.strip()

answer = "Your order A-1001 has shipped, and it should arrive on Thursday. Anything else I can help with?"
tokens = [w + " " for w in answer.split()]
for i, chunk in enumerate(speakable_chunks(tokens), 1):
    print(f"TTS chunk {i}: {chunk!r}")
What you should see
TTS chunk 1: 'Your order A-1001 has shipped,'
TTS chunk 2: 'and it should arrive on Thursday.'
TTS chunk 3: 'Anything else I can help with?'

Chunking at punctuation keeps intonation natural; chunks of one or two words sound robotic. Write voice prompts differently from chat prompts: short sentences, no markdown, no lists or URLs, numbers and order IDs in a form the TTS reads well ("A, ten oh one"), and a confirmation read back before any action ("I'll cancel order A-1002. Shall I go ahead?").

Step 3 · Turn-taking and interruptions

Voice activity detection (VAD) finds speech in the audio stream. The silence threshold that ends a turn is a trade-off: short thresholds cut people off mid-thought, long ones feel sluggish. Here is energy-based VAD on synthetic audio:

import numpy as np

rate, frame_ms = 16_000, 20
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
t = np.arange(int(rate * 2.0)) / rate
audio = rng.normal(0, 0.005, t.size)                                 # 2 s of background noise
speech = (t > 0.4) & (t < 1.1)                                       # the caller speaks from 0.4 s to 1.1 s
audio[speech] += 0.2 * np.sin(2 * np.pi * 220 * t[speech])

frames = audio[: t.size // (rate * frame_ms // 1000) * (rate * frame_ms // 1000)].reshape(-1, rate * frame_ms // 1000)
energy_db = 10 * np.log10(np.mean(frames ** 2, axis=1) + 1e-12)
voiced = energy_db > energy_db.min() + 15                            # simple energy voice-activity detection

def end_of_turn(voiced: np.ndarray, silence_ms: int) -> float | None:
    need = silence_ms // frame_ms
    started = False
    for i in range(len(voiced)):
        started |= voiced[i]
        if started and i >= need and not voiced[i - need + 1 : i + 1].any():
            return (i + 1) * frame_ms / 1000
    return None

first = np.argmax(voiced) * frame_ms / 1000
print(f"speech detected at {first:.2f} s")
for silence in (200, 500, 900):
    print(f"end of turn with {silence} ms silence rule: {end_of_turn(voiced, silence):.2f} s")
What you should see
speech detected at 0.40 s
end of turn with 200 ms silence rule: 1.30 s
end of turn with 500 ms silence rule: 1.60 s
end of turn with 900 ms silence rule: 2.00 s

The caller stopped at 1.1 seconds; every rule adds its silence on top. Production systems use trained VAD models (Silero VAD, WebRTC VAD) that ignore background noise, plus semantic turn detection that waits longer after "my order number is..." than after a complete question. Barge-in is the other half: when the caller starts speaking while the agent talks, stop TTS playback immediately, cancel the pending LLM generation, and keep only what the caller actually heard in the conversation history, or the agent will "remember" saying things the caller never heard.

Voice agents in production

  • Evaluate on audio, not just text: word error rate on names, order IDs, addresses and numbers in your callers' accents and languages (Indian English, Hindi and code-mixed Hinglish behave very differently from benchmark audio); task completion; latency percentiles per turn; interruption handling. Simulated callers (Lesson 14.143) can be voiced with TTS to test end to end.
  • Languages: check STT and TTS quality per language and dialect before committing; Azure Speech, Google Cloud Speech, Amazon Transcribe and Polly, and Indian providers such as Sarvam AI cover Indian languages to different degrees.
  • Safety and compliance: announce recording and AI use at the start of the call, verify identity before revealing account data, redact PII in transcripts, hand off to a person on request or on emergencies, and guard against voice prompt injectionlater · 15.24 (a caller can say "ignore your instructions" as easily as type it).
  • Cost: audio is billed per minute or per audio token at each stage; long calls with long contexts add up, so trim history and summarize.

Exercises

Predict before you run

The latency budget allots 300 ms each to speech recognition, the model's first token and speech synthesis. If the model needs 900 ms for its first sentence, where can you win time without a faster model? (Solution P1.)

Break it on purpose

Use a fixed 1.5-second silence to decide that the caller finished speaking. What do callers experience? (Solution B1.)

Debugging exercise

The agent keeps talking when the caller interrupts. What is missing? (Solution D1.)

Make it your own

Change speakable_chunks to never split inside numbers or order IDs such as SK-1042, and test it on a reply that contains both. (Solution M1.)

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Overlap the stages

Stream: start synthesizing the first speakable chunk as soon as it is complete (Step 2), and start recognition early with streaming ASR. Fillers or short acknowledgments also hide latency.

SolutionB1 · Turn-taking needs more than silence

Long pauses before every reply, and interruptions when callers pause mid-sentence. Use voice activity detection with adaptive or semantic end-of-turn detection, as end_of_turn begins to do.

SolutionD1 · Barge-in

Detecting speech while the agent speaks, stopping playback, cancelling pending synthesis, and telling the model it was interrupted, with what was actually heard.

SolutionM1 · Speak whole units

Treat numbers, IDs and abbreviations as unbreakable, and split on sentence or clause boundaries. Spoken IDs also need formatting: read SK-1042 as "S K one zero four two".

Check your understanding

Explain the mechanismCompare pipeline and speech-to-speech voice agents.

Worked answer. A pipeline chains speech recognition, a text model and speech synthesis: flexible, debuggable, any model, but latency adds up per stage. Speech-to-speech models process audio directly, with lower latency and better prosody, but less control, fewer model choices and harder evaluation. Both need streaming, turn-taking and interruption handling.

A plausible wrong answer. "Speech-to-speech is always better." It trades control and observability for latency.

Worked answer. For high-volume, simple calls (order status, delivery dates) with a clear handoff to people, yes, if it meets latency and accuracy targets and supports the languages callers use. Refunds and complaints may stay with people. Pilot on a slice of calls with measured outcomes.
