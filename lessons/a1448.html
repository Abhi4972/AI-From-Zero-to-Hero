Start with the Current System

In plain words first

Before designing anything, write down what people actually do today: each step, who does it, what triggers it, where it is slow, who has authority, and what evidence shows it happens. Evidence has strengths. Direct records (logs, recordings) and artifacts (tickets, runbooks) are stronger than what people report, which is stronger than what the team infers. Getting from pilot to value is covered in Lesson 14.165 (linked above). This lesson rebuilds an incident workflow from tool logs, compares it with the interview, and checks a small model's summary of the same logs.

Do not begin by asking what features people want. Begin by reconstructing what happens now.

For each step, record:

Field Example
Actor On-call engineer
Trigger Production alert arrives
Action Opens alert, then searches dashboards
Input Alert payload and deployment record
Output Candidate service and owner
Friction Context switching across three tools
Authority Incident commander approves a write
Evidence Screen recording, incident log, runbook

The workflow is larger than the screen. It includes waiting, copy-paste, side channels, approval, error recovery, and the steps people have stopped noticing.

Evidence Has Strength

Use a simple evidence ladder:

  1. Direct behavior: observation, trace, recording, or system event.
  2. Artifact: ticket, runbook, log, form, or completed output.
  3. Reported behavior: a person describes what they do.
  4. Inference: the team concludes what probably happens.

All four can be useful. Only the first two prove current behavior directly. Label the rest so confidence does not silently inflate.

Search for Four Things

  • Friction: repeated effort, delay, re-entry, or recovery.
  • Hidden state: facts carried in memory, chat, or personal notes.
  • Authority: the person or system allowed to make a consequential change.
  • Exceptions: the case where the normal workflow stops being normal.

AI features often fail at handoffs and exceptions because the happy path was the only path shaped.

Do Not Average Away Disagreement

Two users can perform different workflows for good reasons. Preserve the variants until you understand whether they represent:

  • different roles;
  • different risk levels;
  • legacy and current process;
  • expertise differences;
  • a genuine policy disagreement.

An averaged workflow can describe nobody.

The same idea as a formula

Where the time goes is a sum of gaps. For one incident with events at times t0​<t1​<⋯<tm​, the time attributed to step k is the gap gk​=tk​−tk−1​, and the total is T=tm​−t0​=∑k​gk​. Over many incidents, compare steps by their median gap. A median is not pulled about by one stuck incident the way a mean is.

How much rests on evidence. The lesson's direct-evidence ratio is

ρ=#all evidence items#direct evidence items​.

An interview alone gives ρ=0. Each step backed by a log event raises it.

# Reconstruct a workflow from logs, without interviewing anyone (Exercise 1). Six incidents' tool events (alerting,
# dashboards, chat, deploy records, approvals) are generated with a fixed seed, as a stand-in for real exports.
# The log-derived workflow is set beside what the on-call engineer says they do, both go through the lesson's
# audit(), and the incidents are grouped into variants instead of averaged. No model; instant.
import random, statistics
from collections import Counter, defaultdict
from main import Evidence, WorkflowStep, audit
rng = random.Random(7)

def incident(i):
    t, ev = 0.0, []
    def add(actor, tool, action, dt):
        nonlocal t
        t += dt; ev.append((round(t, 1), actor, tool, action))
    add("alerting", "pager", "alert fired", 0)
    add("on-call", "pager", "opened alert", rng.uniform(0.5, 2))
    for d in ("latency", "errors", "deploys")[: rng.choice([2, 3, 3])]:
        add("on-call", "grafana", f"opened {d} dashboard", rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5))
    add("on-call", "chat", "asked #ops who owns the service", rng.uniform(0.5, 1))
    add("teammate", "chat", "replied with owner", rng.uniform(2, 9))
    if i in (2, 5):   # the deploy record is missing for these services
        add("on-call", "git", "searched git log for recent changes", rng.uniform(3, 8))
    else:
        add("on-call", "deploys", "read deploy record", rng.uniform(0.5, 1.5))
    add("on-call", "chat", "requested approval to roll back", rng.uniform(1, 3))
    add("commander", "chat", "approved roll back", rng.uniform(5, 20))
    add("on-call", "deploys", "rolled back", rng.uniform(1, 2))
    return ev

LOGS = {f"INC-{100 + i}": incident(i) for i in range(6)}
first = LOGS["INC-100"]
print("INC-100, as the tools recorded it (minutes since the alert):")
for t, actor, tool, action in first:
    print(f"   {t:>5}  {actor:<10} {tool:<8} {action}")

INTERVIEW = ["open the alert", "check the service dashboard", "roll back the bad deploy"]
print(f"\nwhat the on-call engineer says they do: {INTERVIEW}")

def steps_from_log(ev, name):
    out, order = [], 0
    for k, (t, actor, tool, action) in enumerate(ev[1:], 1):
        wait = round(t - ev[k - 1][0], 1)
        friction = "waiting" if actor != "on-call" and wait >= 2 else ("context switch" if tool == "grafana" else "")
        order += 1
        out.append(WorkflowStep(order, actor, action, (Evidence(f"{name} {tool} event", f"at {t} min", True, 0.95),),
                                friction + (f" ({wait} min)" if friction == "waiting" else "")))
    return out

reported = [WorkflowStep(i, "on-call", a, (Evidence("interview", "said so", False, 0.6),)) for i, a in enumerate(INTERVIEW, 1)]
observed = steps_from_log(first, "INC-100")
for label, steps in (("interview", reported), ("log for INC-100", observed)):
    a = audit(steps)
    print(f"audit({label}): {len(steps)} steps, status {a['status']}, direct_evidence_ratio {a['direct_evidence_ratio']}, "
          f"friction {a['friction_points'] or 'none'}")
hidden = [s.action for s in observed if not any(w in s.action for w in ("alert", "dashboard", "rolled"))]
print(f"steps the interview never mentions: {hidden}")

print("\nwhere the time goes across 6 incidents (median minutes):")
gaps = defaultdict(list)
for ev in LOGS.values():
    for k in range(1, len(ev)):
        key = "dashboards" if "dashboard" in ev[k][3] else ev[k][3]
        gaps[key].append(ev[k][0] - ev[k - 1][0])
for key, vals in sorted(gaps.items(), key=lambda kv: -statistics.median(kv[1])):
    print(f"   {key:<40} {statistics.median(vals):>5.1f}   (in {len(vals)} events)")
total = [ev[-1][0] for ev in LOGS.values()]
print(f"   alert to roll back: median {statistics.median(total):.1f}, range {min(total):.1f}-{max(total):.1f}")

print("\nvariants, not an average:")
paths = Counter(tuple(a for _, _, _, a in ev if "dashboard" not in a) for ev in LOGS.values())
for path, n in paths.items():
    branch = [a for a in path if "git" in a or "deploy record" in a]
    ids = [k for k, ev in LOGS.items() if tuple(a for _, _, _, a in ev if "dashboard" not in a) == path]
    print(f"   {n} incidents {ids}: {branch[0]}")

Part 1 of 2 · Evidence, WorkflowStep, audit, example

# Lesson program: audits workflow evidence, ordering, confidence, and friction.
# Lesson: phases/14-agent-engineering/48-discover-the-real-workflow/docs/en.md
# Canonical source: Nuseibeh and Easterbrook, Requirements Engineering: A Roadmap.
# Canonical source: Gotel and Finkelstein, ICRE 1994, DOI 10.1109/ICRE.1994.292398.
from __future__ import annotations

import json
from dataclasses import asdict, dataclass
from pathlib import Path


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class Evidence:
    source: str
    observation: str
    direct: bool
    confidence: float


@dataclass(frozen=True)
class WorkflowStep:
    order: int
    actor: str
    action: str
    evidence: tuple[Evidence, ...]
    friction: str = ""


def audit(steps: list[WorkflowStep]) -> dict:
    orders = [step.order for step in steps]
    issues: list[str] = []
    if orders != list(range(1, len(steps) + 1)):
        issues.append("workflow order must be contiguous from one")
    for step in steps:
        if not step.evidence:
            issues.append(f"step {step.order} has no evidence")
        for item in step.evidence:
            if not 0 <= item.confidence <= 1:
                issues.append(f"step {step.order} has confidence outside zero to one")
    direct = sum(item.direct for step in steps for item in step.evidence)
    total = sum(len(step.evidence) for step in steps)
    return {
        "status": "grounded" if not issues and direct > 0 else "needs-evidence",
        "issues": issues,
        "direct_evidence_ratio": round(direct / total, 2) if total else 0,
        "friction_points": [step.friction for step in steps if step.friction],
        "steps": [asdict(step) for step in steps],
    }


def example() -> list[WorkflowStep]:
    return [
        WorkflowStep(1, "on-call engineer", "opens the alert", (Evidence("screen recording 01", "alert lacks service owner", True, 0.95),), "owner lookup"),
        WorkflowStep(2, "on-call engineer", "searches dashboards", (Evidence("incident 184", "three dashboards opened", True, 0.9),), "context switching"),
        WorkflowStep(3, "incident commander", "approves mitigation", (Evidence("runbook", "production writes require approval", False, 0.8),)),
    ]


def main() -> None:
    output = Path(__file__).resolve().parents[1] / "outputs" / "workflow-evidence.json"
    output.write_text(json.dumps(audit(example()), indent=2) + "\n", encoding="utf-8")
    print(output.read_text(encoding="utf-8"))

Solutions

SolutionP1 · The logs add waiting and authority

The logs add six things the interview omitted: asking #ops who owns the service, the reply, reading the deploy record, requesting approval, the commander's approval, and, in 2 of 6 incidents, a git-log search because the deploy record was missing.

The slowest step is the approval wait, a median of 16.3 minutes. The step the interview emphasised, checking the dashboard, takes about a minute per dashboard.

SolutionB1 · Interviews instead of the record

They took the interviews as the workflow. The incident timelines would have shown that most of the time to mitigation is spent waiting for the commander's approval and for someone to name the service owner. An agent that reads dashboards faster cannot touch either wait.

The logs would also have shown the exception variant, where there is no deploy record and the engineer searches git. That is exactly where the agent now proposes impossible rollbacks.

Discovery should have rebuilt the workflow from timelines, measured the median gaps per step, kept the variants separate, and marked the commander as the authority over production writes. Then the outcome, perhaps faster approval with the right evidence attached, comes before choosing an output.

What You Keep

Keep outputs/workflow-evidence.json. It turns observed friction and uncertainty into an assumption map in the next lesson.

Break it on purpose

A team builds an incident agent from three on-call interviews. It reads dashboards and proposes a rollback in seconds. After launch, time to mitigation is unchanged, and on some services the agent proposes rollbacks with no deploy to roll back. Using this lesson, what discovery was skipped? (Solution B1.)

Industry practice
What's current (2024–2026)
  • Event logs first. Incident platforms (PagerDuty, incident.io and others) keep timestamped timelines of alerts, chat and actions. Process-mining tools, including the open-source PM4Py, rebuild workflows and variants from such logs.
  • Observe, then ask. Field studies and contextual inquiry, watching people do the work in place, remain the standard way to find hidden steps that interviews miss.
  • Usage traces for AI products. Teams review real traces of how people use an assistant, and privacy-preserving analysis of usage at scale (Anthropic's Clio, for example) shows actual tasks rather than imagined ones.
  • On your laptop: the reconstruction is standard-library Python. PM4Py is a pip install for larger logs, and the model step runs on CPU.

Sources: PM4Py on PyPI, Nielsen Norman Group, Field studies, Anthropic, Clio, incident.io docs.

Recap
What to keep from this lesson
  • Reconstruct the current workflow before asking for features.
  • Label evidence: direct, artifact, reported, inferred.
  • Measure the gaps; the slow step is often a wait for a person or an approval.
  • Keep variants separate, because the exception path is where AI features fail.
  • Check any summary, human or model, against the record.

OWCheat sheet · Specs, Risks, Workflows and Outcomes

Interview questions

Try answering out loud first, then open one answer at a time.

Questions from real AI engineering interviews that this lesson prepares you for. Answer out loud first, then open the worked answer.

Explain the mechanismA customer lead spends an hour describing how their support team handles a refund request. You now have to decide what the first version of an assistant does. Tell me how you treat that hour of description, and what you do next.

Level 2 of 4 · about 10 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. An interview is reported behavior, which is the third rung of a four-rung ladder. I label every claim from the hour by its rung, then look for direct evidence for the claims that matter most: a trace, a recording, a ticket or a completed form. The steps that stay unproven remain visible as open claims instead of turning into requirements.

The ladder.

  1. Direct behavior: an observation, a trace, a recording or a system event.
  2. An artifact: a ticket, a runbook, a log, a form or a finished output.
  3. Reported behavior: a person describes what they do.
  4. Inference: the team concludes what probably happens.

All four help. Only the first two prove current behavior. When I write the workflow down, each step carries its rung, so confidence does not grow on its own.

What I reconstruct.

I do not begin with feature wishes. I rebuild what happens now, step by step, with the actor, the trigger, the action, the input, the output, the friction, who holds authority for a consequential change, and the evidence. The workflow is larger than the screen. It includes waiting, copy and paste, side channels, approvals, error recovery and steps people have stopped noticing.

Four things I search for.

  • Friction: repeated effort, delay, re-entry or recovery.
  • Hidden state: facts carried in memory, chat or personal notes.
  • Authority: the person or system allowed to make the consequential change.
  • Exceptions: the case where the normal path stops being normal.

Assistants fail most often at handoffs and exceptions, because only the happy path was shaped. So the first thing I ask for after the interview is one real trace of an exception.

How this changes the first version.

A step with direct evidence can become a requirement. A step with only reported evidence becomes an assumption to test. The description from the hour tells me where to look, not what to build.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The customer knows their process, so the interview is the requirement." A description is reported behavior; it is useful but it does not prove what happens.
  • "Average the descriptions from several people into one workflow." An averaged workflow can describe nobody; the variants must stay separate until their cause is known.
  • "Infer the missing steps from similar teams." Inference is the weakest rung and must stay labeled as a guess.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) The lead says "we always check the order history first." How do you confirm that? Expected: ask for a recording or a log of three real cases and mark the claim as reported until one of them shows it.
  • (level 2) What is the direct-evidence ratio and why track it? Expected: the share of workflow steps backed by observation or an artifact; a low ratio means the model of the workflow is mostly belief.
  • (level 3) You can only observe the happy path. What is the risk? Expected: the exception path, where most failures live, stays unshaped, so the assistant breaks at the handoff that matters.
  • (level 3) A proposed feature removes a visible step. What do you check before you accept it? Expected: whether hidden work behind that step still exists, so the feature only moves the effort somewhere invisible.

Where it is taught. The workflow discovery lesson gives the evidence ladder and the step record.

Weigh a trade-offTwo senior analysts on the same team show you how they triage a flagged transaction. Their steps differ in order, in the tools they open, and in who they ask before they act. Your deadline allows one workflow in the first version. What do you do with the disagreement?

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. I keep both variants separate until I know why they differ. The difference can come from different roles, different risk levels, a legacy process next to a current one, a difference in expertise, or a real policy disagreement, and each of those leads to a different product. An averaged workflow can describe nobody, so the first version targets one variant on purpose and names the other as a known gap.

Why not average.

Two people can perform different workflows for good reasons. Merging their steps into one path produces a sequence that neither of them follows. The assistant then fails at the first handoff or exception, which is where these features fail most.

What the difference can mean.

Cause What it tells the build
Different roles Two user types, maybe two outcome frames
Different risk levels The branch is a feature, not noise
Legacy and current process One variant may be retiring; confirm with the owner
Expertise difference The expert path may be the target and the other the training gap
Policy disagreement A human decision is needed before any build

How I resolve it.

  1. Record both variants as ordered steps with evidence and the rung of that evidence.
  2. Find where the branch begins and which authority boundary each variant crosses.
  3. Ask the owner of the process which cause applies; if it is a policy disagreement, that decision goes to a human and the build waits for it.
  4. Pick one variant for the first slice by risk and frequency, and write the other as a non-goal with the reason.

Where the exception hides.

Often one analyst's "extra" step is the exception path the other analyst has stopped noticing. I ask for a real case where the normal path failed before I call a step optional.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Merge the two into one standard process." The averaged workflow may describe nobody and breaks at the handoff.
  • "Follow the more senior person." Seniority is not evidence; the cause of the difference decides.
  • "Treat the extra steps as optional and skip them." The extra step is often the exception path that carries most of the risk.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) One analyst asks a supervisor before acting and the other does not. What is that step? Expected: an authority boundary; the build must respect it, and the two variants may differ in risk level or role.
  • (level 2) You can only ship one path. How do you choose? Expected: by the risk and the frequency of each variant, with the other recorded as a named gap, not by averaging.
  • (level 3) The owner says both are fine. What do you record? Expected: that the policy allows both, with the evidence, so the assistant does not force one on everybody.
  • (level 3) How do you keep the second variant from disappearing from the plan? Expected: write it as a non-goal with its reason and a review point, so it stays visible as a decision instead of an omission.

Where it is taught. The workflow discovery lesson explains why disagreement must not be averaged away and lists the causes behind variants.
