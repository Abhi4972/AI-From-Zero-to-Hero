In plain words first
The idea in one line

Goodhart's Law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Every frontier lab games benchmarks. MMLU scores go up while models still can't reliably count the number of R's in "strawberry." The only eval that matters is YOUR eval -- on YOUR task, with YOUR data.

The problem

In plain words first

Evaluation decides which model you ship, so it has to measure what you care about. Public benchmarks (multiple-choice exams, coding puzzles) are cheap and comparable but leak into training data and saturate. Perplexity measures how well a model predicts ordinary text, a quick health check. Pairwise judgements by people or by a strong model, turned into Elo-style ratings, capture open-ended quality. And custom evals built from your own task are the only ones that predict production behaviour. This lesson runs the lesson's mock eval pipeline, then measures real perplexity for three small models with different tokenizers, and scores a real benchmark the way lm-evaluation-harness does, with confidence intervals. No model is trained.

MMLU was published in 2020 with 15,908 questions across 57 subjects. Within three years, frontier models saturated it. GPT-4 scored 86.4%. Claude 3 Opus scored 86.8%. Llama 3 405B scored 88.6%. The leaderboard compressed into a 3-point range where differences are statistical noise, not real capability gaps.

Meanwhile, those same models fail at tasks that a 10-year-old handles without thinking. Claude 3.5 Sonnet, scoring 88.7% on MMLU, initially could not count the letters in "strawberry" -- a task that requires zero world knowledge and zero reasoning, just character-level iteration. HumanEval tests code generation with 164 problems. Models score 90%+ on it while still producing code that crashes on edge cases any junior developer would catch.

The gap between benchmark performance and real-world reliability is the central problem of LLM evaluation. Benchmarks tell you how a model performs on the benchmark. They tell you almost nothing about how that model will perform on your specific task, with your specific data, under your specific failure modes. If you are building a customer support bot, MMLU is irrelevant. If you are building a code assistant, HumanEval only covers function-level generation -- it says nothing about debugging, refactoring, or explaining code across files.

You need custom evals. Not because benchmarks are useless -- they are useful for rough model selection -- but because the final evaluation must match your deployment conditions exactly.

Idea 1 · The Eval Landscape

There are three categories of evaluation, each with different cost and signal quality.

Benchmarks are standardized test suites. MMLU, HumanEval, SWE-bench, MATH, ARC, HellaSwag. You run a model against the benchmark and get a score. The advantage: everyone uses the same test, so you can compare models. The disadvantage: models and training data increasingly contaminate these benchmarks. Labs train on data that includes benchmark questions. Scores go up. Capability may not.

Custom evals are test suites you build for your specific use case. You define the inputs, the expected outputs, and the scoring function. A legal document summarizer gets evaluated on legal documents. A SQL generator gets evaluated on your database schema. These are expensive to create but they are the only evaluation that predicts production performance.

Human evals use paid annotators to judge model outputs on criteria like helpfulness, correctness, fluency, and safety. The gold standard for open-ended tasks where automated scoring fails. Chatbot Arena has collected over 2 million human preference votes across 100+ models. The downside: cost ($0.10-$2.00 per judgment) and speed (hours to days).

Idea 2 · Why Benchmarks Break

Three mechanisms cause benchmark scores to stop reflecting real capability.

Data contamination. Training corpora scrape the internet. Benchmark questions live on the internet. Models see the answers during training. This is not cheating in the traditional sense -- labs do not intentionally include benchmark data. But web-scale scraping makes it nearly impossible to exclude.

Teaching to the test. Labs optimize training mixtures for benchmark performance. If 5% of the training mix is MMLU-style multiple choice, the model learns the format and the answer distribution. MMLU is 4-way multiple choice. Models learn that the answer distribution is approximately uniform across A/B/C/D, which helps even when the model does not know the answer.

Saturation. When every frontier model scores 85-90% on a benchmark, the benchmark stops discriminating. The remaining 10-15% of questions may be ambiguous, mislabeled, or require obscure domain knowledge. Improving from 87% to 89% on MMLU may mean the model memorized two more obscure questions, not that it got smarter.

Idea 3 · Perplexity: A Quick Health Check

Perplexity measures how surprised a model is by a sequence of tokens. Formally, it is the exponentiated average negative log-likelihood:

PPL=exp(−N1​i∑​logP(tokeni​∣context))

A perplexity of 10 means the model is, on average, as uncertain as choosing uniformly among 10 options at each token position. Lower is better. In the GPT-2 paper, the smallest GPT-2 scored 37.5 on WikiText-103 and the 1.5B model 17.5 (zero-shot). Perplexities computed with different tokenizers are not directly comparable, because the "options" are different-sized pieces; bits per byte is the fair cross-model unit (the run under Use it).

Perplexity is useful for comparing models on the same test set, but it has blind spots. A model can have low perplexity by being good at predicting common patterns while being terrible at rare but important patterns. It also says nothing about instruction following, reasoning, or factual accuracy. Use it as a sanity check, not a final verdict.

Idea 4 · LLM-as-Judge

Use a strong model to evaluate a weaker model's output. The idea is simple: ask GPT-4o or Claude Sonnet to rate a response on a 1-5 scale for correctness, helpfulness, and safety. This costs a fraction of a cent to a few cents per judgment, and in the MT-Bench study GPT-4 as judge agreed with human experts about as often as the experts agreed with each other (over 80% when ties are excluded).

The scoring prompt matters more than the model. A vague prompt ("Rate this response") produces noisy scores. A structured prompt with a rubric ("Score 5 if the answer is factually correct and cites a source, 4 if correct but unsourced, 3 if partially correct...") produces consistent, reproducible scores.

Failure modes: judge models exhibit position bias (prefer the first response in pairwise comparisons), verbosity bias (prefer longer responses), and self-preference (GPT-4 rates GPT-4 outputs higher than equivalent Claude outputs). Mitigations: randomize order, normalize for length, use a different judge than the model being evaluated.

Idea 5 · ELO Ratings from Pairwise Comparisons

Chatbot Arena's approach. Show two responses to the same prompt from different models. A human (or LLM judge) picks the better one. From thousands of these comparisons, compute an ELO rating for each model -- the same system used in chess.

ELO advantages: relative ranking is more reliable than absolute scoring, handles ties gracefully, and converges with fewer comparisons than scoring every output independently. Chatbot Arena (now LMArena) fits a Bradley–Terry model to all votes at once rather than updating Elo one game at a time, and reports confidence intervals; the top models change every few months and often overlap within those intervals.

Idea 6 · Eval Frameworks

lm-evaluation-harness (EleutherAI): the standard open-source eval framework. Supports 200+ benchmarks. Run any Hugging Face model against MMLU, HellaSwag, ARC, etc. with one command. Used by the Open LLM Leaderboard.

RAGAS: evaluation framework specifically for RAG pipelines. Measures faithfulness (does the answer match the retrieved context?), relevance (is the retrieved context relevant to the question?), and answer correctness.

promptfoo: config-driven eval for prompt engineering. Define test cases in YAML, run against multiple models, get a pass/fail report. Useful for regression testing prompts -- make sure a prompt change does not break existing test cases.

Idea 7 · Building Custom Evals

The only eval that matters for production. The process:

  1. Define the task. What exactly should the model do? Be precise. "Answer questions" is too vague. "Given a customer complaint email, extract the product name, issue category, and sentiment" is a task you can evaluate.

  2. Create test cases. Minimum 50 for a prototype eval, 200+ for production. Each test case is an (input, expected_output) pair. Include edge cases: empty inputs, adversarial inputs, ambiguous inputs, inputs in other languages.

  3. Define scoring. Exact match for structured outputs. BLEU/ROUGE for text similarity. LLM-as-judge for open-ended quality. F1 for extraction tasks. Combine multiple metrics with weights.

  4. Automate. Every eval runs with one command. No manual steps. Store results in a format that enables comparison over time.

  5. Track over time. An eval score is meaningless in isolation. You need the trendline. Did the score improve after the last prompt change? Did it regress after switching models? Version your eval alongside your prompts.

Eval Type Cost per judgment Agreement with humans Best for
Exact match ~$0 100% (when applicable) Structured output, classification
BLEU/ROUGE ~$0 Low for open-ended text (rough guide) Translation, summarization
LLM-as-judge ~$0.01 ~80% Open-ended generation
Human eval $0.10-$2.00 N/A (is the ground truth) Ambiguous, high-stakes tasks

The same idea as a formula

Perplexity. For N tokens, PPL=exp(−N1​∑i​logp(xi​∣x<i​)). Because N depends on the tokenizer, models are compared fairly in bits per byte: BPB=number of UTF-8 bytes−∑i​log2​p(xi​∣x<i​)​.

Multiple choice by likelihood (how lm-evaluation-harness scores MMLU, ARC, HellaSwag): pick argmaxc​logp(c∣q) ("acc"), or divide by the answer's length in characters ("acc_norm") so long answers are not penalised for having more tokens.

Uncertainty of an accuracy measured on n questions: standard error p^​(1−p^​)/n​, 95% interval about p^​±1.96SE. Two models differ meaningfully only if their difference is large compared with SE12​+SE22​​ (less if they are scored on the same questions, using paired tests).

Elo / Bradley–Terry. P(A beats B)=1+10(RB​−RA​)/4001​. The online Elo update is RA​←RA​+K(SA​−EA​); fitting all ratings jointly by maximum likelihood (what Arena does) removes the dependence on the order of games.

Worked example
Evaluation arithmetic by hand

Perplexity. Four tokens with probabilities 0.5, 0.25, 0.5, 0.125: mean negative log-likelihood =(0.693+1.386+0.693+2.079)/4=1.213 nats; perplexity e1.213=3.36.

Bits per byte. GPT-2 needs 4,503 tokens for 20,054 bytes of WikiText at 3.676 nats each: 4,503×3.676/ln2/20,054=1.19 bits per byte.

Confidence. 33 of 60 questions right: p^​=0.55, SE =0.55×0.45/60​=0.064, 95% interval 42% to 68%. To halve that width you need four times as many questions.

Elo. Ratings 1666 and 1470: E=1/(1+10−196/400)=0.755, so the higher-rated model is expected to win 75% of comparisons. If it wins, with K=32 it gains 32×(1−0.755)=7.8 points.

Step 1 · A Minimal Eval Framework

Define the core abstractions. An eval case has an input, an expected output, and an optional metadata dict. A scorer takes a prediction and a reference and returns a score between 0 and 1.

import json
from collections import Counter

class EvalCase:
    def __init__(self, input_text, expected, metadata=None):
        self.input_text = input_text
        self.expected = expected
        self.metadata = metadata or {}

class EvalSuite:
    def __init__(self, name, cases, scorers):
        self.name = name
        self.cases = cases
        self.scorers = scorers

    def run(self, model_fn):
        results = []
        for case in self.cases:
            prediction = model_fn(case.input_text)
            scores = {}
            for scorer_name, scorer_fn in self.scorers.items():
                scores[scorer_name] = scorer_fn(prediction, case.expected)
            results.append({
                "input": case.input_text,
                "expected": case.expected,
                "prediction": prediction,
                "scores": scores,
            })
        return results

Step 2 · Scoring Functions

Build exact match, token F1, and a simulated LLM-as-judge scorer.

def exact_match(prediction, expected):
    return 1.0 if prediction.strip().lower() == expected.strip().lower() else 0.0

def token_f1(prediction, expected):
    pred_tokens = set(prediction.lower().split())
    exp_tokens = set(expected.lower().split())
    if not pred_tokens or not exp_tokens:
        return 0.0
    common = pred_tokens & exp_tokens
    precision = len(common) / len(pred_tokens)
    recall = len(common) / len(exp_tokens)
    if precision + recall == 0:
        return 0.0
    return 2 * (precision * recall) / (precision + recall)

def llm_judge_simulated(prediction, expected):
    pred_words = set(prediction.lower().split())
    exp_words = set(expected.lower().split())
    if not exp_words:
        return 0.0
    overlap = len(pred_words & exp_words) / len(exp_words)
    length_penalty = min(1.0, len(prediction) / max(len(expected), 1))
    return round(overlap * 0.7 + length_penalty * 0.3, 3)

Step 3 · ELO Rating System

Implement pairwise comparisons with ELO updates. This is exactly the system Chatbot Arena uses to rank models.

class ELOTracker:
    def __init__(self, k=32, initial_rating=1500):
        self.ratings = {}
        self.k = k
        self.initial_rating = initial_rating
        self.history = []

    def _ensure_player(self, name):
        if name not in self.ratings:
            self.ratings[name] = self.initial_rating

    def expected_score(self, rating_a, rating_b):
        return 1 / (1 + 10 ** ((rating_b - rating_a) / 400))

    def record_match(self, player_a, player_b, outcome):
        self._ensure_player(player_a)
        self._ensure_player(player_b)

        ea = self.expected_score(self.ratings[player_a], self.ratings[player_b])
        eb = 1 - ea

        if outcome == "a":
            sa, sb = 1.0, 0.0
        elif outcome == "b":
            sa, sb = 0.0, 1.0
        else:
            sa, sb = 0.5, 0.5

        self.ratings[player_a] += self.k * (sa - ea)
        self.ratings[player_b] += self.k * (sb - eb)

        self.history.append({
            "a": player_a, "b": player_b,
            "outcome": outcome,
            "rating_a": round(self.ratings[player_a], 1),
            "rating_b": round(self.ratings[player_b], 1),
        })

    def leaderboard(self):
        return sorted(self.ratings.items(), key=lambda x: -x[1])

Step 4 · Perplexity Calculation

Compute perplexity using token probabilities. In practice you would get these from the model's logits. Here we simulate with a probability distribution.

import numpy as np

def perplexity(log_probs):
    if not log_probs:
        return float("inf")
    avg_neg_log_prob = -np.mean(log_probs)
    return float(np.exp(avg_neg_log_prob))

def token_log_probs_simulated(text, model_quality=0.8):
    np.random.seed(hash(text) % 2**31)
    tokens = text.split()
    log_probs = []
    for i, token in enumerate(tokens):
        base_prob = model_quality
        if len(token) > 8:
            base_prob *= 0.6
        if i == 0:
            base_prob *= 0.7
        prob = np.clip(base_prob + np.random.normal(0, 0.1), 0.01, 0.99)
        log_probs.append(float(np.log(prob)))
    return log_probs

Step 5 · Aggregate Results

Compute summary statistics across an eval run: mean, median, pass rate at a threshold, and per-metric breakdowns.

def summarize_results(results, threshold=0.8):
    all_scores = {}
    for r in results:
        for metric, score in r["scores"].items():
            all_scores.setdefault(metric, []).append(score)

    summary = {}
    for metric, scores in all_scores.items():
        arr = np.array(scores)
        summary[metric] = {
            "mean": round(float(np.mean(arr)), 3),
            "median": round(float(np.median(arr)), 3),
            "std": round(float(np.std(arr)), 3),
            "min": round(float(np.min(arr)), 3),
            "max": round(float(np.max(arr)), 3),
            "pass_rate": round(float(np.mean(arr >= threshold)), 3),
            "n": len(scores),
        }
    return summary

def print_summary(summary, suite_name="Eval"):
    print(f"\n{'=' * 60}")
    print(f"  {suite_name} Summary")
    print(f"{'=' * 60}")
    for metric, stats in summary.items():
        print(f"\n  {metric}:")
        print(f"    Mean:      {stats['mean']:.3f}")
        print(f"    Median:    {stats['median']:.3f}")
        print(f"    Std:       {stats['std']:.3f}")
        print(f"    Range:     [{stats['min']:.3f}, {stats['max']:.3f}]")
        print(f"    Pass rate: {stats['pass_rate']:.1%} (threshold >= 0.8)")
        print(f"    N:         {stats['n']}")

Reading this output

The pipeline's machinery is sound; its "models" are not real. Model A returns the expected strings word for word, so every metric gives it 1.0. Model B paraphrases correct answers at length: exact match gives it 0, token F1 0.25, and the "LLM judge" 0.58. That judge is a simulation (word overlap plus a length term), not a model, but it shows the real point: the same outputs can pass or fail depending on the metric. The disagreement table makes this concrete. "The capital of France is Paris" is correct but scores 0 on exact match and 0.29 on F1; "paris" passes only because the scorer lower-cases; "Paris, France" fails exact match although nobody would call it wrong. Pick scoring rules from the answers you would accept, then check them on borderline cases like these.

What to keep from this lesson

You are an LLM evaluation engineer. I will describe a task that an LLM performs in production. You will design a complete evaluation suite for that task.

## Design Protocol

### 1. Task Analysis

Break down the task into measurable sub-capabilities:

- **Core capability**: what must the model do correctly for the output to be useful?
- **Edge cases**: what inputs are likely to cause failures?
- **Failure modes**: what does a bad output look like? (wrong format, wrong content, hallucination, refusal)
- **Quality dimensions**: accuracy, completeness, format compliance, latency, cost

### 2. Test Case Generation

Generate test cases in three tiers:

**Tier 1 -- Happy path (40% of cases):** typical inputs that represent the most common usage. These establish a baseline.

**Tier 2 -- Edge cases (40% of cases):** boundary conditions, ambiguous inputs, empty inputs, very long inputs, multilingual inputs, adversarial inputs.

**Tier 3 -- Regression cases (20% of cases):** specific inputs that have caused failures in the past. These prevent known bugs from recurring.

Each test case must include:
- `input`: the exact prompt sent to the model
- `expected`: the expected output (exact for structured tasks, reference answer for open-ended)
- `metadata`: category, difficulty, known failure mode being tested

### 3. Scoring Function Selection

Recommend scoring functions based on the task type:

| Task Type | Primary Scorer | Secondary Scorer | Threshold |
|-----------|---------------|-----------------|-----------|
| Classification | Exact match | N/A | >= 0.95 |
| Extraction | Field-level F1 | Schema compliance | >= 0.90 |
| Summarization | ROUGE-L + LLM-judge | Factual accuracy check | >= 0.80 |
| Generation | LLM-as-judge (rubric) | Diversity score | >= 0.75 |
| Code | Execution pass rate | Static analysis | >= 0.85 |
| Translation | BLEU + LLM-judge | Fluency score | >= 0.80 |

### 4. Pass/Fail Criteria

Define what "good enough" means:

- **Overall pass rate**: what percentage of test cases must pass? (typically 90%+)
- **Per-tier requirements**: Tier 1 must be >= 95%, Tier 2 >= 80%, Tier 3 >= 90%
- **Metric weighting**: how to combine multiple metrics into a single score
- **Regression gate**: any regression case that previously passed must still pass

### 5. Automation Plan

Specify how to run the eval:

- Command to execute the full suite
- Expected runtime and cost (LLM-as-judge adds ~$0.01 per case)
- Output format (JSON results file with per-case scores)
- Integration with CI/CD (run on every prompt change, model upgrade, or code deployment)

## Input Format

Provide:
- Task description (what the LLM does)
- Example input and expected output
- Known failure modes (if any)
- Production constraints (latency, cost, volume)

## Output Format

1. **Task Breakdown**: sub-capabilities and failure modes
2. **Test Cases**: 20 cases across all three tiers (as JSON)
3. **Scoring Functions**: which to use and why
4. **Pass/Fail Criteria**: thresholds and regression gates
5. **Automation Plan**: how to run and integrate the eval

# LLM Evaluation Strategy

When evaluating an LLM system, apply this decision framework to choose the right approach.

## When to use each eval type

**Benchmarks (MMLU, HumanEval, SWE-bench):** You are doing initial model selection. You need to narrow 10 candidate models to 3. Benchmarks give rough ranking at zero cost. Do not use benchmarks as your final evaluation.

**Custom evals:** You are building for production. You have a specific task with specific failure modes. Custom evals are the only evaluation that predicts real-world performance. Minimum 50 test cases for prototype, 200+ for production.

**LLM-as-judge:** Your task is open-ended (summarization, writing, conversation). Exact match and token overlap metrics are too rigid. LLM-as-judge costs ~$0.01 per judgment and agrees with humans ~80% of the time. Always use a rubric, not a vague prompt.

**Human evals:** The stakes are high and automated metrics disagree. Human eval is the ground truth but costs $0.10-$2.00 per judgment. Reserve for ambiguous cases and periodic calibration of automated metrics.

**ELO from pairwise comparisons:** You are comparing multiple models on the same task. Pairwise is more reliable than absolute scoring because humans (and LLM judges) are better at relative judgments.

## Scoring function selection

- **Exact match**: classification, entity extraction, structured outputs with known answers
- **Token F1**: extraction tasks where partial credit matters
- **ROUGE-L**: summarization, translation
- **BLEU**: machine translation
- **LLM-as-judge**: open-ended generation, conversational quality, helpfulness
- **Execution-based**: code generation (run the code, check if tests pass)
- **Schema compliance**: structured outputs (does the JSON match the schema?)

## Red flags in eval design

- Eval set smaller than 50 cases: results are statistically meaningless
- No edge cases: you are measuring happy-path performance, which is always higher than real-world
- Single metric: different metrics tell different stories, use at least two
- No versioning: you cannot track improvement without versioned eval sets
- Eval set contamination: never include eval examples in fine-tuning data or few-shot prompts
- Testing only one model: you need a baseline (even a simple heuristic) for comparison

## Eval pipeline checklist

1. Define the task precisely (not "answer questions" but "classify support tickets into 5 categories")
2. Create test cases across happy path, edge cases, and known regressions
3. Select 2-3 scoring functions appropriate for the task type
4. Set pass/fail thresholds based on production requirements
5. Automate execution: one command runs the full suite
6. Version everything: test cases, scoring functions, prompts, model versions
7. Run on every change: prompt updates, model swaps, code deployments
8. Track trends: a single score is noise, a trendline is signal
9. Calibrate against human judgment quarterly
10. Add regression cases whenever a production failure is discovered

Exercises

  1. Add a "consistency" scorer that runs the same input through the model 5 times and measures how often the outputs match. Inconsistent answers on deterministic inputs reveal fragile prompts or high temperature settings.

  2. Extend the ELO tracker to support multiple judge functions (exact match, F1, LLM-as-judge) and weight them. Compare how the leaderboard changes when you weight exact match heavily versus F1 heavily.

  3. Build an eval suite for a specific task: email classification into 5 categories. Create 100 test cases with diverse examples including edge cases (emails that could belong to multiple categories, empty emails, emails in other languages). Measure how different "models" (rule-based, keyword matching, simulated LLM) perform.

  4. Implement contamination detection: given a set of eval questions and a training corpus, check what percentage of eval questions (or close paraphrases) appear in the training data. This is how researchers audit benchmark validity.

  5. Build a "model diff" tool. Given eval results from two model versions, highlight which specific test cases improved, which regressed, and which stayed the same. This is the eval equivalent of a code diff -- essential for understanding whether a change helped or hurt.

Solutions

SolutionP1 · Higher

Higher: 25.7 for the instruct model against 22.6 for the base on the same WikiText text (1.10 against 1.05 bits per byte). Post-training moves probability mass toward the assistant's way of writing (answers, lists, chat markers) and away from continuing encyclopaedia prose. That is the intended trade: a lower raw-text perplexity is not the goal of an assistant model, and a small rise is normal. A large rise would be a warning sign of forgetting.

SolutionB1 · Different tokens, different units

Perplexity is "average uncertainty per token", and the two models use different tokens: SmolLM2 splits the text into 4,690 pieces and GPT-2 into 4,503 of different sizes, so a perplexity of 22.6 and one of 39.5 are not measured in the same unit, and "twice as good" has no meaning. Compare total information instead: bits per byte (or per character, or per word) divides the total negative log-likelihood by a quantity that does not depend on the tokenizer. Here that gives 1.05 against 1.19, a 12% advantage for SmolLM2. Ratios of perplexities across tokenizers can exaggerate or hide differences in either direction.

Defend a designA model you fine tuned jumped eight points on a public benchmark after you added a new web scraped corpus to the training mix. Leadership wants to announce it. Design the audit that tells you whether the jump is capability or leakage, and say what you would publish instead if it is leakage.

Level 4 of 4 · about 12 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Audit for contamination before anyone announces anything. Search the new corpus for the benchmark questions and their close paraphrases and report the share found, then rerun the model on a versioned custom eval that the corpus cannot contain and diff the individual test cases against the previous model. If the overlap is material and the custom eval did not move, the jump is memorization, and you publish the custom eval result with the contamination share.

Why the jump is suspect.

Training corpora scrape the web and the benchmark questions live on the web. Nobody has to intend it. A new scraped corpus is the most likely path for the test to enter training, and a saturated benchmark rewards memorization with large jumps. The same lesson lists teaching to the test and saturation as the other two mechanisms that move a score without moving capability.

The audit.

  1. Contamination search. Check what share of the eval questions, or close paraphrases of them, appear in the new corpus. Report exact matches and near matches separately with the method.
  2. Model diff. Compare the two model versions case by case: which items improved, which regressed, which stayed. If the improvements cluster on the items the search found, the diff names the leak.
  3. Custom eval. Run both models on your own versioned suite of at least 200 cases with edge cases, kept separate from every training and prompt example. A real gain shows there with an interval; a leak does not.
  4. Separation check. Confirm that no eval case overlaps with the prompt examples or the fine tuning data, because a test case that was also a training example measures memorization.

What to publish.

The custom eval numbers with intervals, the contamination share and the method, and the benchmark score with that caveat attached. Never a bare benchmark number. And add the dedup step to the training pipeline so the next corpus is checked against every eval set before training starts.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "We did not include the benchmark on purpose, so it is clean." Web scale scraping includes it without intent; the lesson calls this the normal path to contamination.
  • "Eight points is too large to be noise." Noise is not the alternative; memorization is, and it produces large jumps.
  • "Announce the benchmark and add the caveat later." Publish the custom eval with intervals and the contamination share; a bare benchmark number is the claim you cannot defend.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 3) The search finds exact matches for a small share and paraphrases for more. Which count? Expected: both; paraphrases leak the answer as well, so report exact and near matches separately with the method.
  • (level 3) The custom eval also rose. Is the jump real? Expected: more likely, if the suite is versioned, separate from training data and large enough that the rise exceeds its interval.
  • (level 4) How do you prevent the question next time? Expected: deduplicate training corpora against every eval set before training, keep eval data out of prompts and fine tuning data, and record the check in the training report.

Explain the mechanismA vendor shows you that their new model scores 89 percent on a public multiple choice benchmark and the model you run today scores 87. They say the gap proves the new model is smarter. Why are you not convinced, and what do you ask for instead?

Level 1 of 4 · about 6 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Public benchmarks stop telling models apart once every frontier model sits in a narrow band near the top. Three mechanisms cause this: training data that contains the test, training mixes tuned to the test format, and saturation, where the questions that remain are ambiguous or mislabeled. A gap of two points in that band is noise or memorization. Ask for a run on your task with your data.

The compression.

The benchmark in the lesson has 15,908 questions across 57 subjects. Within three years the frontier models compressed into a 3-point range at the top. A model that scored 88.7 percent on it could not count the letters in a short word, a task that needs no world knowledge. The score measures the benchmark, not the job.

Three mechanisms.

  1. Data contamination. Training corpora scrape the web, and the questions live on the web. Nobody has to cheat for the answers to end up in training.
  2. Teaching to the test. Labs tune the training mix toward the format. On a 4-way multiple choice test, a model that learns the answer distribution gains points without knowing the answers.
  3. Saturation. When every model scores 85 to 90 percent, the remaining 10 to 15 percent of questions are often ambiguous, mislabeled or obscure. Moving from 87 to 89 can mean two more memorized questions.

What to ask for.

  • A custom eval on your task: at least 50 cases for a prototype and 200 or more for a production decision, with edge cases and adversarial inputs.
  • Per-category results, not one aggregate.
  • A contamination check: search the training corpus for the eval questions and close paraphrases.

Benchmarks still help with rough model selection. The final decision needs your eval.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Two points on a standard benchmark is a real gap." At the top of a saturated benchmark the remaining questions are often ambiguous or obscure, so two points can be two memorized questions.
  • "Labs cheat by training on the test." Web scale scraping pulls the questions in without intent; the effect is the same, and the fix is a contamination audit, not an accusation.
  • "Use the coding benchmark instead." A function completion benchmark with 164 problems covers isolated function generation and says nothing about debugging or refactoring across files.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 1) Name the three categories of evaluation. Expected: public benchmarks, custom evals on your own data, and human evals.
  • (level 2) A model does not know an answer. How does the test format still help it? Expected: the test has four options with a near uniform answer distribution, so format knowledge alone raises the score.
  • (level 2) What are benchmarks still good for? Expected: rough model selection before you build a custom eval.
  • (level 3) How would you test whether a benchmark leaked into training? Expected: search the training corpus for the eval questions and close paraphrases, and report the share that appears.

Debug a failureA faithfulness dashboard reports a mean of 0.85 over a thousand cases, above the 0.8 release threshold. Support tickets say the assistant invents policy details in some answers. The eval owner says the metric is green. Where do you look, and what do you change in the report?

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. A mean hides the tail. A mean of 0.85 is consistent with a 5 percent slice of catastrophic failures, and that slice is what becomes tickets. Sort the cases by score and read the lowest 10 percent. Then add a pass rate at a threshold and a bottom quantile alert to the report, next to the mean.

Why the dashboard is green.

Most cases score high while a small slice scores near zero. The average barely moves, because the slice is small. The users in that slice get an invented policy, and no average tells them it was rare.

What to report.

  • Pass rate. The fraction of cases above a threshold. It measures reliability, which is what users feel, so it is more actionable than the mean.
  • Bottom quantile. The lesson refuses aggregate only reporting without the lowest 10 percent surfaced. Read those cases by hand every run.
  • Per-category scores. If the tail comes from one category, the fix is local and the gate should watch that category.

How to debug it.

  1. Pull the lowest scoring cases and read them. Confirm they match the tickets.
  2. Check how missing scores were counted. A judge that returned bad JSON produces a missing score, and dropping missing scores raises the mean.
  3. Check that the judge was calibrated at the low end. A hand labeled set made of easy cases says nothing about the tail.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The mean passed, so the release passed." A mean of 0.85 often hides a 5 percent catastrophic slice; the gate needs the tail.
  • "Raise the mean threshold to 0.9." A higher mean threshold still hides a small slice of zeros; add a distribution measure.
  • "The judge is noisy at the bottom, so ignore those cases." That is where the failures live; calibrate the judge on low scoring cases too.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Why does the lesson prefer the share of cases above a cutoff to the mean? Expected: it counts how many cases are good enough, so it measures reliability.
  • (level 2) The lowest cases all come from one category. What changes? Expected: the fix is local; report per-category scores and gate on the weakest category, not the aggregate.
  • (level 3) Missing scores from parse failures were dropped before averaging. What did that do? Expected: it raised the mean, because the worst outputs often fail to parse; count them as failures or report them separately.
  • (level 3) Which alert do you add to the release gate? Expected: a floor on the lowest 10 percent and a pass rate threshold, both next to the mean.

Explain the mechanismA colleague reports that the new checkpoint reaches a perplexity of 10 on the held out text, better than last week, and wants to ship it as the stronger model. What does the number mean, and what does it not tell you?

Level 1 of 4 · about 5 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. Perplexity is the exponential of the mean negative log likelihood per token. A value of 10 means that at each position the model is as uncertain as a uniform choice among 10 options. Lower is better only while the test set and the tokenizer stay fixed. It says nothing about instruction following, reasoning or factual accuracy, so treat it as a sanity check and run the task evals before you ship.

The definition.

PPL = exp( -(1/N) * sum_i log P(token_i | context) )

A perplexity of one means the model assigns probability one to every real token. A perplexity equal to the vocabulary size means the model is uniform and learnt nothing. A strong base model on a standard corpus sits around eight to twelve; a bad one on the same text sits at fifty plus.

What it misses.

A model can reach low perplexity by predicting common patterns well while it is bad at rare but important ones. The number also ignores everything a chat model is for: following instructions, reasoning and being right. Use it to catch a broken checkpoint or a tokenizer mismatch, not to pick a winner.

Two checks before you trust it.

  1. Same text, same tokenizer. The number depends on both. Two models measured on different held out sets cannot be compared.
  2. Honest counting. Sum the loss over real token positions and divide once at the end. Pad positions must not be in the denominator, and the logits at one position predict the next token. A value below one is impossible. It means the summed negative log likelihood came out negative, and the usual cause is an adapter that returned log p instead of negative log p.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "Lower perplexity means a better assistant." It measures fit to the text distribution, not instruction following or correctness.
  • "Perplexities from two papers are comparable." They depend on the test set and the tokenizer, so only same set comparisons hold.
  • "Aim for a perplexity near one." The held out text is not the model's own output; a value near one would suggest the test text was in the training data.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 1) Which perplexity value means the model is never surprised? Expected: one, because every real token then has probability one.
  • (level 2) Two models were measured on different test sets. Can you compare their perplexities? Expected: no; the number depends on the text and the tokenizer, so measure both on the same set.
  • (level 2) The adapter reports a perplexity of 0.8. What happened? Expected: the summed loss is negative, most likely because the adapter returned log p instead of negative log p; a perplexity below one is impossible, so the contract was violated.
  • (level 3) Why does the eval sum the loss over non pad positions and divide at the end instead of averaging per batch perplexities? Expected: averaging per batch gives each token of a short batch more weight than each token of a long batch, and pad positions shrink the loss; the sum over real tokens matches the definition.

Where it is taught. The evaluation lesson presents perplexity as a quick health check, and the perplexity and calibration capstone gives the corpus level computation and its contract.

Weigh a trade-offYour product generates answers with one model, and your eval pipeline grades them using that same model plus a rubric. Scores rose after the last release and the team is pleased. A reviewer asks why the gain might not be real. What is the risk, and what do you change?

Level 2 of 4 · about 8 minutes to answer well.

Short answer. A model that grades its own family prefers its own style, so self evaluation inflates scores by about 10 to 20 percent. The rise can mean the judge likes the new outputs more, not that users do. Move the judge to a different model family, calibrate it against human labels, and score the baseline and the new version again under that judge.

Where the bias comes from.

A judge is a model with preferences. It favors longer answers, answers from its own model family and answers that match the prompt style. When the judge comes from the generator's own family, every release that moves the output toward the judge's taste looks like an improvement. The lesson calls this self preference and measures the inflation at about 10 to 20 percent.

Why the release looked better.

A release changes tone, length and structure as well as content. The judge rewards the first three. Without a separate signal you cannot tell which part of the gain is content.

What to change.

  1. Pick a judge from a different model family. Use the cheapest model that still meets the calibration threshold.
  2. Calibrate it: hand label at least 50 cases, the lesson uses 100, and require a Spearman correlation of 0.7 or more with the human ranking.
  3. Score the old and the new release under the same judge and report the difference, not the absolute score.
  4. Normalize for length, or state in the rubric that length does not count.

Plausible wrong answers.

  • "The judge has a rubric, so its family does not matter." The rubric does not remove the preference for its own style; the lesson measures a 10 to 20 percent inflation.
  • "Use the strongest model we have as the judge, which is the one we ship." Strength does not remove self preference; a different family at similar strength is the fix.
  • "Scores went up, so quality went up." The score is a proxy; the output may have moved toward what the judge rewards, such as length and tone.

Follow-ups an interviewer may ask.

  • (level 2) Which two other judge biases does the lesson name next to self preference? Expected: position bias in pairwise comparison and verbosity bias toward longer answers.
  • (level 2) The judge from another family is cheaper but correlates less with people. Which one do you take? Expected: the cheapest judge that still meets the calibration threshold; below the threshold the number is noise whatever the family.
  • (level 3) You cannot change the judge family this quarter. What limits the damage? Expected: calibrate against human labels and publish the correlation, normalize for length, and read only relative changes under the frozen judge.
  • (level 3) After the switch, how do you tell a real gain from a judge preference? Expected: both versions scored by the new judge, the difference with a confidence interval, and a human labeled slice of the cases that moved most.

Check your understanding

Q1Why have benchmarks like MMLU become less useful for comparing frontier models?

When GPT-4, Claude 3, and Llama 3 all score 86-89% on MMLU, a 1-point difference is not meaningful. The benchmark no longer discriminates between models, yet it still dominates leaderboard culture.

Q2What is Goodhart's Law in the context of LLM evaluation?

Labs optimize for benchmark scores (data contamination, benchmark-specific prompting). The score goes up, but real-world capability doesn't necessarily improve. Your own task-specific eval is the only reliable measure.

Q3What is the LLM-as-judge evaluation approach?

LLM-as-judge uses a capable model to score responses against defined criteria. It's cheaper and faster than human evaluation, though it has biases (e.g., preferring verbose responses) that must be calibrated.

Q4Why is building a custom evaluation suite important rather than relying on public benchmarks?

A model scoring 90% on MMLU might fail on your specific task (e.g., extracting dates from legal documents in your format). Only a custom eval with your data, your edge cases, and your success criteria measures what matters.

Q5What is data contamination in the context of LLM benchmarks?

If MMLU questions appeared in the training corpus, the model memorized the answers rather than reasoning about them. This inflates scores and makes benchmark comparisons unreliable. It's a growing problem as training corpora expand.
