Theory tells you what each part does. Production tells you which parts mattered, which failed first, and what the team would do differently. This lesson is practice on real systems. Lesson 14.126 summarizes what the field keeps teaching, and Lesson 14.109 gives you the method for designing a system; here you apply that method to problems real companies faced, with their real numbers, and check yourself against what they shipped.

Why this came about

Much of what works in production is learned by teams before it reaches documentation. Talks, case studies and post-mortems are where those lessons appear first.

In plain words first
Learning from people who shipped

Talks, case studies and post-mortems from teams running agents in production show what the documentation does not: what failed, what they measured, what they would do differently. Use them as design drills: read the problem, sketch your own design, then compare with what the team actually built and why.

How to learn from a practitioner talk

Most people watch a conference talk passively and keep one quote. Engineers who get better from talks watch actively, in three passes:

When What to do Why it works
Before you press play Read the title and write the one question you want answered ("How do they evaluate a multi-step agent?") A question turns 20 minutes of video into one usable answer
When the problem is stated Pause. Spend five minutes sketching your own design with the nine steps from Lesson 14.109, at least steps 1 to 5 Comparing your design with theirs is where the learning happens; agreeing with a slide teaches little
At the end Fill in a decision log (below) and note one thing you would do differently in your own system The log is what you will remember and reuse in design reviews 
Decision log: <company> · <system> · <talk link and timestamp>
Problem       : who uses it, for what, at what volume, what a wrong answer costs
Constraints   : latency, cost, data, regulation, team size
First attempt : what they tried first, and how it failed (the most valuable line)
Design        : the shape (workflow, agent, router, multi-agent), knowledge, tools, guardrails
Measurement   : the metric that moved, how they evaluated, who labeled
Trade-off     : what they gave up, and why it was worth it
Steal this    : the one decision you would copy
Not for us    : the one decision that only works at their scale or in their domain
Watch out
Read talks critically
  • Survivorship bias: conference talks describe systems that worked well enough to present. The failed projects rarely get a slot, so the base rate of success is lower than it looks.
  • Self-reported numbers: a "60% reduction" usually comes from the team's own benchmark, at one point in time. Treat numbers as evidence of direction, and ask what was measured against what.
  • Incentives: a vendor's talk about its own product is still useful, but separate the general lesson (narrow the context) from the product pitch (buy our context engine).
  • Model generation: an architecture designed around 2024 models may be overbuilt for 2026 models. Ask which parts exist because the model was weak, and which because the problem is hard. The second kind lasts.

Case-study drills: design it, then see what they built

Each drill gives you the real problem and constraints, as the company described them. Work through the brief with the nine steps from Lesson 14.109 (at least clarify, success, estimate, shape, knowledge and tools, safety), write your design down, then open "What they built". The critique that follows lists the questions an architecture board would ask about your version. Facts and numbers come from the sources linked in each drill, all covered earlier in the course.

Drill 1 · Uber QueryGPT: text-to-SQL for a whole company

Case brief
Uber, 2024

Uber's data platform serves about 1.2 million interactive queries a month, many written by people who know the business question but not the warehouse. The catalog has a very large number of tables; some schemas alone run to 40,000 to 60,000 tokens. Leadership wants an assistant that turns a question in plain English into correct SQL. The team's first prototype retrieved tables and sample queries by similarity search and fed them to a model.

Your design. (1) Why did the first prototype get worse as more tables were added? (2) Draw the request path from question to SQL: which steps are model calls, which are code, and where does a person check? (3) How would you evaluate it so a failure points to the step to fix? (4) Estimate the input tokens per query if you pass five full schemas, and what that does to cost and latency.

What they builtWhat Uber built

A chain of narrow agents instead of one big prompt. Workspaces are curated sets of tables and sample SQL for one business area. An intent agent maps the question to one or more workspaces, which shrinks the search space. A table agent proposes the tables and lets the user confirm or edit them. A column-prune agent removes irrelevant columns from the very large schemas before generation. Only then is SQL generated. Evaluation uses a golden set with verified intent, tables and SQL, and scores each stage separately. In a limited release, 78% of users said it cut their query-writing time. Source: Uber Engineering, "QueryGPT".

CritiqueCritique your design against theirs
  • Did you narrow the context before generating, or did you rely on a bigger context window? Similarity over a whole catalog fails because many tables look alike.
  • Is there a cheap human confirmation at the step where errors compound (table choice), rather than only at the end?
  • Can your evaluation say "intent was right, tables were wrong"? End-to-end accuracy alone cannot.
  • Did you account for schema size in cost and latency? Five schemas at 40,000 tokens each is 200,000 input tokens per question before any example queries.
  • What runs the SQL, with which permissions, and with what row and cost limits? Uber's write-up focuses on generation; your design must still say how execution is made safe (Lesson 14.35).

Drill 2 · Uber Genie: a policy copilot that gave wrong advice

Case brief
Uber, 2025

Genie, an internal on-call copilot, answers engineers' security and privacy questions from 40-plus policy documents. The first version used standard RAG over PDFs, and experts found too many answers unacceptable and some actively wrong, which is worse than no answer for policy. The team has a golden set of more than 100 questions with expert-written reference answers.

Your design. (1) List the first three things you would inspect before changing the model or the retriever. (2) Policy questions use different words from the policy text. What do you add to each chunk to bridge that gap? (3) Which small, single-purpose model calls would you place around retrieval? (4) How do you make one evaluation round take minutes instead of weeks of expert time?

What they builtWhat Uber built

They started with ingestion: moved source documents from PDFs to Google Docs so structure survived extraction, had a model convert tables into Markdown, kept table chunks whole, and attached a summary, keywords and generated FAQs to every chunk as metadata for keyword search. Retrieval became hybrid (BM25 over the enriched metadata plus vectors, results unioned), surrounded by three narrow agents: a query optimizer that clarifies and splits questions, a source identifier that picks likely documents from their titles, summaries and FAQs, and a post-processor that removes duplicates and restores document order. An LLM judge scoring answers against the expert references cut each evaluation round from weeks to minutes. Acceptable answers rose by a relative 27% and incorrect advice fell by a relative 60%. Source: Uber Engineering, "Enhanced Agentic-RAG".

CritiqueCritique your design against theirs
  • Did you look at the parsed documents first? Most RAG quality problems start with broken tables and lost headings, not with the embedding model (Lesson 4.22).
  • Does your design treat incorrect answers as a separate, more important metric than unhelpful ones? For policy, a confident wrong answer is the expensive failure.
  • Can your system say "I don't know" and route to the policy owner, rather than guessing?
  • Is your judge calibrated against the experts before you trust it (Lesson 14.117)?
  • When a policy changes, how quickly does the index reflect it, and who is told?

Drill 3 · DoorDash: support for drivers, without hallucinated policy

Case brief
DoorDash, 2024

Delivery drivers hit problems mid-delivery and need fast, correct answers drawn from knowledge-base articles. Thousands of drivers use the assistant every day. A wrong answer about pay or policy creates real harm and compliance exposure. The team must keep latency low and release prompt changes often.

Your design. (1) What do you retrieve for: the driver's last message or something else? (2) Design the check that runs before an answer reaches a driver, within a tight latency budget. (3) Which quality areas would you monitor separately in production? (4) How do you stop a prompt change from silently breaking old cases?

What they builtWhat DoorDash built

Retrieval runs on a summary of the conversation, because the issue is spread across turns. Every answer passes a two-tier guardrail: first a cheap in-house semantic-similarity check between the answer and the retrieved article segments; only answers that fail go to an LLM evaluator that checks groundedness, coherence and policy compliance. In production they monitor five areas (retrieval correctness, response accuracy, grammar, coherence and relevance) with LLM judges and regex checks, calibrated by a human team reviewing sampled transcripts. Prompt changes must pass a promptfoo regression suite before release. They report 90% fewer hallucinations and 99% fewer severe compliance issues. Source: DoorDash Engineering.

CritiqueCritique your design against theirs
  • Is your guardrail tiered by cost, so the expensive check runs only where the cheap one is unsure? Running a judge on every answer may break the latency budget.
  • What happens to an answer that fails both checks: a safe fallback, a handoff, or silence?
  • Do your monitors separate kinds of failure, so you can see that "retrieval correctness" is falling while "grammar" is fine?
  • Who calibrates the judges, how often, and on what sample?
  • Is the regression suite built from real failures (Lesson 14.117), or from examples someone imagined?

Drill 4 · Shopify Sidekick: an assistant that outgrew its prompt

Case brief
Shopify, 2025

Sidekick helps merchants run their stores by calling tools in the Shopify admin. As features were added it grew to dozens of tools, and the system prompt filled with special-case rules. Tool boundaries blurred past about 20 tools and became hard to reason about past 50. The team needs to keep adding capabilities without quality falling, and to know whether a release is better than the last.

Your design. (1) Where should a rule that applies to only one tool live, if not the system prompt? (2) What does a stable system prompt buy you in cost and latency? (3) Build the evaluation: where do the test cases come from, who labels them, and how do you know the LLM judge is trustworthy? (4) What do you run before a release that single-turn evals cannot catch?

What they builtWhat Shopify built

Just-in-time instructions: guidance specific to a tool is returned together with that tool's result, only when it is used, keeping the base prompt small and stable (which also helps prompt caching). For evaluation they replaced curated golden examples with ground-truth sets sampled from real merchant conversations, labeled by at least three product experts. Human agreement set the ceiling for the judge: iterating on one judge prompt raised its Cohen's kappa from 0.02 to 0.61, against a human-to-human 0.69. Before release, an LLM-powered merchant simulator replays the goals of real conversations against candidate versions. Later, reinforcement learning found loopholes in the rewards, fixed with gated rewards that combine procedural checks with judges. Source: Shopify Engineering.

CritiqueCritique your design against theirs
  • Did you consider routing or splitting into sub-agents first? That is a valid alternative; the question is whether it is needed before cheaper fixes like just-in-time instructions and better tool descriptions (Lesson 14.4).
  • Does your judge have a measured agreement with people, and a target, before you use it to gate releases?
  • Do your test cases come from real conversations, including the messy ones?
  • Do you test multi-turn behavior, for example with a simulator, before release?
  • If you plan to train with rewards, how will you detect the model gaming them?

Drill 5 · Checkr: the hard 2% of a classification problem

Case brief
Checkr, 2024

Checkr classifies records from background checks into 230 categories. A logistic regression model already handles 98% of records well. The remaining 2% are hard, ambiguous cases that currently need people. Volume is high, so latency and cost per call matter. Someone proposes sending everything to the largest available model with retrieved examples.

Your design. (1) Do you replace the existing model? (2) What is your eval set, exactly? (3) Rank three options before testing them: a large model with a prompt, the same with retrieved examples (RAG), a small fine-tuned model. (4) Which numbers besides accuracy decide the choice?

What they builtWhat Checkr found

They kept the cheap model for the 98% and compared options on the hard 2% only. GPT-4 with a prompt reached 81.8% accuracy at about 15 seconds per call. Adding retrieved examples made it worse, 79.3%, because similar-looking examples pulled the model toward wrong labels. A fine-tuned Llama-2 7B reached 85%, and a Llama-3 8B fine-tuned with LoRA reached 90%, with production latency around 0.15 seconds and inference about five times cheaper than GPT-4. Source: Rubrik and Predibase.

CritiqueCritique your design against theirs
  • Did you assume RAG would help? For classification with look-alike categories, retrieved examples can mislead; test it instead (Lesson 14.139).
  • Is your eval set the hard cases only? An average over all records would hide the difference you are paying for.
  • Did you cost the options per call at real volume, including latency?
  • Who owns the fine-tuned model afterwards: retraining when categories change, drift monitoring, rollback?
  • For background checks, what is the human review path for low-confidence cases, and how is fairness checked (Lesson 15.25)?

Drill 6 · Klarna: when the automation rate is not the goal

Case brief
Klarna, 2024 to 2025

In February 2024 Klarna announced that its AI assistant had handled 2.3 million customer conversations in its first month, work it equated to about 700 full-time agents. In May 2025 its CEO said the cost-focused approach had lowered service quality, and the company began hiring human support staff again. You are asked to design the next version.

Your design. (1) Define success with at least three metrics, only one of which is cost. (2) Design the escalation path to people: when, how, and with what context. (3) How do you decide which intents to automate fully? (4) What would your weekly dashboard show the head of support?

What they builtWhat the public record shows

Klarna repositioned human support as part of its quality offering. The lesson for architects is general: design escalation to people from day one, measure resolution quality and customer satisfaction alongside deflection, and widen automation intent by intent only where quality holds. Source: Entrepreneur, May 2025.

CritiqueCritique your design against theirs
  • Is "conversations handled by AI" on your dashboard as a cost metric only, next to resolution rate, repeat contacts and satisfaction?
  • Can a customer reach a person in one step when they ask, and does the person see the whole conversation?
  • Do you automate per intent with its own quality threshold, rather than a global switch?
  • Do you measure what happens after the conversation (refunds reversed, complaints, churn)?

Drill 7 · EchoLeak: review a design for a zero-click leak

Case brief
Microsoft 365 Copilot, 2025

An enterprise assistant answers employees' questions by retrieving from their email, chats and documents. It renders answers with Markdown, including links and images. In June 2025 Microsoft patched EchoLeak (CVE-2025-32711, CVSS 9.3), found by Aim Security: an attacker only had to send an email. No exploitation in the wild was reported. Before reading on, find the attack in this design yourself.

Your design. (1) Trace how data could leave the organization without the user clicking anything. (2) Name the three conditions that together make this possible (Lesson 15.22). (3) List one control for each step of the path. (4) Which of your controls hold even if the attacker rephrases the payload?

What they builtWhat happened, and what fixes it

The email carried instructions hidden in ordinary-looking Markdown. Later, when the employee asked an unrelated question, retrieval pulled the email into context next to sensitive internal data, and the injected instructions made the assistant send that data out through Teams and SharePoint URLs, with no click needed. Aim Security called the flaw an LLM scope violation: untrusted content steering the model's use of privileged data. The defenses: treat retrieved outside content as untrusted (spotlighting, classifiers), track where every piece of context came from, keep untrusted content away from privileged data in the same unchecked step, and control every exit, including links and images rendered in answers. Source: The Hacker News.

CritiqueCritique your design against theirs
  • Did you find the exit through rendered images and links? It is the one most designs miss, because no tool call is involved.
  • Is your main defense structural (provenance, scoping, exit control), with detection as an extra layer?
  • Would your design pass the Rule of Two check (Lesson 14.126)?
  • How would you test it: do you have injection cases in your red-team suite that use your own retrieval sources (Lesson 15.23)?

Drill 8 · Manus: an agent whose bill is decided by its cache

Case brief
Manus, 2025

A general-purpose agent averages around 50 tool calls per task. Each step re-reads the growing context and writes a short action, so the input-to-output token ratio is near 100:1. At the time, cached input on Claude Sonnet cost 0.30 USD per million tokens against 3 USD uncached. Usage is growing fast and the cost per task decides whether the product is viable.

Your design. (1) Estimate the cost per run with and without caching, using the code below. (2) List everything in your agent that could change the start of the prompt between steps. (3) How do you change the set of available tools mid-task without breaking the cache? (4) Where does the agent keep what it has learned so the context does not grow without limit?

# Back-of-envelope: why an agent's bill is decided by its cache hit rate.
PRICE = {"input": 3.00, "cached": 0.30, "output": 15.00}   # USD per million tokens (Claude Sonnet list prices used in the Manus write-up)

def run_cost(steps=50, prefix=8_000, growth=500, out=200, cache="append-only"):
    """One agent run. Each step re-reads the prompt prefix plus everything appended so far,
    adds one observation (`growth` tokens) and writes one short action (`out` tokens)."""
    fresh = cached = 0
    for i in range(steps):
        context = prefix + growth * i
        if cache == "append-only" and i > 0:
            hit = prefix + growth * (i - 1)          # everything the previous step already sent
        elif cache == "stable-prefix-only":
            hit = prefix if i > 0 else 0            # only the system prompt and tools are reused
        else:                                        # "broken": e.g. a timestamp at the top of the prompt
            hit = 0
        cached += hit
        fresh += context - hit
    output = steps * out
    usd = (fresh * PRICE["input"] + cached * PRICE["cached"] + output * PRICE["output"]) / 1e6
    return {"input": fresh + cached, "ratio": round((fresh + cached) / output), "hit_rate": cached / (fresh + cached), "usd": usd}

for mode in ["broken", "stable-prefix-only", "append-only"]:
    r = run_cost(cache=mode)
    print(f"{mode:19} input {r['input']:>9,} tokens | in:out {r['ratio']}:1 | cache hit {r['hit_rate']:5.1%} | "
          f"USD {r['usd']:.2f}/run | USD {r['usd'] * 100_000:,.0f} per 100k runs")
What you should see
broken              input 1,012,500 tokens | in:out 101:1 | cache hit  0.0% | USD 3.19/run | USD 318,750 per 100k runs
stable-prefix-only  input 1,012,500 tokens | in:out 101:1 | cache hit 38.7% | USD 2.13/run | USD 212,910 per 100k runs
append-only         input 1,012,500 tokens | in:out 101:1 | cache hit 96.8% | USD 0.54/run | USD 54,150 per 100k runs

The same agent, the same tokens, a sixfold difference in cost. One timestamp at the top of the prompt moves you from the last line to the first. The model is simplified: real providers also charge for writing to the cache and expire entries after a few minutes, so measure your own hit rate from traces (Lesson 14.116).

What they builtWhat Manus does

The team treats KV-cache hit rate as its most important production metric. Their rules: keep the prompt prefix stable (no timestamps at the top), make context append-only, mask tools rather than removing them mid-run, use the file system as memory the agent can reload, keep a todo file the agent rewrites to keep its goal in recent attention, and leave failed actions in the context so the model learns from them. Source: Manus blog.

CritiqueCritique your design against theirs
  • Did your list of prefix changes include tool definitions, user profile injection and retrieved content placed before the history?
  • Is your serialization deterministic (sorted keys, stable tool order)? A reordered JSON object breaks the cache as surely as a timestamp.
  • Do you summarize or drop old observations? That saves tokens but rewrites the context; weigh it against the cache loss, and prefer moving detail to files.
  • Do you track cost per successful task, not per run (Lesson 14.116)?

def review(case: str, mine: set[str]) -> float:
    rubric = RUBRICS[case]
    unknown = mine - {k for k, _, _ in rubric}
    if unknown:
        raise ValueError(f"not in the {case} rubric: {sorted(unknown)}")
    hit = [k for k, _, _ in rubric if k in mine]
    print(f"{case}: you made {len(hit)} of {len(rubric)} load-bearing decisions")
    for key, decision, why in rubric:
        if key not in mine:
            print(f"  missed  {decision}\n          why: {why}")
    return len(hit) / len(rubric)

Watch out
Do not copy a case study wholesale

Each design fits its company's scale, data and team. Uber's chain of agents makes sense for a catalog of very many tables; for a warehouse with 30 well-documented tables, one good prompt with the schemas may be enough. Copy the reasoning (narrow the context, measure each stage, put a person where errors compound), then size the design to your problem.

Write your own case study

The best way to learn from the field is to add to it. Every production system you build is a case study; writing it up in the same shape makes it reusable for your team and your organisation.

Title         : <company or team> · <system> · <year>
Brief         : users, task, volume, what a wrong answer costs, constraints (half a page at most)
First attempt : what you tried and the number that showed it was not good enough
What you built: shape, knowledge, tools, guardrails, evaluation, operations
What moved    : the metric, before and after, and how it was measured
Trade-offs    : what you gave up and why
Rubric        : five load-bearing decisions, each with why it mattered

What a convincing portfolio piece contains. One finished project, explained well, shows more than five demos. For each project, write down:

  1. The problem and the number. Who had the problem, and which metric would show it was solved.
  2. The design, with the alternatives you rejected. A diagram of the request path, and one paragraph each on the choices you did not make and why.
  3. The eval set and the results. Where the test cases came from, how they are graded, and the results before and after your main changes.
  4. What failed. Two or three failures you found, how you found them, and what you changed. Reviewers trust a write-up that shows failures.
