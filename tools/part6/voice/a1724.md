@@top The problem
:::plain In plain words first
**Chaos engineering** means breaking parts of your own system on purpose, in a controlled way, to see whether it survives as designed. You kill a server, cut a network link or make a dependency fail, and watch whether traffic reroutes, fallbacks kick in and alerts fire. LLM stacks have failure modes ordinary services do not: a provider returning "too many requests" for minutes, a KV cache that overflows under long prompts, a strange prompt that stalls the tokenizer. Chaos is only safe with guardrails. You need a defined **error budget** (how many failures your objective allows), a **burn-rate** guard that stops an experiment when it eats that budget too fast, a limited **blast radius** (how much traffic an experiment can touch) and a way to roll back.
:::
@@

@@end Idea 8 · Numbers you should remember
:::math
**Error budget and burn rate.** An objective of $1 - b$ availability allows a failure rate $b$ (for 99.9%, $b = 0.1\%$). If an experiment produces failure rate $e$ on the traffic it touches, the

$$\text{burn rate} = \frac{e}{b}$$

says how many times faster than allowed the budget is being spent. A burn rate of 30 empties a 30-day budget in a day, if it applies to all traffic.
:::

:::worked How much budget does a 5-minute experiment spend?
Objective 99.9% (budget 0.1% of requests). Service: 1,000 requests a minute, so 1,440,000 a day and a daily budget of 1,440 failed requests.

1. Provider-429 experiment: touches 30% of traffic for 5 minutes = 1,500 requests; 1.5% fail = 22 failures. Burn rate on that slice: 1.5 / 0.1 = 15× (on the whole service: 0.45 / 0.1 = 4.5×).
2. Budget spent: 22 of 1,440, about 1.5% of the day's budget.
3. Without a working fallback, 100% of that slice fails for 5 minutes: 1,500 failures, more than the whole day's budget. That is what the burn-rate guard is for: abort within a minute and the cost stays near 300.
:::

:::check
Which statements are right?

- ✓ Chaos in production needs SLOs, observability, automated rollback, runbooks and on-call first.
- ✗ A high burn rate always means the experiment must stop.
  Burn rate on a tiny slice can be high while the total budget spent is small; combine it with blast radius.
- ✓ Tagging experiment traffic with a trace ID lets on-call tell chaos from real incidents.
- ✗ Unit tests catch tokenizer stalls on malformed prompts.
  They show up only under real traffic shapes; that is what chaos experiments are for.
:::

:::predict
Before running the code below: a malformed-prompt experiment fails 4% of requests on 10% of traffic for 3 minutes. What share of the day's error budget does it spend?
:::

:::visual Budget spent per experiment
```python run
RPM, BUDGET = 1000, 0.001
daily_budget = RPM * 60 * 24 * BUDGET
experiments = [("pod kill", 5, 0.002, 0.05), ("provider 429", 5, 0.015, 0.30),
               ("malformed prompt", 3, 0.04, 0.10), ("429 with broken fallback", 5, 1.0, 0.30)]
for name, mins, err, blast in experiments:
    failures = RPM * mins * blast * err
    print(f"{name:26s} {failures:7.0f} failures = {failures / daily_budget:6.1%} of the daily budget")
```
:::

:::read
Most experiments spend a few per cent of a day's budget or less, which is what makes them affordable to run weekly. The broken-fallback case spends more than the whole day in five minutes: that is the failure the experiment exists to find, and the reason the safety plane must abort automatically rather than wait for someone to notice.
:::

:::broken
A team runs its first chaos experiment: cut all connectivity to its main LLM provider in production for 15 minutes, on all traffic, during business hours. The fallback was never tested; the product is down for 15 minutes. List three guardrails they skipped. (Solution B1.)

:::recap What to keep from this lesson
- Prerequisites: SLOs, observability, automated rollback, runbooks, on-call.
- Four planes: control, target, safety, observability, plus a feedback loop.
- Guard with burn rate and blast radius; suppress and tag experiment alerts.
- LLM experiments: memory overload, network cut, provider outage, malformed prompts, KV storms.
- Start small (kill one replica in staging), then grow.
:::

## Solutions

??? sol | P1 · Reading the experiment runner
In `code/main.py` the provider-429 experiment aborts (30× burn on a 30% blast radius) while the malformed-prompt experiment completes despite an 80× burn, because its blast radius is only 10%. The gate requires both a high burn rate and a large blast radius, so small experiments may run hot briefly; large ones are stopped.
???

??? sol | B1 · Three skipped guardrails
(1) Blast radius: start with a few per cent of traffic, not all of it. (2) A burn-rate abort: stop automatically within a minute when the budget burns faster than 2× expected. (3) Staging first and an off-peak window: test the fallback where failure is cheap before testing it in production. A fourth: the fallback itself should have been verified before the experiment.
???
