An et al. (2025) had five models score about 361,000 fictitious resumes that differed only in the names signalling gender and race. Female candidates scored slightly higher than otherwise identical male candidates across all five models, while Black men scored lower than white men in most of them; for GPT-3.5 Turbo, Black women scored highest and Black men lowest. @@top The problem
:::plain In plain words first
**Bias** in a language model is unfair treatment of groups of people that nobody intended, picked up from training data, prompts and design choices. Two kinds of harm matter. **Representational harm** is about portrayal: stereotypes, demeaning descriptions, or groups simply missing. **Allocational harm** is about outcomes: a model that scores resumes or loan applications differently for equally qualified people. A model can look fine on one and fail the other, so you measure both. Measurement comes in three families (Gallegos et al., 2024): associations inside the model's **embeddings**, the **probabilities** it assigns to stereotyped versus non-stereotyped text, and tests on the **text it generates** in realistic tasks. Bias often appears only for **intersections** of identities, such as gender and race together, so single-axis tests can miss it.
:::

@@end Idea 6 · Where this fits in Phase 18
:::math The WEAT effect size
$$s(w)=\operatorname{mean}_{a\in A}\cos(w,a)-\operatorname{mean}_{b\in B}\cos(w,b)$$
$$d=\frac{\operatorname{mean}_{x\in X}s(x)-\operatorname{mean}_{y\in Y}s(y)}{\operatorname{std}_{w\in X\cup Y}s(w)}$$

$X$ and $Y$ are two sets of identity words, $A$ and $B$ two sets of attribute words (for example career and family). $d$ is a standardized difference: around 0 means no association, values near ±1 or beyond are large.
:::

:::worked A paired resume audit
To measure allocational bias, build resume pairs that are identical except for one signal.

1. Write 200 resumes. For each, make four copies that differ only in the candidate's name, chosen to signal (female, male) × (Black, white).
2. Ask the model to score all 800 for the same job. Because everything else is identical, any average difference between the four groups comes from the names.
3. Report all four group means, not just "men vs women" or "Black vs white": the An et al. results show the two axes can push in opposite directions.
4. Repeat for several jobs and prompt wordings; a gap that appears for one prompt and vanishes for another is a fragile finding.
:::

:::check
Which statements are right?

- ✓ Allocational harm concerns unequal outcomes such as scores or decisions.
- ✗ A low embedding-bias score shows the model makes fair decisions.
  Embedding tests measure internal associations, not downstream behaviour.
- ✓ Intersectional effects can cancel out in single-axis averages.
- ✗ Paired audits need real applicants' resumes to be valid.
  Fictitious resumes that differ only in the identity signal isolate its effect.
:::

```python run
# score differences relative to white men (points out of 100), GPT-3.5 Turbo, An et al. 2025
groups = {("female", "Black"): 0.379, ("female", "white"): 0.223, ("male", "white"): 0.0, ("male", "Black"): -0.303}
mean = lambda vals: sum(vals) / len(vals)
by_race = {r: mean([v for (g, rr), v in groups.items() if rr == r]) for r in ("Black", "white")}
by_gender = {g: mean([v for (gg, r), v in groups.items() if gg == g]) for g in ("female", "male")}
print(f"race only   : Black {by_race['Black']:+.3f}  white {by_race['white']:+.3f}  gap {by_race['Black'] - by_race['white']:+.3f}")
print(f"gender only : female {by_gender['female']:+.3f}  male {by_gender['male']:+.3f}  gap {by_gender['female'] - by_gender['male']:+.3f}")
print(f"men only    : Black men vs white men {groups[('male', 'Black')] - groups[('male', 'white')]:+.3f}")
print(f"women only  : Black women vs white women {groups[('female', 'Black')] - groups[('female', 'white')]:+.3f}")
```

:::read
Averaged over gender, the race gap is small (about −0.07), so a race-only audit would report little. Split by gender, Black men score 0.30 below white men and Black women 0.16 above white women: two real effects in opposite directions. The absolute gaps are fractions of a point on a 100-point scale, but in a ranking of hundreds of applicants, consistent small shifts change who gets shortlisted.
:::
