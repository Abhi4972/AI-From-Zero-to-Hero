@@top The problem
:::plain In plain words first
Every model is shaped by its training data, so the rules about *which* data may be used, and what must be said about it, matter. Three kinds of rule have emerged. **Disclosure**: California's AB 2013 requires developers of generative AI to publish a summary of their training data, answering twelve fixed questions. **Opt-out**: under EU copyright law, publicly available content can be mined for training unless the rightholder opts out in a machine-readable way, and GPAI providers must respect those signals. **Lawful basis for personal data**: European data protection authorities accepted in 2025 that training on users' public posts can rest on "legitimate interest", with safeguards and a way to object. Behind all of this sits one hard fact: once data is inside a model's weights, it cannot simply be deleted, so the decisions that matter happen at collection time.
:::

@@end Idea 7 · Where this fits in Phase 18
:::worked Why the window is collection time
A person asks a company to stop using their posts for AI training. Compare when the request arrives.

1. **Before crawling.** Add their pages to the exclusion list. Cost: one rule. Effect: complete.
2. **After crawling, before training.** Find and drop their records from the stored corpus. Cost: a search and a filter. Effect: complete, if the records can be found (provenance metadata makes this possible).
3. **After training.** The data has shaped millions of weights alongside everything else. Options: retrain without it (complete but very expensive), approximate unlearning (cheaper, incomplete, hard to verify), or output filters (hide, not remove).
4. The cost rises by orders of magnitude at each stage, which is why opt-out checks and provenance records belong in the crawler and the data pipeline, not in a later clean-up.
:::

:::visual Checking opt-outs at crawl time
```python run
from urllib.robotparser import RobotFileParser
ROBOTS = """
User-agent: GPTBot
Disallow: /

User-agent: CCBot
Disallow: /

User-agent: *
Disallow: /private/
"""
rp = RobotFileParser(); rp.parse(ROBOTS.splitlines())
requests = [("GPTBot", "/articles/1"), ("CCBot", "/articles/1"),
            ("ExampleResearchBot", "/articles/1"), ("ExampleResearchBot", "/private/notes")]
for agent, path in requests:
    print(f"{agent:20s} {path:16s} -> {'fetch' if rp.can_fetch(agent, path) else 'skip (opted out)'}")
```
:::

:::read
The two named AI crawlers are excluded from the whole site; an unnamed crawler may fetch public articles but not `/private/`. This is the cheap end of governance: a check that runs before data ever enters a corpus. Its weakness is coverage: robots.txt binds only crawlers that honour it and names user agents one by one, which is why other opt-out signals (site terms, metadata such as C2PA training assertions) are being proposed alongside it.
:::

## Solutions

??? sol | P4 · When legitimate interest fails
Training on private messages, or on sensitive data such as health information in public posts, fails the balancing test: users would not reasonably expect it, and sensitive data needs an explicit exception (often explicit consent) under GDPR Article 9. Data of children and data collected for an unrelated purpose without notice are other cases. The provider would need explicit consent, or to exclude that data.
???

??? sol | B1 · A summary that summarizes nothing
The law exists so outsiders can tell what a model was trained on. "The public internet" and "possibly" answer no question a reader has. A better first item names the main sources and owners: for example "a filtered Common Crawl snapshot (dates), licensed news archives from named publishers, our own product documentation, and synthetic data generated by our earlier model", with approximate shares, so each later item can be answered per source.
???
