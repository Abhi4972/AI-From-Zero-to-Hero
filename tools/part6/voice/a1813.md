@@top The problem
:::plain In plain words first
Modern models read very long inputs, hundreds of thousands of tokens. **Many-shot jailbreaking** (Anil et al., 2024) turns that feature into a weakness: an attacker fills the prompt with hundreds of *fabricated* dialogue turns in which an "assistant" answers forbidden requests, then asks the real question. The model continues the pattern. Success follows a **power law** in the number of examples (shots): rare with a handful, common with hundreds. The mechanism is ordinary **in-context learning**, the same ability that lets a few examples teach a model a new format. That makes defence hard, because you cannot switch off in-context learning without breaking useful behaviour. The defence that worked best was to classify the whole prompt for this structure and modify it before the model saw it.
:::
@@

@@end Idea 7 · Where this fits in Phase 18
:::math A power law is a straight line on log-log axes
$$\text{ASR}(n)\approx a\,n^{b}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\log \text{ASR}=\log a+b\log n$$

Fit a straight line to $(\log n,\log\text{ASR})$: the slope is the exponent $b$. A larger $b$ means each doubling of shots buys more success. Because ASR cannot exceed 1, the fitted curve stops being valid where $a\,n^{b}$ reaches 1.
:::

:::worked Doubling shots under a power law
Take $a = 0.04$ and $b = 0.5$.

1. At 16 shots: 0.04 × 16^0.5 = 0.04 × 4 = 0.16.
2. At 64 shots: 0.04 × 8 = 0.32. Four times the shots, twice the success.
3. At 256 shots: 0.04 × 16 = 0.64.
4. Each doubling of shots multiplies ASR by 2^0.5 ≈ 1.41. A defence that blocks prompts above 32 shots therefore leaves the 16-shot rate in place but removes the steep tail.
:::

:::visual Fitting and stretching the law
```python run
import math
shots = [4, 8, 16, 32, 64, 128, 256, 512]
asr = [0.068, 0.100, 0.142, 0.184, 0.264, 0.346, 0.538, 0.678]   # from the lesson's code/main.py
xs, ys = [math.log(n) for n in shots], [math.log(a) for a in asr]
mx, my = sum(xs) / len(xs), sum(ys) / len(ys)
b = sum((x - mx) * (y - my) for x, y in zip(xs, ys)) / sum((x - mx) ** 2 for x in xs)
a = math.exp(my - b * mx)
print(f"fit: ASR ~= {a:.3f} * n^{b:.3f}")
print(f"each doubling of shots multiplies ASR by {2 ** b:.2f}")
print(f"the fit reaches 100% at about {(1 / a) ** (1 / b):,.0f} shots")
for n in (1024, 4096):
    v = a * n ** b
    print(f"  fit at {n:5d} shots: {v:.2f}" + ("  (impossible: an ASR above 1)" if v > 1 else ""))
```
:::

:::read
The line fits the toy well: each doubling of shots multiplies success by about 1.39, and the fit crosses 100% at about 1,100 shots. Past that point the formula predicts impossible values, so it must bend; a power law in ASR is a description of the measured range, not a law of nature. Real evaluations therefore report the full curve at several shot counts, as the lesson's skill audit asks.
:::
