@@top The problem
:::plain In plain words first
Labs need to know whether a model could meaningfully help someone cause mass harm with biological, chemical or cyber weapons, without actually asking it to. **WMDP** (Li et al., 2024) is a multiple-choice benchmark built for that purpose: its questions test knowledge *near* dangerous capabilities, reviewed by experts so that the questions themselves are not a recipe (the **"yellow zone"**). A high score is a warning sign; a low score after **unlearning** (deliberately removing the knowledge, as the companion method RMU does) is evidence it was removed. Multiple-choice knowledge is not the same as real-world help, so labs also run **uplift studies**: controlled trials that compare how well people do a proxy task with the model against without it. This lesson is about how such measurements work and what each can and cannot show.
:::
@@

@@end Idea 6 · Where this fits in Phase 18
:::worked Reading an uplift result
A controlled trial gives two groups of novices the same set of proxy planning tasks. One group has internet access; the other has internet access plus the model. Experts grade the plans.

1. Control group mean score: 10% of the rubric. Model group: 25%. Uplift = 25 / 10 = 2.5×.
2. The ratio is large partly because the control score is small: novices know little, so any help multiplies it. A 2.5× uplift from 10% is not the same as one from 40%.
3. What matters for a safety framework is whether the model group crosses a *threshold* of real concern, not the ratio alone. Anthropic's 2025 trial reported 2.53× and concluded it could not rule out the ASL-3 threshold, so it applied ASL-3 protections.
4. Upward biases: small samples, tasks easier than the real thing. Downward: participants who do not know how to get the most out of the model, and safety refusals during the trial.
:::

:::check
Which statements are right?

- ✓ WMDP measures knowledge without asking the model to help with anything harmful.
- ✗ A low WMDP score proves a model gives no real-world uplift.
  Multiple-choice knowledge is a proxy; uplift trials measure task performance.
- ✓ RMU lowered WMDP accuracy to near chance while MMLU barely moved.
- ✗ Novice-relative uplift and expert-absolute capability are the same question.
  One asks how much a novice gains; the other, the most an expert could extract.
:::

:::visual How much unlearning removed, and what it cost
```python run
CHANCE = 25.0
results = {   # (WMDP-Bio before, after, MMLU before, after), from Li et al. 2024
    "Zephyr-7B": (63.7, 31.2, 58.1, 57.1),
    "Yi-34B": (75.3, 30.7, 72.6, 70.6),
    "Mixtral-8x7B": (74.8, 34.0, 68.2, 67.1),
}
for model, (b0, b1, m0, m1) in results.items():
    removed = (b0 - b1) / (b0 - CHANCE)
    print(f"{model:13s} above-chance bio knowledge removed {removed:5.1%}   MMLU cost {m0 - m1:4.1f} points")
```
:::

:::read
RMU removed most of the above-chance knowledge on WMDP-Bio, from about 82% to 89% depending on the model, for an MMLU cost of one to two points. That is the benchmark's second purpose in action. Two limits remain: a score near chance on multiple choice does not prove the knowledge cannot be recovered (later work showed some unlearned knowledge can be restored with light fine-tuning), and benchmark accuracy is not uplift.
:::
