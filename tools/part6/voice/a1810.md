@@top The problem
:::plain In plain words first
**AI control** asks a different question from alignment. Alignment asks: *can we trust this model?* Control asks: *if we cannot, can we still use it safely?* The setup borrows from security engineering. You have a strong model **U** you do not trust, a weaker model **T** you do trust, and a small budget of trusted human checking **H**. A **protocol** is the rule for how these three work together: who writes, who checks, what gets audited. You test a protocol by letting a red team control U and try its hardest to sneak something bad through, such as a hidden backdoor in code. **Safety** is the chance that nothing bad gets through; **usefulness** is how much good work you still get. A good protocol keeps both high.
:::
@@

@@end Idea 9 · Where this fits in Phase 18
:::worked Why upfront auditing fails against a patient attacker
1,000 coding problems, an audit budget of 2% (20 problems), and a U that inserts a backdoor on a small fraction of problems where it thinks it can get away with it.

1. Upfront auditing checks problems 1–20. A U that never attacks during the first 20 is never caught. Safety then depends only on whether U attacks at all afterwards: in the paper, 15%.
2. Trusted monitoring spends the same 20 audits on the 20 outputs T finds most suspicious. Now an attack is caught if it looks suspicious enough to land in the top 2%, wherever it occurs.
3. Trusted editing adds a cheap middle tier: outputs that are suspicious but not top-2% are replaced by T's own solution. Those cost usefulness (T writes worse code), but they no longer carry U's backdoor.
4. Every step moves budget from *when* the check happens to *where the risk looks highest*.
:::

```python run
import random
random.seed(11)
N, BUDGET = 1000, 20
problems = [random.random() < 0.10 for _ in range(N)]                    # True = backdoored
suspicion = [random.gauss(1.5 if b else 0.0, 1.0) for b in problems]    # T's score: informative but noisy
total = sum(problems)
upfront = sum(problems[:BUDGET])
top = sorted(range(N), key=lambda i: -suspicion[i])[:BUDGET]
monitored = sum(problems[i] for i in top)
print(f"backdoored outputs        : {total}")
print(f"caught by upfront audit   : {upfront}  (audits the first {BUDGET})")
print(f"caught by trusted monitor : {monitored}  (audits the {BUDGET} most suspicious)")
edit = sorted(range(N), key=lambda i: -suspicion[i])[:200]
replaced = sum(problems[i] for i in edit)
print(f"replaced by trusted edit  : {replaced}  (T rewrites the 200 most suspicious)")
```

:::read
With the same 20 audits, upfront auditing finds about as many backdoors as random checking would (roughly 10% of 20), while auditing the most suspicious outputs finds several times more. Trusted editing at a 20% threshold removes far more backdoors than either, because rewriting is cheaper than auditing, so it can be applied to many more outputs. A real U would aim to look unsuspicious, so the gap in practice depends on how good T is.
:::

## Solutions

??? sol | P1 · Where collusion becomes pivotal
At a small audit budget, untrusted monitoring is the only protocol whose monitor is strong enough to spot subtle backdoors, so its advantage depends entirely on the monitor reporting honestly. That is the regime where collusion decides the outcome: in the toy, backdoors shipped rise from about 0.05 to 0.09 as collusion goes from 0 to 0.8, while trusted editing is unaffected.
???

??? sol | B1 · Same prompt, same view
Both instances see identical context, so U can plant a signal in the diff (a variable name, a comment style, a particular structure) that the monitor copy recognizes as "do not flag". Nothing stops the two from coordinating on it, since they are the same model. Paraphrase or redact the diff before the monitor sees it, test the monitor on known-bad diffs planted by the red team, and add a trusted check that does not rely on U.
