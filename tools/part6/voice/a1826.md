@@top The problem
:::plain In plain words first
A model, a dataset or a whole AI system is hard to judge from the outside. **Cards** are standard documents that answer the questions a user, auditor or regulator would ask. A **model card** (Mitchell et al., 2019) says what the model is for, how it was evaluated, and how well it works for different groups. A **datasheet** (Gebru et al., 2018) does the same for a dataset: why it was made, what is in it, how it was collected. A **system card** covers the deployed product as a whole, including the safety layers around the model; frontier labs now publish one with each major release. The common problem is not the format but the filling-in: many published cards leave out exactly the sections, such as limitations and results per group, that a careful reader needs most.
:::

@@end Idea 6 · Where this fits in Phase 18
:::worked Reading a card for what is missing
A model card for a resume-screening model reports: "Accuracy 91% on our test set. Intended use: ranking applicants. Limitations: none known."

1. **Disaggregated results.** Accuracy is not broken down by gender, age or other factors, so there is no evidence about fairness (Lessons 20–21).
2. **Evaluation data.** "Our test set" is not described; if it was drawn from the same source as training data, 91% overstates real performance.
3. **Intended and out-of-scope use.** "Ranking applicants" is a high-risk use under the EU AI Act (Lesson 24); the card should say who reviews the rankings and what decisions they must not drive alone.
4. **Limitations.** "None known" is never true; it usually means nobody looked. A good card lists known failure cases and conditions it was not tested under.
:::

:::check
Which statements are right?

- ✓ A model card should report results broken down by relevant groups or conditions.
- ✗ A datasheet documents the model's architecture.
  It documents a dataset: motivation, composition, collection, uses, maintenance.
- ✓ A system card covers the deployment, including safeguards around the model.
- ✗ A complete-looking card proves its claims.
  Cards record claims; attestation work tries to make some of them verifiable.
:::

:::visual Headings are not content
```python run
SECTIONS = ["model details", "intended use", "factors", "metrics", "evaluation data",
            "training data", "quantitative analyses", "ethical considerations", "caveats and recommendations"]
card = {s: "described in detail ..." for s in SECTIONS}
card.update({"factors": "N/A", "quantitative analyses": "TBD", "ethical considerations": "none"})

EMPTY = {"", "n/a", "tbd", "none", "none known", "-"}
present = sum(s in card for s in SECTIONS)
filled = [s for s in SECTIONS if card.get(s, "").strip().lower() not in EMPTY]
print(f"headings present : {present}/{len(SECTIONS)}")
print(f"sections filled  : {len(filled)}/{len(SECTIONS)}")
print("empty sections   :", ", ".join(s for s in SECTIONS if s not in filled))
```
:::

:::read
A heading check scores the card 9 of 9; a content check scores it 6 of 9, and the three empty sections are exactly the ones that matter for fairness and risk: factors, disaggregated analyses and ethical considerations. Audits of public model hubs find the same pattern at scale: basic sections are usually filled in, while limitations, evaluation detail and ethics are the most often missing.
:::

:::recap What to keep from this lesson
- Model cards document a model's use, evaluation and per-group results.
- Datasheets document a dataset's origin, contents and intended uses.
- Data Cards layer detail for different readers; system cards cover the deployment.
- The most important sections are the ones most often left empty.
- Regulation now asks for card-like documentation, such as the GPAI model documentation form.

## Solutions

??? sol | B1 · Model card, not system card
Everything listed is about the model. A system card is about the deployed system: which tools the agent can call, what data it can read, which safety layers sit around it (input and output classifiers, monitoring), how prompt injection is handled (Lessons 15 and 25), and what happens when something goes wrong. Without those, a reader cannot judge the deployment's risk, which is the card's purpose.
