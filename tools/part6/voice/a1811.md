@@top The problem
:::plain In plain words first
Every safety method so far needs someone who can tell good behaviour from bad. Once a model is better than its overseers at a task, that check gets weaker. Two research lines respond. **Scalable oversight** makes the overseer stronger, for example by letting two copies of the model argue opposite sides so a weaker judge only has to pick the better argument (**debate**). **Weak-to-strong generalization (W2SG)** asks whether a strong model trained on a weak supervisor's imperfect labels learns what the supervisor *meant*, not just what it said. Burns et al. (2023) tested this by fine-tuning GPT-4 on labels from a GPT-2-level model. The strong model did better than its teacher, recovering part of the gap to its own ceiling. The share recovered is the **performance gap recovered (PGR)**.
:::
@@

@@end Idea 6 · Where this fits in Phase 18
:::math Performance gap recovered
$$\text{PGR}=\frac{\text{acc}_{\text{strong on weak}}-\text{acc}_{\text{weak}}}{\text{acc}_{\text{strong on gold}}-\text{acc}_{\text{weak}}}$$

The denominator is the gap a perfect supervisor would close; the numerator is how much of it weak supervision actually closed. PGR = 1 means the weak labels were as good as gold for this purpose; PGR = 0 means the strong model only learned to copy the weak one.
:::

:::worked PGR from three numbers
A weak supervisor scores 60% on a task. The strong model trained on gold labels scores 90%. The strong model trained on the weak supervisor's labels scores 78%.

1. Gap a perfect supervisor would close: 90 − 60 = 30 points.
2. Gap actually closed: 78 − 60 = 18 points.
3. PGR = 18 / 30 = 0.6. The strong model recovered 60% of what it could have learned, despite labels that were wrong 40% of the time.
4. It did not copy the errors because its pretraining already contains a better idea of the task; the weak labels mostly tell it *which* task is wanted.
:::

:::check
Which statements are right?

- ✓ Debate tries to make a weak judge's task easier; W2SG tries to make the strong learner generalize correctly.
- ✗ A positive PGR means the strong model is aligned.
  It measures task performance under weak labels, a proxy for one piece of the problem.
- ✓ In Burns et al., GPT-4 trained on GPT-2-level labels outperformed its supervisor.
- ✗ If the strong model imitates its supervisor perfectly, PGR is 1.
  Perfect imitation of the weak labels gives PGR = 0.
:::

:::predict
Before running the code below: a strong learner either imitates the weak labels or falls back on its own prior about the task. If it trusts its prior more on examples where the prior is confident, what PGR do you expect when weak labels are 70% correct?
:::

:::visual PGR from a strong prior and weak labels
```python run
import random
random.seed(5)
def trial(weak_acc, prior_acc=0.92, trust=0.7, n=4000):
    truth = [random.random() < 0.5 for _ in range(n)]
    weak = [t if random.random() < weak_acc else not t for t in truth]
    prior = [t if random.random() < prior_acc else not t for t in truth]
    # the strong learner follows its prior where weak and prior disagree, with probability `trust`
    strong = [p if (w != p and random.random() < trust) else w for w, p in zip(weak, prior)]
    acc = lambda xs: sum(x == t for x, t in zip(xs, truth)) / n
    a_w, a_s, ceiling = acc(weak), acc(strong), prior_acc
    return a_w, a_s, (a_s - a_w) / (ceiling - a_w)

for wa in (0.6, 0.7, 0.8):
    a_w, a_s, pgr = trial(wa)
    print(f"weak {a_w:.3f}  strong on weak {a_s:.3f}  ceiling 0.920  PGR {pgr:.2f}")
```
:::

:::read
PGR here stays close to the learner's trust in its own prior (0.7), whatever the weak accuracy. That is one honest reading of W2SG: the strong model's gain comes from what it already knew, and weak labels mostly steer it. It also shows the risk: a strong model that trusts its prior will "correct" the supervisor in exactly the cases where the supervisor cannot check whether the correction was right.
:::

- Scalable oversight strengthens the overseer; W2SG makes the learner generalize past it.
- PGR = share of the weak-to-ceiling gap that weak supervision closes.
- Burns et al.: GPT-4 trained on GPT-2-level labels beat its supervisor.
- Debate's premise: true answers are easier to defend than false ones; results are mixed.
