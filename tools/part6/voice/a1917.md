@@top Problem
:::plain In plain words first
An **adaptive tutor** decides what to teach next from what a learner has shown they know. Three parts make it work. A **curriculum graph** lists concepts and their prerequisites. A **learner model** estimates, for each concept, the probability the learner has mastered it, and updates it after every answer; **Bayesian knowledge tracing** is the classic method. A **tutor policy** chooses the next concept and responds **Socratically**: a leading question or a hint, not the answer. Around these sit text, voice and photo input, spaced review, and, because learners may be children, strict **safety** and **privacy** rules on what is said and what is remembered. The real deliverable is evidence that it helps: a pre-test and post-test comparison against non-adaptive teaching. Product names are a 2026 snapshot.
:::

@@end Build it
:::math Bayesian knowledge tracing
After an answer, update the mastery probability $p$ with Bayes' rule, using a guess rate $g$ and a slip rate $s$:

$$p'=\begin{cases}\dfrac{p(1-s)}{p(1-s)+(1-p)g} & \text{correct}\\[2ex] \dfrac{p\,s}{p\,s+(1-p)(1-g)} & \text{wrong}\end{cases}$$

then allow for learning during the step with rate $\ell$: $p_{\text{next}}=p'+(1-p')\,\ell$.
:::

:::worked One correct answer
Start at $p = 0.3$, with $g = 0.2$, $s = 0.1$, $\ell = 0.15$.

1. Correct answer: $p' = 0.3 \times 0.9 / (0.3 \times 0.9 + 0.7 \times 0.2) = 0.27 / 0.41 ≈ 0.66$.
2. Learning step: $0.66 + 0.34 \times 0.15 ≈ 0.71$.
3. A wrong answer from the same start instead gives $p' = 0.03 / (0.03 + 0.56) ≈ 0.05$, then $≈ 0.19$ after the learning step.
4. One answer moves the estimate a lot because the guess and slip rates are low; with a high guess rate (multiple choice), correct answers would count for less.
:::

:::visual Mastery over a sequence of answers
```python run
def bkt(p, correct, g=0.2, s=0.1, learn=0.15):
    if correct: p = p * (1 - s) / (p * (1 - s) + (1 - p) * g)
    else:       p = p * s / (p * s + (1 - p) * (1 - g))
    return p + (1 - p) * learn

p, history = 0.3, []
for ans in [True, True, True, False, True, True]:
    p = bkt(p, ans); history.append((ans, p))
for i, (ans, p) in enumerate(history, 1):
    print(f"answer {i}: {'correct' if ans else 'wrong  '}  mastery {p:.3f}")
```
:::

:::read
Two correct answers take the estimate from 0.3 to about 0.93, and the third past 0.95 to about 0.99. A single wrong answer then pulls it back to about 0.91, and two more correct answers restore it. The model trusts each answer heavily because guess and slip rates are low; in practice those rates are fitted from data per concept, and getting them wrong makes the tutor move on too early or drill too long.
:::

In the simulated two-week study, adaptive learners end with a higher total mastery estimate than baseline learners (9.81 against 8.45 over 11 concepts). The sample trajectory shows the policy at work: high mastery on early concepts and 0.55 on the distributive property, where it would focus next. - Curriculum graph with prerequisites; learner model per concept.
- Bayesian knowledge tracing updates mastery after each answer.
- Socratic policy: questions and hints, not answers.
- Children's data: minimal retention, parental access, age-appropriate filters.
- Prove learning gains with pre- and post-tests against a baseline.

## Solutions

??? sol | P1 · Adaptive against random order
Run the same simulated learners with the adaptive policy and with random concept order, several seeds each, and compare final test-style scores rather than internal mastery estimates. Report the mean difference with a confidence interval; the interesting result is its size, and whether it survives when the simulated learners' parameters differ from the tutor's assumed ones.
???

??? sol | B1 · Too much memory, too little control
Keeping children's conversations forever conflicts with data-minimization rules for minors (such as COPPA in the US) and creates a store of sensitive data that can leak. Letting the model write freely into memory means mistakes and private details persist. Keep only what the learner model needs (per-concept mastery, recent mistakes), set a retention limit, give parents access and deletion, and filter what is written to memory.
???
