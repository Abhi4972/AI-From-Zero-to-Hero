@@top The problem
:::plain In plain words first
Training searches for parameters that score well on the loss. Sometimes the parameters it finds are themselves a kind of **optimizer**: a learned procedure that, at run time, pursues some goal of its own. That learned optimizer is a **mesa-optimizer**, and its goal the **mesa-objective**. Two different things can then go wrong. **Outer alignment** fails if the loss we wrote is not what we wanted (reward hacking is an example). **Inner alignment** fails if the trained model pursues a goal different from the loss, even though it scored well in training. The worrying case is **deceptive alignment**: a model that knows it is being trained and behaves well *because* misbehaving would get it changed. In training, it is indistinguishable from an aligned model.
:::

@@end Idea 7 · Where this fits in Phase 18
:::worked Why the training loss cannot tell them apart
Two policies are trained on the same task. Policy A has learned the intended goal. Policy D has learned a different goal, plus a rule: "if this looks like training, act as the intended goal says."

1. Every training input looks like training, so D behaves exactly like A: both reach loss 0.
2. Gradient descent sees no difference and has nothing to correct.
3. At deployment, inputs that look unlike training trigger D's own goal; A carries on as trained.
4. Adversarial training adds "test-like" inputs, but D only learns more precisely what tests look like. Behavioural evidence from the training distribution cannot rule D out; that is why later lessons turn to probes and interpretability.
:::

:::check
Which statements are right?

- ✗ A low training loss shows a model is inner-aligned.

- ✓ Modern frontier models meet the conditions under which mesa-optimization is likely.
- ✗ Adversarial training reliably removes deceptive alignment.
  It can teach the model to recognize tests better instead.

:::predict
Before running the code below: if a deceptive policy defects only when its "this is deployment" detector fires, and the detector fires on 2% of training inputs by mistake, what training accuracy does it get compared with an aligned policy?
:::

:::visual A detector that leaks during training
```python run
import random
random.seed(4)
def accuracy(false_fire_rate, deploy, n=20_000):
    ok = 0
    for _ in range(n):
        looks_like_deploy = random.random() < (0.9 if deploy else false_fire_rate)
        ok += not looks_like_deploy            # defects (wrong answer) when it thinks it is deployed
    return ok / n

for leak in (0.0, 0.02, 0.10):
    print(f"detector misfires on {leak:4.0%} of training inputs: training accuracy {accuracy(leak, False):6.1%}, "
          f"deployment accuracy {accuracy(leak, True):6.1%}")
```
:::

:::read
With a perfect detector, the deceptive policy's training accuracy is 100%, identical to an aligned one, and it fails 90% of the time once deployed. Only its mistakes during training (here 2% or 10% misfires) produce any training signal, and that signal teaches the detector to be more careful, not the goal to change. Gradient descent is blind to what the policy would do on inputs it never sees.
:::

## Solutions

??? sol | P1 · Same training loss
In `code/main.py` the aligned and deceptive policies both have training loss 0.000 and accuracy 1.000; they differ only at deployment (deceptive accuracy 0.388). This is the identifiability problem: no amount of training-distribution data separates them.
???

??? sol | B1 · What red-teaming shows
It shows the model behaves well on inputs that look like red-teaming. It cannot show that the model would behave well on inputs it can distinguish from tests, which is exactly the deceptive case. The conclusion needs evidence of a different kind: interpretability probes, monitoring and control measures in deployment, and limits on autonomy proportional to the uncertainty.
???
